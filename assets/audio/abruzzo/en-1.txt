Montepulciano: the grape, not the town.

The most confusing name in Italian wine belongs to a grape that has nothing to do with the Tuscan town it is named after. Where it grows, why it was cheap for so long, and what the good bottles taste like.

Two Italian wines are called Montepulciano and they have nothing in common. Vino Nobile di Montepulciano comes from a hill town in Tuscany and is made from Sangiovese. Montepulciano d'Abruzzo comes from the other side of the Apennines and is made from a grape called Montepulciano, which is not grown around the Tuscan town at all. Nobody has explained the name. What is certain is that the grape is one of Italy's most planted, that for fifty years it filled the cheapest bottles on the shelf, and that a handful of growers have spent those same fifty years proving it can do far more.

A mountain region with a coast.

Abruzzo is the point where Italy stops being a peninsula and becomes a mountain range. Two thirds of it is mountain, the rest is hill, and the flat land is a thin strip along the Adriatic. The vineyards sit in that strip and on the hills behind it, between the sea and the snow of the Gran Sasso, which rises to 2,912 metres about forty kilometres inland. The result is a climate with two engines: warm Adriatic air from the east, cold mountain air falling from the west at night. Grapes ripen fully and still keep their freshness, which is exactly what a late-ripening variety needs.

The traditional training system is the pergola abruzzese, a horizontal canopy on posts that holds the fruit high and shades it from a sun that can be fierce. It was designed for volume, and volume is what Abruzzo delivered: for decades the region produced enormous quantities of correct, cheap red that was sold in bulk or under supermarket labels across Europe. The wine was rarely bad and almost never interesting. The change came from growers who cut yields, moved to lower vines on the best slopes, and treated the grape as though it deserved attention.

Key facts.

Montepulciano d'Abruzzo DOC (1968): at least 85% Montepulciano, minimum 12% alcohol. Riserva needs 12.5% and two years of ageing, at least nine months of it in wood.

Colline Teramane, the hills behind Teramo in the north, was raised to DOCG with the 2003 vintage, the region's first.

The grape is Italy's second most widely dispersed native variety after Sangiovese, permitted in twenty provinces, and is also the base of Rosso Conero and Rosso Piceno in Marche.

It ripens late and buds late, which protects it from spring frost but means a cold October can ruin the crop.

What it tastes like.

Montepulciano gives a wine of deep, almost opaque purple with relatively low acidity and soft, ripe tannin. At the cheap end that means a soft, dark, inoffensive red. At the serious end it means black cherry and plum, dried herbs, liquorice, tobacco and a faintly bitter, savoury finish that stops the fruit from cloying. The best examples age for fifteen or twenty years and take on leather and tar. It is never as perfumed as Nebbiolo or as sharp as Sangiovese; its virtue is depth and a kind of dark, warm generosity.

In the glass: Montepulciano d'Abruzzo.

 Colour: Deep ruby to purple, almost opaque; garnet with age.

 Nose: Black cherry, plum, dried herbs, liquorice, tobacco, a note of tar in older bottles.

 Palate: Full body, low to medium acidity, soft ripe tannin, a savoury, faintly bitter finish.

 Alcohol: 13–14.5%.

 Serve: 16–18 °C; simple bottles drink young, a Riserva from five years and keeps fifteen.

 At the table: Spaghetti alla chitarra with pallottine, grilled lamb, ventricina, aged pecorino.

The growers who changed it.

The region's reputation was rebuilt by a small number of estates, and two names come first. Edoardo Valentini, in Loreto Aprutino, farmed as his family always had, sold off most of each harvest and bottled only what he judged worthy; his wines are made without modern intervention and are among the most sought-after in Italy. Emidio Pepe, in the Colline Teramane, ferments in concrete, crushes by foot, ages in bottle for years and re-corks old vintages by hand. Both were considered eccentrics for decades. Around them a generation of cooperatives and estates has raised the floor: Masciarelli, Torre dei Beati, Tiberio, Cataldi Madonna, De Fermo, and the vast but reliable Cantina Tollo and Citra.

Abruzzo also grows whites worth knowing. Trebbiano d'Abruzzo DOC (1972) is a puzzle of its own: the grape called Trebbiano d'Abruzzo is thought not to be a Trebbiano at all but the southern variety Bombino Bianco, and vineyards often hold a field blend of both plus Trebbiano Toscano. Made carelessly it is neutral; made by Valentini it is regularly called one of the great dry whites of Italy. Pecorino, a white grape and not the cheese, was nearly extinct in the 1980s and is now the region's fashionable white, firm and citrus-scented with real body. Passerina and Cococciola fill out the list.

Before you move on.

The Montepulciano grape has no connection to the Tuscan town of Montepulciano, whose Vino Nobile is Sangiovese.

Montepulciano d'Abruzzo DOC dates from 1968; Colline Teramane became the region's first DOCG with the 2003 vintage.

The wine is deep, soft-tannined and savoury; Valentini and Emidio Pepe showed it could be serious, and Pecorino is the white to watch.
