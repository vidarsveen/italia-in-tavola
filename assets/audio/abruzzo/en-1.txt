Montepulciano: the grape, not the town.

The Valentini family at Loreto Aprutino made no red wine in 2007. They made none in 2008 either. Their white is bottled in about seven vintages out of ten. Even from a harvest they are satisfied with, they keep back something like five parts in a hundred, and the rest of the fruit is sold locally. What wine does not go into a bottle leaves the cellar door loose and cheap, to whoever comes for it. Gabriele Valentini calls that an old tradition and an uneconomic choice. At the harvest, he says, nobody yet knows what will be sold loose and what will be bottled, so everything is given the same care.

Most of the region's growers also sold wine that never carried a label, and for the opposite reason. Buyers called cariarelli, many of them from Campania, came at the harvest and took the crop as it stood. What they wanted was Montepulciano, and the trade wrote down plainly what they wanted it for: a taglio nobilitante, an ennobling cut, colour and extract to be poured into thinner wines made elsewhere. In a weak year it went north to Piedmont and the Veneto. For most of the twentieth century the grape's value lay in what it could do to somebody else's wine.

The structure that grew around that is still standing. Abruzzo has about forty co-operative wineries, most of them in the province of Chieti. A study of the mid-2000s found that they sold 86 per cent of what they made in bulk, while the private estates sold 85 per cent of theirs in bottle. Two industries grew out of one grape.

Two wines are called Montepulciano and they have nothing to do with each other. Vino Nobile di Montepulciano is made from Sangiovese in a hill town in Tuscany. Montepulciano d'Abruzzo is made from a grape of that name on the other side of the Apennines, and that grape is not grown around the Tuscan town at all. The muddle is an old one among the people who classified vines, and nobody has produced an explanation of it that holds. What can be dated is the grape's arrival on paper. Michele Torcia, travelling through the country of the Peligni in 1792, left the first notice of it in Abruzzo, and the appellation's own rules quote him. Sixty-two years later Panfilo Serafini wrote that the commonest vines there were the montepulciano, both the early-ripening sort and the late one they called cordisco. There were already two of it by 1854.

Two thirds of Abruzzo is mountain and almost all the rest is hill. The plain is one per cent of it, a thin strip along about a hundred and thirty kilometres of Adriatic coast. The vineyards sit on that strip and on the hills behind, up to seven hundred metres where the rules stop them. Warm air comes in off the sea. At night cold air falls off the Gran Sasso and the Maiella at their backs. The appellation's own text credits that swing for the wine, and it is what a late-ripening variety needs: heat enough to finish, cold enough at night to keep the fruit from going flat.

The traditional canopy is the pergola abruzzese, carried overhead on posts, which shades the fruit from a sun that can be fierce and takes some of the force out of hail. The rules allow it, and simple and double trellis beside it. They also allow fourteen to fifteen tonnes of grapes a hectare, dropping to nine and a half in the hills of Casauria. That number, rather than the shape of the canopy, is where a wine sold by the tanker and a wine sold by the bottle part company.

The wine comes out dark to the point of opacity, with low acidity and tannin that is soft rather than firm. Made cheaply that gives a dark, mild red nobody could object to and nobody remembers. Made carefully it gives black cherry and plum, dried herbs, liquorice and tobacco, and a faintly bitter finish underneath that stops the fruit going sweet. The best bottles take fifteen or twenty years and turn towards leather and tar. Nebbiolo is more perfumed and Sangiovese is sharper; what this grape has instead is depth.

Two and a half hectares at San Martino sulla Marrucina made Gianni Masciarelli's first vintage in 1981. He planted the vines closer together than was usual there, changed how they were trained, and put the wine into French oak. He died in Munich in July 2008 of a cerebral haemorrhage, in his early fifties. By then the estate ran to some four hundred hectares and shipped more than a quarter of a million cases. Riccardo Cotarella's verdict was that he had dragged winemaking in Abruzzo into the modern era.

The other cellar went the opposite way, and started earlier. Emidio Pepe founded his estate at Torano Nuovo in 1964 with about a hectare and a half. The reds are de-stemmed by hand and the whites crushed underfoot. Fermentation starts with nothing but the yeast that came in on the fruit, in concrete vats lined with glass. The lining is the whole point: glass gives the wine nothing. No filtering, no added sulphur. About half of every vintage stays in the cellar instead of going out, and the reds are released some four years on. Old bottles are decanted by hand after ten or twenty years. The estate was certified biodynamic in 2006. Pepe died on 28 July 2026, aged ninety-four, the day after his birthday, and the bottles his obituaries reached for were a 1975 Montepulciano and a 1971 Trebbiano.

The region's white DOC has a tangle inside it that nobody has finished untying. Trebbiano d'Abruzzo has existed since 1972, and its rules let the wine be made from Trebbiano Abruzzese, from Bombino Bianco, from Trebbiano Toscano, or from any mixture of the three. Old vineyards hold field blends of all of them. The first two were entered as separate varieties in the national register only in 1994, and no DNA study has settled the relationship between them. It is often said in print that the Abruzzo grape is simply Bombino under another name. That is further than the evidence goes. Made carelessly the wine is neutral. Made at Loreto Aprutino it is put among Italy's finest dry whites.

The white that has actually changed the region is Pecorino, which is a grape and not the cheese. It had been rescued in the Marche a few years earlier. Abruzzo's part was the label. Luigi Cataldi Madonna planted two thousand vines of it at Ofena in 1990, in an eight-thousand-square-metre plot called Frontone. Then he secured an IGT, Alto Tirino, which let the grape's name be printed on the front of the bottle instead of the back. The first two thousand bottles were the 1996. His own account of the division of labour is blunt: the Cocci Grifoni estate rediscovered pecorino, and he christened it. Eighty-seven hectares of the grape were recorded in 2000. By 2010 there were about fifteen hundred.

Abruzzo sold this grape by the tanker for fifty years, to improve wine made somewhere else. The bottles that are asked for now are the ones that were never sold.
