Cerasuolo and the Adriatic rosé.

Italy's darkest rosé is made from the same grape as the region's red, given a few hours on the skins. Why it is a wine in its own right rather than a by-product, and what the coast eats with it.

Most rosé is pale, cold and drunk without much thought. Cerasuolo d'Abruzzo is none of those things. It is the colour of a ripe cherry, which is what its name means, it has the weight of a light red, and in Abruzzo it is drunk all year with the region's most substantial food. It comes from the same Montepulciano grapes as the red, picked from the same vineyards, and the difference is made in a few hours in the cellar.

Hours, not days.

Montepulciano has thick, densely pigmented skins. Left to macerate for a week or two, they give the opaque purple of the red wine. Left for six to twenty-four hours and then pressed off, they give a wine of deep pink to light cherry red, with some of the grape's tannin and structure but none of its heaviness. That short contact is the whole technique. It is not a by-product of red winemaking, and the growers are firm on the point: the grapes for a good Cerasuolo are picked with the rosé in mind, often a little earlier, to keep acidity.

For forty years the wine lived inside the red's appellation as a mere colour variant. In 2010 it was given its own Cerasuolo d'Abruzzo DOC, which is the moment the region decided the wine deserved to be taken seriously. It must be at least 85% Montepulciano and reach 12% alcohol, 12.5% for a Superiore.

Key facts.

Cerasuolo d'Abruzzo DOC since 2010, before that a style within the Montepulciano d'Abruzzo DOC. Minimum 85% Montepulciano, 12% alcohol (12.5% Superiore).

Cerasuolo means cherry-coloured, from cerasa, the dialect word for cherry. Do not confuse it with Cerasuolo di Vittoria, which is a red DOCG in Sicily.

The colour comes from six to twenty-four hours of skin contact, far more than the pale rosés of Provence and far less than a red.

Abruzzo's coast runs about 130 kilometres along the Adriatic, and the southern stretch is the Costa dei Trabocchi, named after its wooden fishing platforms.

A wine for the table, not the terrace.

Because it keeps tannin and body, Cerasuolo handles food that would flatten a pale rosé. In Abruzzo it is poured with arrosticini straight off the coals, with fried fish, with the tomato-heavy sauces of the coast, and with the sheep's cheeses of the interior. It is served cool but not ice-cold, around 12 °C, and it is drunk young, within a couple of years, although the best from Valentini and Cataldi Madonna will age for a decade and turn savoury and orange-tinged.

In the glass: Cerasuolo d'Abruzzo.

 Colour: Cherry red to deep coral, much darker than a Provence rosé.

 Nose: Wild strawberry, dried cherry, orange peel, a hint of cinnamon and almond.

 Palate: Medium body, fresh acidity, a light grip of tannin, dry with a faintly bitter finish.

 Alcohol: 12.5–13.5%.

 Serve: 12 °C, in a wine glass rather than a flute; drink within two years, the best keep ten.

 At the table: Arrosticini, fried Adriatic fish, brodetto, pizza, ventricina, young pecorino.

The coast the wine belongs to.

The Adriatic side of Abruzzo is a working coast rather than a glamorous one: fishing ports at Giulianova, Ortona and Vasto, long shallow beaches, and south of Ortona the Costa dei Trabocchi, where trabocchi, spidery timber platforms with nets on long arms, stand out from the rocks. They were built so that fishermen could work without putting to sea in unpredictable water; Gabriele d'Annunzio, born in Pescara, compared one to the skeleton of a great beast. The disused coastal railway behind them has been turned into a cycle path, and several of the platforms now serve fish dinners over the water. The wine on those tables is Cerasuolo, or the region's Pecorino, and the fish is whatever the boats brought in: brodetto alla vastese, a tomato-and-pepper fish stew that is cooked and served in a terracotta pot and never stirred with a spoon, only shaken.

Buying it.

Two things are worth knowing at the shelf. First, the colour is not a guide to quality here: a Cerasuolo that looks almost like a light red may be perfectly judged, while a pale one may simply have been pressed too early. Second, the wine is cheap for what it is, because rosé is still priced as a summer drink even when it takes a serious vineyard to make. The names to look for are the same as for the red, Valentini and Emidio Pepe at the top, Cataldi Madonna and Torre dei Beati more easily found, and the good cooperatives underneath them. Drink it with food that has fat or chilli in it, and do not save it for July.

Before you move on.

Cerasuolo is made from Montepulciano with six to twenty-four hours on the skins, giving a rosé the colour of cherries.

It became its own DOC in 2010; it is not the same thing as Sicily's red Cerasuolo di Vittoria.

Its body and tannin let it handle grilled mutton, fried fish and brodetto, the food of the Costa dei Trabocchi.
