Cerasuolo and the Adriatic rosé.

A cellarman who does nothing at all still has a cherry-pink wine on his hands eight hours after the grapes come in. Leave it a fortnight and he has the region's red instead. Montepulciano's skins carry so much pigment that the juice under them starts taking colour almost at once, which is why the rules of Cerasuolo d'Abruzzo are written as a clock. Either the juice comes off the skins with no maceration at all, the way a white wine is made, or it lies on them cold for eight to twelve hours and is then pressed away.

Ask in a cellar and the answer is six to twenty-four hours. The trade has a phrase for the short end of that, vino di una notte, a wine of one night. Neither the range nor the phrase appears in the disciplinare. Eight to twelve is what is written there. The gap is the ordinary distance between what growers do and what a legal text will commit itself to. It matters more here than it usually would, because with this grape the hours are the wine. Short of pressing the juice off almost at once, there is no way to make a pale Provençal pink out of Montepulciano. The colour comes whether anyone wants it or not, and the only question left to the winemaker is how much of it to keep.

The wine is still described as what becomes of the red's surplus. It is called a blend of red grapes and white, a compromise between the two, and the wine a grower makes when the fruit is not good enough for anything else. None of that survives contact with the rules, which require at least 85 per cent Montepulciano, set the same minimum alcohol as the red, and describe a method rather than a fallback.

There is also a record. Ottavi and Marescalchi wrote in 1903 that vini cerasuoli had long been made in Abruzzo, and particularly in the province of Chieti. That is a single secondary source and should be carried as one. But it puts the style sixty-five years in front of the red's own appellation, which is the wrong way round for a by-product. What the wine was, on the evidence there is, was the everyday wine of the house and the field.

For forty-two years Cerasuolo lived inside Montepulciano d'Abruzzo as a colour variant of it. A ministerial decree of 5 October 2010 gave it a denomination of its own: at least 85 per cent Montepulciano, 12 per cent alcohol and 12.5 for a Superiore, and no ageing required at all. The rules also prescribe the appearance. The wine must be rosa ciliegia, cherry pink, more or less deep. And they ask for something at the end of the mouth, un retrogusto gradevolmente mandorlato, a pleasantly almondy aftertaste, which is a good deal more particular than a colour.

Tannin and body are what let Cerasuolo stand up to food that would flatten a pale rosé. In Abruzzo it is poured with arrosticini straight off the coals, with fried fish, with the tomato sauces of the coast and with the sheep's cheeses of the interior. It is served cool rather than cold, around 12 °C, and drunk young, within a couple of years. The best from Valentini and Cataldi Madonna will go ten and turn savoury with an orange edge.

The Adriatic side of Abruzzo is a working coast rather than a glamorous one: fishing ports at Giulianova, Ortona and Vasto, long shallow beaches, and along about sixty kilometres of it the Costa dei Trabocchi. A trabocco is a timber platform built out from the rocks with nets slung on long arms, so that a man could fish without putting out into water that turns quickly. The structures are documented from the eighteenth century. Gabriele d'Annunzio, born at Pescara, put the one at San Vito Chietino, the Turchino, into a novel of 1894. The coastal railway behind them has been lifted and its bed made into a cycle path. Some of the platforms now serve dinner over the water: Cerasuolo or the region's Pecorino, and whatever the boats brought in. On this stretch that means brodetto alla vastese, a tomato-and-pepper fish stew cooked and served in the same terracotta pot, never stirred with a spoon, only shaken.

Two glasses of the same cherry pink may have been made in two different ways, one pressed off at once and one left overnight on the skins. A dark one is not the better wine, and a pale one is not the worse. Nothing in the glass will tell you which.
