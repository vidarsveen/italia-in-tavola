Arrosticini and the shepherd's kitchen.

A sheep that could not finish the walk was not left behind. The flocks took about a fortnight to come down from the Abruzzo mountains to the plains of Puglia. An animal that went lame on the way, or dropped from exhaustion, was jointed where it fell. It went into the cauldron the party carried with it, brass or copper, hung from a tripod over the fire. The shepherds called those pots cottore, and the dish takes its name from the pot in the same way: pecora alla callara. Old sheep is tough, so it cooks four to six hours, with tomato, potato and whatever roots and herbs the party had with it. There is nothing to do in the meantime but watch the flock.

The other way of cooking a sheep takes three minutes.

The meat for arrosticini is cut into cubes about a centimetre a side, small enough to brown all over in the few minutes the fire gives them. It is castrato, a ram castrated and killed between six months and two years, or a young ewe that has not yet lambed. The cubes go onto a wooden skewer of some twenty centimetres, lean and fat alternating. The skewers have a dialect name, li cippe. They were cut from vingh, a willow-like shrub along the banks of the Pescara; today they are birch or bamboo. They are grilled on a fornacella, a charcoal trough shaped like a gutter and barely wider than the meat. That width does two things at once. Every coal sits under meat, so the whole thing is over in three or four minutes. And the wooden ends rest over cool air on both sides, where they cannot burn. They are turned constantly, salted as they come off, and eaten by gripping the meat between the teeth and drawing the skewer out from under it. Nobody is served fewer than ten.

The region tells a story about two shepherds on the Voltigno, the plateau on the south-eastern flank of the Gran Sasso where the provinces of L'Aquila, Teramo and Pescara meet. They cut up an animal too old to sell and found a use for every scrap, including the meat closest to the bone. Italian sources date it to the 1930s; English ones have copied a date a century earlier. No document supports either, and neither shepherd has a name. What is on record is duller and firmer: arrosticini are entry nine on the national list of traditional foods for Abruzzo.

Every autumn the flocks walked south to winter on the Tavoliere in Puglia, and in spring they walked back. The roads they used, the tratturi, were grass. Their width was fixed by law at sixty Neapolitan paces, 111.6 metres, with stone posts set along the edges. The width was the point. Millions of animals could move without straying into the crops on either side. The longest, the Tratturo Magno, ran 243 kilometres from L'Aquila to Foggia, and the network came to about 3,100. A thousand sheep needed seven to ten shepherds. With the men went muleteers, dog handlers and a cheesemaker, because milk drawn on the road could not wait a fortnight for a dairy. That is the honest link between the walking and the dairy, and it is why the food of these mountains is made of sheep.

The region's pasta, spaghetti alla chitarra, is cut on a beech frame strung with parallel steel wires. A sheet of egg pasta goes over the wires and a rolling pin presses it through. The wires cut where a machine's rollers would squeeze. So the strand comes out square, with flat faces, four sharp corners and the rough surface of hand-rolled dough, and a sauce holds on to all of it. The wires look like the strings of a guitar, and the box beneath resonates as the pasta drops into it. The tool reaches the record late. A notary at Teramo listed one in a bride's dowry in 1871, and what it replaced was lu rentrocelo, a grooved rolling pin used around Pescara and L'Aquila.

In Teramo the strands are dressed with pallottine, meatballs the size of a hazelnut, made from bread, cheese and mixed meats in a tomato sauce. It is the one traditional Italian version of spaghetti and meatballs. The same town serves a first course that sounds like an accident. Scrippelle 'mbusse are thin crêpes rolled around grated pecorino, with hot chicken or capon broth poured over them at the table. The word 'mbusse is dialect for soaked, and the broth is flavoured with celery, onion, clove and cinnamon. Teramo tells a story about that as well. A local man named Enrico Castorani, assistant to the cook of the French officers garrisoned in the town, is said to have dropped a tray of crêpes into the broth and served them anyway. The sources that tell it call it a legend before they begin.

Sheep on the eastern slopes of the Gran Sasso give Pecorino di Farindola, and its rennet comes from a pig rather than a lamb. The women of the house make that themselves at the winter killing. They take the lining of the stomach, wash it, hang it to dry, keep it about forty days in salt, vinegar and white wine, and then strain it. The cheese was made at home by the women because the men were out at pasture with the animals. It passes from mother to daughter, and the region calls it the women's cheese. It is rubbed and washed with oil and vinegar as it ages, three months to twelve. Very few cheeses anywhere are set with pig's rennet, and as far as anyone has established this is the only one in Italy. It is not a DOP. Canestrato di Castel del Monte and plain table pecorino fill the rest of the board.

From the pig also comes ventricina. The Vasto one is about four fifths lean, cut with the point of a knife into irregular cubes of three or four centimetres. It is worked with dried peppers, fennel, salt and pepper, packed into a bladder and hung at least a hundred days below 13 °C. It comes out coarse and firm, and it is sliced. The soft one, spread rather than cut, is the ventricina teramana. That is most of the way fat, flavoured with orange peel, garlic, pepper paste and rosemary, and ready in weeks. It is the one to set beside Calabria's 'nduja.

On the plateau of Navelli, between seven hundred and a thousand metres, the crop is saffron. A Dominican of the Santucci family of Navelli is said to have brought the bulbs back from Spain in the thirteenth century. The flowers are picked by hand in the early morning before they open, the three red stigmas are pulled out of each one, and they are toasted over almond or oak embers. It takes about two hundred thousand flowers to make a kilogram.

What the spice was worth can be read off the list of people who came to buy it. L'Aquila's merchants sold into Milan, Venice, Frankfurt, Marseille, Vienna, Nuremberg and Augsburg. About 1513 the Nuremberg houses decided they would rather not go through Venetian intermediaries, and opened their own delegation in the city. Production peaked in 1583 and 1584. Then came plague, war and Spanish tariffs, and in 1646 the harvest was one kilogram, against four thousand two centuries before. It recovered — by 1830 it was four and a half thousand kilograms, off forty-five hectares — and then it went again. Today about ninety growers make thirty to forty kilograms a year between them. Most of it leaves the region. What stays goes into risotto and the sweets of L'Aquila.

Sulmona, where Ovid was born, has made confetti, sugared almonds, since at least the end of the fifteenth century. The earliest record of the trade is in documents of 1492 and 1493 in the town's own archive. The nuns of the monastery of Santa Chiara tied them with silk thread into flowers, ears of wheat, bunches and rosaries, which is still how they are sold. Bernardino Pelino founded his firm there in 1783, and seven generations have run it since. Part of the factory is now a museum of the trade. Confetti are given in odd numbers, white for a wedding and wired into bouquets for a christening, five at a time, for fertility, long life, health, wealth and happiness.

The two sheep dishes come out of the same economy. An animal that could not be sold was cooked all day in a pot on the road. The scraps off one that could be were cooked in three minutes over a trough of coals. Either way, what a shepherd ate was the meat nobody would buy.
