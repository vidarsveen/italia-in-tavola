Arrosticini and the shepherd's kitchen.

A kitchen built by shepherds who walked to Puglia and back every year. Skewered mutton, pasta cut on a guitar, sugared almonds, saffron worth more than gold, and the chilli sausage of the south.

Abruzzo's food comes from two places that barely met: the mountains, where shepherds kept sheep and cooked with fire, salt and hard cheese, and the coast, where fishermen cooked with tomato, garlic and chilli. The mountain half won the region's reputation. Its emblem is a skewer of mutton the length of a finger, grilled over charcoal, eaten in handfuls, and served with nothing but bread and oil.

Arrosticini.

Arrosticini are cubes of sheep meat, traditionally castrato or older mutton rather than lamb, alternated with pieces of fat and threaded onto thin wooden skewers. They are grilled over charcoal on a long, narrow brazier shaped like a gutter, the fornacella, which is barely wider than the meat so the wooden ends stay clear of the heat. They cook in a few minutes, are turned constantly, salted, and eaten straight from the skewer, pulled off with the teeth. Nobody eats three. They arrive in bundles of twenty-five or fifty standing in a jar, and the accompaniment is bread soaked in olive oil, held under the skewer to catch what drips.

The story the region tells is of two shepherds in the hills between L'Aquila, Teramo and Pescara in about 1830, cutting up an animal too old to sell and finding a way to use every scrap, including the meat close to the bone. Whether or not that is true, the dish belongs to the world of transhumance, the seasonal migration in which flocks walked south to the plains of Puglia for the winter along the tratturi, broad grass droving roads up to a hundred metres wide, and returned in spring. The tratturi were Roman in origin, protected by law under the kings of Naples, and they shaped what Abruzzo ate: portable, salted, dried, grilled.

Key facts.

Arrosticini: mutton or castrato, cut small, on wooden skewers, grilled on a fornacella; the traditional count is fifty, and the bread is dipped in oil.

Spaghetti alla chitarra is cut by pressing a sheet of egg pasta through the steel strings of a wooden frame, the chitarra, giving a square-edged strand that holds sauce.

Confetti di Sulmona, sugared almonds, have been made in the town since the fifteenth century and are wired into flowers for weddings and christenings.

Zafferano dell'Aquila DOP is grown on the Navelli plain; the crocus is picked before dawn and the three red stigmas are separated by hand.

The guitar and the pot.

Spaghetti alla chitarra is the region's pasta and its cleverest tool. A sheet of egg pasta is laid over a wooden box strung with steel wires and pressed through with a rolling pin; the strings cut square strands and the box beneath resonates, which is why it is called a guitar. In Teramo the classic dressing is pallottine, meatballs the size of a hazelnut, in a tomato sauce. Teramo also gives the region its most surprising first course, scrippelle 'mbusse: thin crêpes rolled with grated pecorino and served in hot chicken broth, a dish that sounds like an accident and works completely.

The other great Teramo dish is the timballo, layers of scrippelle instead of pasta with meatballs, spinach and cheese, baked; and at Easter and in early summer the virtù of Teramo is cooked, a soup that empties the larder of the old year and the garden of the new, with seven of everything, dried beans and fresh vegetables together, traditionally made on the first of May.

Cheese, sausage, saffron and sugar.

Sheep mean cheese. Pecorino di Farindola, made on the eastern slopes of the Gran Sasso, is the rarity: it is one of the very few cheeses in the world set with pig's rennet rather than lamb's, made only by women by tradition, and produced in tiny quantity. Canestrato di Castel del Monte and simple table pecorino fill the rest of the board. From the pig comes ventricina, in the Vasto style a coarse-cut salami of large chunks of lean and fat, heavily flavoured with sweet and hot dried peppers, packed into a bladder; it is the region's answer to Calabria's 'nduja, firmer and less fiery.

Two luxuries sit oddly in a poor region. On the high plain of Navelli, at around 700 metres, saffron has been grown since a Dominican friar brought the crocus back from Spain in the fourteenth century; the flowers are picked before they open and the stigmas plucked by hand, which is why it takes roughly 150,000 flowers to make a kilo. Most of it is exported, and the region uses it sparingly in risotto and in the Aquila sweets. And in Sulmona, Ovid's birthplace, sugared almonds have been made since at least the fifteenth century; the town's confetti are wired into bouquets of flowers and are a fixture at every Italian wedding.

To finish, two green liqueurs from L'Aquila: Centerbe, ferociously strong and made from mountain herbs, and the gentler orange-and-saffron Aurum from Pescara.

Before you move on.

Arrosticini are mutton skewers grilled on a narrow brazier and eaten by the dozen with oiled bread; they come from the shepherds of the Gran Sasso.

Spaghetti alla chitarra is cut on a strung wooden frame and served in Teramo with pallottine; scrippelle 'mbusse are crêpes in broth.

Pecorino di Farindola, ventricina, Navelli saffron and the confetti of Sulmona complete the region's table.
