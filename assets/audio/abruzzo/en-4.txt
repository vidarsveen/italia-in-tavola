Gran Sasso and the hill fortresses.

A corridor runs underground from the top station of the Gran Sasso cable car into the hotel beside it. Neither a guest nor a crate of supplies ever had to cross the open mountain. The engineer Vittorio Bonadè Bottino built that hotel in 1934, at 2,130 metres on the flank of Monte Portella, and the cable car had come up from Fonte Cerreto between 1931 and 1934. In winter it was the only way onto Campo Imperatore at all. That is the practical answer to how anybody lives at two thousand metres in the Apennines. You do not carry things over the snow. You go under it.

The building is famous for a prisoner: Mussolini was held in room 220 through the late summer of 1943 and taken off the mountain by glider. It closed long afterwards and stood empty for years. Work began in September 2022 on putting eighty of its rooms back.

The massif is the Gran Sasso d'Italia, the great rock of Italy. Its summit, Corno Grande, is the highest point in the Apennines at 2,912 metres, high enough to hold snow into July and to be seen from the Adriatic beaches fifty kilometres away. Under its north face lies the Calderone. In the summer of 2000 it broke in two, and the pieces are classed now as glacierets rather than as a glacier. They are the last perennial ice in the Apennine chain. A field campaign in 2022 found about twenty-five metres of ice left under the rock debris, thinning by roughly a metre a year.

South of the summit the mountain flattens into Campo Imperatore, eighteen kilometres by eight, some seventy-five square kilometres of treeless upland lying between 1,460 and 2,138 metres. Fosco Maraini, a mountaineer and orientalist, called it the Little Tibet after the valley of Phari Dzong, and the name stuck. In summer it is grazing, and the flocks that come up to it belong to Castel del Monte, Calascio and Santo Stefano di Sessanio.

Those three villages were not poor. In 1474 the barony of Carapelle, which held Calascio, Rocca Calascio, Santo Stefano di Sessanio and two more, ran over ninety thousand sheep. Calascio's wool was known well outside the region. Santo Stefano's was better still: lana carfagna, worked in Tuscany and sold across Europe. In 1579 the last Piccolomini heir, Costanza, was in debt from building the church of Sant'Andrea della Valle in Rome. She sold the barony and the marquisate of Capestrano to the Medici for a hundred and six thousand scudi, and the Medici held it until 1743.

The money came off the road south. Every autumn the flocks walked down to winter on the Tavoliere in Puglia, and from 1447 the crown taxed them for it. The tax office was the Dogana della Mena delle Pecore, founded at Lucera by Alfonso of Aragon and later moved to Foggia. Winter grazing cost eight ducats a hundred sheep. In 1592 the Dogana counted 4,471,496 sheep and 9,600 cattle on those pastures, and took 622,173 ducats. Rocca Calascio stood on that road with a customs post in it. The fortress on the ridge was never only a watchtower; it was a toll gate on wool. Giuseppe Bonaparte abolished the whole system on 21 May 1806.

One animal here lives nowhere else on earth. The Marsican brown bear is concentrated in the Abruzzo, Lazio and Molise National Park south of the Gran Sasso, which was founded in 1922 and exists largely because of it. A genetic census in 2025 counted about eighty-one of them. They are smaller than the brown bears of the Alps, they are shy and mostly nocturnal, and what kills them is people. Alongside them the parks hold the Apennine wolf, which came back across Italy from a remnant that survived in these mountains, and the Abruzzo chamois on the rock above.

These mountains were a frontier for a thousand years, and they are studded with fortresses. The finest of them is tenth-century by tradition, though the first document that names the place is from 1380, and it stands at 1,460 metres above the Navelli plain. An earthquake in 1461 wrecked much of the settlement. The castle was then rebuilt rather than abandoned. Antonio Todeschini Piccolomini, given the fief by King Ferdinand in 1463, put a wall of river cobbles round it, added the four round corner towers that everybody photographs, and adapted the whole thing for firearms. What finished it was a second earthquake, on 2 February 1703. That one destroyed the castle and the village under it, and sent the people down the hill to Calascio. Just below the walls stands the octagonal church of Santa Maria della Pietà, built in 1596.

The ridge is filmed constantly. Ladyhawke in 1985 and The American in 2010 were shot there. So were the closing scenes of The Name of the Rose, in the valley below between Rocca Calascio and Santo Stefano di Sessanio. The abbey in that film was a set built outside Rome at Fiano Romano.

The village of Santo Stefano di Sessanio, at 1,251 metres, has about a hundred and ten inhabitants. Daniele Kihlgren arrived in 1994 and bought much of it, and the old houses are now rooms of a hotel scattered through the village. Its Medici tower came down in the earthquake of 6 April 2009 and was finished again in October 2021, after three years of work and about a million euros. Rocca Calascio's village went the other way. The transhumant flocks stopped paying from the late nineteenth century, the people left for America and the northern cities, and by 1957 nobody lived up there at all.

Thirty-six per cent of Abruzzo is under protection, the largest share of any Italian region, and none of it was planned. The mountains were too cold and too poor to farm at a profit, so the wolves and the bears were never driven out and it was the villages that emptied instead. What is left is wilderness two hours from Rome. The eighty rooms are going back in at two thousand one hundred and thirty metres, on a mountain that once had to be supplied through a tunnel.
