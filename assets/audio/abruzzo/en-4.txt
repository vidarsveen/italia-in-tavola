Gran Sasso and the hill fortresses.

The tallest mountain in the Apennines, the last ice in central Italy, a high plain that looks like Tibet, a ruined castle from a dozen films, and the bear that gave the region half its parkland.

Abruzzo calls itself the greenest region in Europe, and the claim is not empty: roughly half its territory is under some form of protection, in three national parks, a regional park and dozens of reserves. That is not because the region planned it. It is because the mountains were too high, too cold and too poor to farm profitably, so the wolves and the bears were never driven out, the villages emptied instead, and what was left was wilderness within two hours of Rome.

The Gran Sasso.

The Gran Sasso d'Italia, the great rock of Italy, is the highest massif in the Apennines. Corno Grande reaches 2,912 metres, high enough to hold snow into July and to be visible from the Adriatic beaches fifty kilometres away. Under its north face lies the Calderone, long described as the southernmost glacier in Europe; it broke into two separate ice patches in 2000 and is now classed as a pair of glacierets, the last perennial ice in the whole Apennine chain, and unlikely to survive many more decades.

South of the summit the mountain flattens into Campo Imperatore, a treeless upland plain roughly twenty-seven kilometres long and eight wide at around 1,800 metres, grazed by sheep in summer and empty in winter. Italians call it the Little Tibet, and film crews use it when they need somewhere that looks like nowhere in Europe. Its hotel has a place in history: Mussolini was held prisoner there in the summer of 1943 after his arrest, and in September a German commando force landed by glider on the plain and flew him out in a light aircraft.

Rocca Calascio: a tenth-century watchtower at 1,460 m, walled in the thirteenth century, wrecked by the 1461 earthquake and never rebuilt. Ladyhawke and The Name of the Rose were filmed there.

The bear.

The Marsican brown bear is a subspecies found nowhere else on earth, and there are only a few dozen of them, concentrated in the Abruzzo, Lazio and Molise National Park south of the Gran Sasso. That park was founded in 1922, making it the oldest in the Apennines and the second oldest in Italy, and it exists largely because of the bear. The animals are smaller and markedly less aggressive than northern brown bears; no Marsican bear has ever killed a person. The threats are cars, poison left for other animals, and the sheer smallness of the population. Alongside them the parks hold the Apennine wolf, which came back from near-extinction across Italy from a remnant that survived in these mountains, and the Abruzzo chamois on the high rock.

Castles and abandoned villages.

The mountains are studded with fortresses, because this was a frontier for a thousand years. The finest is Rocca Calascio, which began as a single stone watchtower in the tenth century, was given walls and corner towers in the thirteenth, and stood at 1,460 metres as the highest fortress in the Apennines, watching the sheep roads across the Navelli plain. An earthquake in 1461 destroyed much of it. The village below was rebuilt; the castle was left as it fell, and its silhouette on the ridge has since been filmed for Ladyhawke, The Name of the Rose and The American. Just below stands the small octagonal church of Santa Maria della Pietà.

Nearby, Santo Stefano di Sessanio is a Medici-era village of pale stone that had almost emptied by the 1990s and has been restored as a scattered hotel, its rooms in the old houses. The pattern is common here: earthquakes and emigration hollowed out the interior, and tourism is refilling parts of it. The largest wound is recent. On 6 April 2009 an earthquake struck L'Aquila, the regional capital, killing just over three hundred people and emptying the historic centre; the rebuilding has taken more than a decade, and the city's churches and palaces are only now coming back out from behind their scaffolding.
