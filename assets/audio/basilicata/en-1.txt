Vulture: wine on a volcano.

In the poorest corner of the south, an extinct volcano grows one of Italy's great red wines. What the mountain does to the grapes, why the harvest waits until November, and the towns that live off it.

Basilicata is the instep of the boot, a region of mountains and emptiness that most Italians have never driven through. It has half a million people, two provinces and eight per cent flat land; the rest is mountain and hill. It also has a dead volcano in its northern corner, Monte Vulture, and on the volcano's flanks grows Aglianico, a grape that ripens so late the pickers are working in November, and that makes a wine serious enough to be called the Barolo of the south. This reading is about the mountain. The next is about the grape.

A volcano that stopped.

Vulture last erupted long before anyone was there to write it down, and it has been quiet for tens of thousands of years. What it left is the thing that matters: a cone of basalt, tuff and volcanic ash rising to 1,326 metres out of a landscape that is otherwise clay and limestone. The soil on its slopes is dark, porous and full of potassium, it drains fast, and it holds the heat of the day into the night. Two crater lakes, the Laghi di Monticchio, sit in the old caldera under beech forest, and the mineral water bottled at their foot is sold across Italy. The vineyards ring the mountain between roughly 200 and 600 metres, and some climb higher.

Altitude is the second half of the story. This is the far south, on the latitude of Naples, and without the height the grapes would ripen fast and flabby. At 450 to 600 metres the nights are cold from September onward, the difference between day and night temperature is wide, and the grape hangs on the vine for weeks after the rest of Italy has finished picking. The harvest here runs from late October into November, later than almost anywhere else in the country.

Key facts.

Aglianico del Vulture DOC dates from 1971. The wine is 100% Aglianico, minimum 12.5% alcohol, and may not be sold until a year after the harvest.

Aglianico del Vulture Superiore was raised to DOCG in 2010: minimum 13.5% alcohol and at least three years of ageing. The Riserva needs five years, two of them in wood.

Fifteen communes may use the name, among them Rionero in Vulture, Barile, Rapolla, Venosa, Melfi and Ginestra.

Harvest is late October to early November, among the latest in Italy. Vineyards sit mostly between 200 and 600 metres.

The cellars in the rock.

The volcano gave the growers something else: tuff soft enough to dig. Around Barile the hillside is honeycombed with cellars cut straight into the rock, more than a hundred of them, where the temperature sits near cellar-cool all year without any help. Some are still working wineries; some were houses. It is the same instinct that produced the cave city at the other end of the region, and it is worth remembering that in Basilicata people have always dug rather than built when the rock allowed it.

The towns of the Vulture are small and were poor. Rionero and Barile have Albanian roots, settled in the fifteenth century by refugees from the Ottoman advance, and Barile still keeps traces of the Arbëreshë language. Melfi has a Norman castle where Frederick II issued his constitutions in 1231. Venosa, on the eastern edge, was a Roman colony and the birthplace of the poet Horace in 65 BC; its unfinished abbey church, the Incompiuta, was begun in the eleventh century over a Roman site and never roofed.

Why the wine was overlooked.

Aglianico del Vulture has been a DOC since 1971, which makes it one of the older appellations in the south, and it was respected by anyone paying attention long before that. It never became famous, for reasons that have nothing to do with the wine. Basilicata is hard to reach and has almost no coast, so no tourists passed through. It had no wealthy merchant class to promote it, and for most of the twentieth century the young left. The wine was sold in bulk northward or drunk locally. Only a handful of houses bottled seriously, above all D'Angelo and Paternoster, both in Rionero, who kept the standard through the lean decades. Since the 1990s a second wave has arrived, along with money from outside, and the wine now costs a fraction of what a Piedmontese equivalent does.

Before you move on.

Monte Vulture is an extinct volcano whose dark, fast-draining soil and altitude of 200–600 metres make the region's wine possible.

Aglianico del Vulture has been a DOC since 1971; the Superiore became a DOCG in 2010, with three years' ageing and five for the Riserva.

Harvest runs into November, the latest in Italy, and the growers' cellars are cut into the volcano's tuff at Barile.
