Vulture: wine on a volcano.

In April 1964 Pier Paolo Pasolini took a film crew down into the wine cellars at Barile and filmed the birth of Christ. He cast more than a hundred people from the town, picked out in the street, in the bars and in the cellars themselves. Nothing had to be built for him. The hillside was already a honeycomb of caves cut into soft volcanic rock, a hundred and thirty-one of them, holding cellar temperature all year without any help.

The people who dug them came from across the Adriatic. The first Albanians reached Barile about 1477, after Scutari fell to the Ottomans; they cut shelters into the slope and called the quarter the Sheshë, the square. The shelters became cellars, and the town keeps its wine in them still, if less of it than it once did.

Three of the fifteen communes entitled to put Aglianico del Vulture on a label are Albanian foundations: Barile, Ginestra and Maschito. Italian law protects Arbëresh as a historic linguistic minority, and Basilicata's own regional law names those communities. Barile still uses the language. Rionero, settled in the same decades by refugees whom King Ferdinand I placed there, has lost it.

Monte Vulture stands at 1,326 metres in the northern corner of Basilicata, the instep of the boot, east of the Apennine chain rather than along it. It is built of lava, tuff and ash, out of a country that is otherwise clay and limestone, and that loose volcanic ground is what the vines stand in: dark, porous and quick to drain. The last thing the mountain did was blow two holes in its western flank. Those explosions are dated to roughly 141,000 and 132,000 years ago, and both craters filled with water. They are the Laghi di Monticchio, lying under a beech wood at only 650 metres, so the postcard view of the mountain is a photograph of its final eruption.

Italy's civil protection service sorts volcanoes by the date of the last eruption, and the line it draws is ten thousand years. Vulture is past that line by a factor of thirteen, so it is listed as extinct, which the Alban Hills outside Rome are not. Extinct is not the same as finished. The mountain goes on releasing carbon dioxide from the mantle, and the magma that fed it has been traced to a reservoir twelve to fourteen kilometres down.

One of the lakes has been keeping a record ever since. A hundred metres of sediment have settled in Lago Grande since the crater formed, holding more than three hundred and forty layers of ash from eruptions across Italy, some of them five hundred kilometres away. Geologists now date other volcanoes against it.

Two of the towns around the mountain mattered long before the wine did. At Melfi, Frederick II promulgated his constitutions in the Norman castle on 1 September 1231. Venosa, on the eastern edge of the zone, was a Latin colony from 291 BC and the birthplace of Horace in 65 BC. Its abbey church, the Incompiuta, was begun in the eleventh century partly with stone taken from the Roman amphitheatre, and was never finished.

Fruit grown below 200 metres or above 700 cannot carry the name. Height is what makes the wine possible here. Rionero lies barely north of Naples, and without five or six hundred metres under it the fruit would ripen fast and flat. As it is, the pickers on this mountain are often still working in November.

The volcano's largest business is not wine. The plant at Melfi can fill two million bottles of mineral water a day, and Gaudianello is the fourth-largest naturally sparkling water in Italy; the water pays better. But some of the wine still goes back down into the rock the refugees cut, into the same hundred and thirty-one cellars.
