Aglianico, the Barolo of the south.

A grape with more tannin than almost anything else in Italy, a name whose origin nobody can prove, and a wine that needs a decade. How Aglianico tastes, how it ages, and what to eat with it.

There are three Italian red grapes that everybody agrees belong in the first rank: Nebbiolo in Piedmont, Sangiovese in Tuscany, and Aglianico in the south. Aglianico is the least known of the three and the hardest to like young. It gives a wine almost black in colour, with tannin that grips, acidity that lasts, and a sourness of dark cherry and iron underneath. Left alone for ten years it turns into something else entirely. The comparison to Barolo is a marketing phrase, but it points at something true: this is a wine that is unpleasant early and remarkable late, and both facts come from the same chemistry.

The name nobody can settle.

For a long time the story was that Aglianico came from Greece with the colonists of Magna Graecia, and that the name is a worn-down form of ellenico, Hellenic. It is a good story and it may be wrong. DNA work has found little relationship between Aglianico and the Greek varieties it would be related to if the story held. The alternatives are not much firmer: the name may come from Apulianicum, the Latin term for the land of the south, or from the Spanish llano, plain, applied after the Aragonese took the kingdom in the fifteenth century. What is certain is that the grape is very old, that it is at home in volcanic soil, and that it appears in Basilicata and in Campania, where it makes Taurasi, and nowhere much else.

What it does in the glass.

Young Aglianico del Vulture is opaque, purple-black at the rim, and smells of black cherry, plum skin, violet and something mineral and smoky that growers put down to the volcano. The tannin arrives immediately and does not leave; the acidity is high, which is unusual in a wine of this weight and southern origin, and it is what lets the wine live. With five years the fruit darkens towards dried cherry and prune, tobacco and leather come forward, and the tannin loosens its grip. At ten to fifteen years the best bottles are balanced, savoury and long, and they will hold. The Superiore rules exist to stop the wine reaching the market before it is drinkable.

In the glass: Aglianico del Vulture Superiore.

 Colour: Deep ruby to garnet, opaque when young, brick at the rim with age.

 Nose: Black cherry, plum, violet, tobacco, leather, wood smoke, a mineral note.

 Palate: Full body, high acidity, firm and dusty tannin, dark savoury fruit, a long dry finish.

 Alcohol: 13.5–14.5%.

 Serve: 17–18 °C, decanted when young; drink from six years, the best keep twenty.

 At the table: Lamb, roast pork, lucanica sausage, aged caciocavallo, game, baked pasta.

Rosé, sparkling and the everyday bottle.

Not all of it is built for the cellar. The same grape run off its skins after a few hours makes a deep pink rosato with real body, drunk cold in the summer heat. There is a sparkling version, made because the acidity is there to carry it. And the plain Aglianico del Vulture DOC, released a year after harvest and drunk at three or four, is the bottle that actually appears on tables in Potenza and Matera: still tannic by any normal standard, but a wine for a weekday.

Key facts.

Aglianico is ranked with Nebbiolo and Sangiovese as one of Italy's three great red varieties.

The derivation of the name from ellenico (Hellenic) is traditional but doubted; DNA shows little link to Greek varieties. Apulianicum and Spanish llano are the alternatives, none proven.

The grape buds early and ripens very late, wants sun and dry weather, and keeps high acidity even in the south.

Its other great appellation is Taurasi DOCG in Campania; Aglianico del Taburno became a DOCG in 2011.

What to eat with it.

The wine was made by people who ate pork, lamb and hard cheese, and that is still where it belongs. Lucanica, the sausage the Romans named after this region and carried across the empire, is the obvious partner; so is lamb cooked slowly with potatoes and bay. Caciocavallo podolico, made from the milk of a half-wild breed that grazes the scrub, is aged for years and gets sharp and complex enough to stand up to old Aglianico. The pairing that surprises people is the simplest: a plate of pasta with dried peppers and breadcrumbs, which the next reading is about.

Before you move on.

Aglianico is one of Italy's three great red grapes, with high tannin and high acidity that need years to settle.

The Hellenic derivation of its name is traditional but unproven, and DNA does not support it.

Young it is black, tannic and mineral; at ten to fifteen years it is savoury and long, and it wants pork, lamb and old cheese.
