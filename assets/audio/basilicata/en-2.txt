Aglianico, the Barolo of the south.

Most of Italy has finished picking by the end of September, and the 2026 vintage was the earliest on record. On the Vulture they have not started. Aglianico is rarely picked before the second half of October and often not before November. The growers are not waiting for sugar.

The sugar is ready weeks before the rest of the grape is. Aglianico carries small berries with thick, waxy skins, unusually rich in colour and in condensed tannin, and those ripen on their own schedule. Pick when the sugar says so and the wine comes out strong and green at the same time. Wait for the skins and you get what the mountain is known for. The wine is almost opaque when young, with acidity a southern summer ought to have burned away, and tannin that takes years to loosen its grip.

Cold nights slow the vine and conserve its acid, which is ordinary physiology in a high vineyard. Nobody has published day-to-night temperature figures for these slopes, so what the growers say about their own hillsides is what there is.

The rules then add a delay of their own. Aglianico del Vulture Superiore was raised to DOCG by decree on 2 August 2010. Its three years of ageing run from 1 November of the harvest year. At least twelve months of that must pass in wood and twelve in bottle, and the wine cannot be released before 1 November of the third year after the vintage. A Superiore is in its fourth calendar year before it reaches a shelf. The Riserva needs five years, with twenty-four months of that in wood.

The DOCG rulebook ends with a list of seventy named places, the menzioni geografiche aggiuntive, contrade and hamlets that may be printed on a label, running from Accovatura to Vizzarro. It is a cru map in all but name. One of the seventy is Notarchirico, which is also a Lower Palaeolithic site outside Venosa, whose layers archaeologists date using ash from Vulture itself.

For forty-seven years the Italian register of vine varieties carried two entries: number 002, Aglianico, and number 266, Aglianico del Vulture. The rules of the appellation still name both, and still ask for Aglianico del Vulture, or Aglianico, or both. On 30 May 2018 a ministerial decree recorded each as a synonym of the other. The genetics had already settled it. The three biotypes, of Taurasi, the Taburno and the Vulture, descend from a single vine.

The name is less settled than the plant. It first appears in print in 1520, as aglianiche, by which time the south was under Spanish rule. Three derivations are in play: from ellenico, Hellenic; from Apulianicum, the Roman word for the southern land; and from the Spanish llano, plain. The Greek story is often said to have been killed off by DNA. The largest recent study of Magna Graecia's vines found the opposite, a robust link between southern Italian and Greek genotypes, and Aglianico's own parents remain unknown.

The denomination covers two wines. There is the red, and there is a traditional-method spumante that spends at least nine months on its second fermentation. A pink Aglianico is made on the mountain, but it leaves the cellar as Basilicata IGT.

For thirty-two years Aglianico del Vulture was the only DOC Basilicata had, until Terre dell'Alta Val d'Agri broke the monopoly in 2003. Two more followed. Matera DOC was recognised in 2005, at the other end of the region, and makes a Primitivo among much else. Grottino di Roccanova arrived by decree in July 2009.

The comparison with Barolo is a commonplace of the trade rather than anything in the rules, and nobody can say who coined it. The standard ranking puts Aglianico third among Italy's red grapes, behind Nebbiolo and Sangiovese; Ian D'Agata thinks that undersells it.

Through the first half of the twentieth century most of what the mountain made went away in bulk. It left in barrels of four and five quintals, loaded onto carts and taken to the railway station, bound for Piedmont, where it was blended in to give body and colour to other people's wine.
