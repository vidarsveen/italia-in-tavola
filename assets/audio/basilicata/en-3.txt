Peperoni cruschi and the bread of Matera.

In the lane they call u strittul ru zafaran, the pepper alley at Senise, pods are threaded onto twine so that each one sits at about a hundred and twenty degrees to the last. That is the spacing at which air can pass between them, and air is the difference between a pepper that dries and a pepper that rots.

The crop is picked by hand from the first ten days of August, and picked repeatedly, because it ripens in stages. The pods go first onto cloths or nets in a dry, airy room away from the light, and they stay there two or three days. Only then are they threaded, and only then do the strings, the serte, go out into the sun. They hang there, a metre and a half to two metres of them, until the flesh is light and brittle. The rules that replaced the old ones in 2020 allow drying out of direct sun.

The flesh is no more than a couple of millimetres thick, which is why the pod can be dried at all. The drying is what lets it hang on a wall through a winter instead of moulding. Zafarana is the local name for the pepper itself, a loan from the Arabic za'farān, saffron: what travelled was the colour, not the taste.

A dried pod goes into oil at about a hundred and seventy degrees for two or three seconds, and comes out while it is still bright red. The last of the water flashes to steam and sets the thin wall into a brittle sheet, so the pepper finishes crisping as it cools rather than in the pan. A moment too long and the sugars the drying concentrated will scorch, and the bitterness runs right through. They are eaten as a snack, crumbled over pasta, or scattered on salt cod.

Around 1990 there were as many as two hundred hectares of these peppers. Then the Monte Cotugno dam went up on the Sinni and flooded a good deal of the ground they grew on. By 2020 the certified area was no more than eight hectares; in 2021 it rose to thirty-six. A few dozen hectares cannot supply a national appetite for cruschi.

The bread has been protected since 2008. Pane di Matera must take at least a fifth of its durum semolina from the old local varieties the document names. The starter is specified from the beginning. A kilo of semolina is worked with two hundred and fifty grams of the pulp of ripe fresh fruit, steeped in water, and held a little under thirty degrees until it doubles. The rules then allow half a kilo to a kilo of baker's yeast per hundred of semolina alongside the mother, which the romance around the loaf tends to leave out.

A loaf is shaped as a horn or a tall dome, runs from five hundred grams to ten kilos, and the keeping runs with it: seven days at a kilo, fourteen at ten. A wood oven is allowed but not required, and only a loaf actually baked in one may say so on the wrapper.

Every loaf still takes a stamp on its crown before it goes in, and what is pressed into it now is MT, for the province. It used to be the initials of the head of the household. Families baked once a week, the ovens were public and the dough was not, so the women knew their own bread when the door came off after three hours. Shepherds carved the stamps out on the drove roads, retiring one and cutting a new one when the eldest man of a house died. A suitor might give one to a woman, who kept it if she meant yes. The museum in Matera has the old ones.

The sausage carries the region's Roman name. Varro, writing in the 40s BC, says the thing stuffed into the large intestine is called lucanica because the soldiers learned it from the Lucanians. That one sentence is the whole basis of the story. Apicius gives an actual recipe, pounded meat and spices in a fine casing, hung in the smoke. The modern Lucanica di Picerno took the old name up again only in the 1970s, as family production around the town turned commercial. The pig that supplied it was killed in the cold months, and nothing was thrown away.

The cheese comes from a half-wild cow that grazes scrub. Caciocavallo podolico is made from the milk of the Podolica, which gives eleven to nineteen hundred litres in a whole lactation, about five a day. The cows will not accept a machine, so they are milked by hand beside the calf, and that is why the cheese is scarce. It is held for months and sometimes for years. In a shop, podolico carries no protected designation at all, only a Slow Food presidium.

Pasta here is made without egg and shaped by hand: cavatelli hollowed with a thumb, ferricelli wound around an iron rod, strascinati dragged down the board. Bread that has gone hard is fried into mollica and scattered where a richer kitchen would grate cheese. The south calls it the poor man's cheese. Baccalà alla lucana is salt cod under a handful of fried peppers, and lagane e ceci, broad ribbons with chickpeas, is the dish Horace wrote of going home to. But the plate the region is known by is strascinati con peperoni cruschi, which brings the peppers back from the alley at Senise.

The dish that gathered a whole courtyard is crapiata, a soup of chickpeas, broad beans, cicerchie, peas, lentils, wheat and new potatoes. Each woman brought a handful of whatever she had. It is cooked on the first of August to mark the end of the harvest, and still cooked on that date at La Martella, the village built for the families moved out of Matera's caves.
