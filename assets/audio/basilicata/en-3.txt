Peperoni cruschi and the bread of Matera.

The cooking of a region that had nothing: dried peppers instead of meat, a bread that keeps for a week, and a sausage the Roman army carried across Europe. Cucina povera meant literally.

Every Italian region claims cucina povera. Basilicata has the strongest claim of any of them. This was the poorest region in Italy for most of the twentieth century, a place Carlo Levi described in 1945 as one the modern world had simply passed by, and its cooking shows it: durum wheat, dried peppers, pork when there was pork, sheep's cheese, wild greens, and almost nothing that had to be bought. What makes it worth a lesson is that the constraints produced things nobody would think to invent otherwise.

The crusco.

The peperone di Senise is a sweet pepper with unusually thin flesh, one and a half to two millimetres, and very little water in it. That is what makes the whole thing possible. Picked in August, the peppers are threaded on long strings called serte, one and a half to two metres of them, and hung outside in the shade to dry for weeks until they are light, brittle and deep red. They have been protected as an IGP since 1996 and come in three shapes: pointed, hooked and blunt.

Dried, they can be ground into a sweet red powder the locals call zafarana for its resemblance to saffron. But the famous treatment is to drop a whole dried pepper into hot olive oil for a few seconds, no more, and lift it out before it darkens. It comes out crisp enough to shatter between the fingers, sweet and faintly smoky, and it is eaten as a snack, crumbled over pasta, scattered on salt cod, or stirred into scrambled eggs. A few seconds too long and it turns bitter and is thrown away, which is why the frying is done by whoever in the house is trusted with it.

Key facts.

Peperone di Senise IGP (1996): sweet, thin-fleshed (1.5–2.2 mm), sun-dried on serte, fried for seconds to make peperoni cruschi.

Pane di Matera IGP (2008): durum semolina from local wheats, natural sourdough, salt and water only. Horn-shaped or tall, 1–2 kg, crust at least 3 mm, keeps 7 days at 1 kg and 9 at 2 kg.

Lucanica is named after Lucania. Cicero and Martial record Roman soldiers bringing it back; it is the ancestor of luganega, loukaniko, longaniza and linguiça.

Caciocavallo podolico comes from the Podolica breed, which grazes poor scrubland; the cheese can be aged five or six years.

The bread.

Pane di Matera is made from durum semolina, a sourdough mother, salt and water, and nothing else. It is shaped either as a high domed loaf or as a cornetto, a horn, weighs one to two kilos, and is baked in a wood oven until the crust is at least three millimetres thick and almost black in places. Inside it is straw yellow and full of irregular holes. A one-kilo loaf keeps a week; two kilos keeps nine days. That was the point. Families baked once a week in the communal oven and stamped each loaf with a carved wooden or terracotta seal bearing the head of the household's initials, so the loaves could be told apart when they came out. The museum in Matera has a collection of the stamps.

Stale bread was never wasted. Torn into pieces and fried in oil it becomes the breadcrumb topping, mollica, that goes on almost every pasta dish here in place of cheese, which cost money. That is the origin of the region's signature plate: strascinati con peperoni cruschi e mollica, dragged pasta with crumbled fried peppers and toasted crumbs. Three cheap ingredients, and it is one of the best things in southern Italy.

Pork, cheese and the rest.

Pig-killing in January supplied the year. Lucanica, the sausage, carries the region's Roman name: Cicero and Martial both mention Roman soldiers bringing it home from Lucania, and its descendants are eaten from Greece to Brazil under names that are all the same word worn down. The version from Picerno is the one with a protected name today. Cheese is sheep and cow: pecorino di Filiano from the Vulture, and caciocavallo podolico, made only from the milk of the Podolica cattle that graze the scrub and give very little of it, tied in pairs and hung over a beam to age for years. Pasta is made without egg, dragged or rolled by hand: strascinati, cavatelli, and ferricelli rolled around an iron rod. And there is always chilli, and always bread.

Before you move on.

Peperoni cruschi are Senise peppers dried on strings and fried for seconds until brittle; a moment too long and they are bitter.

Pane di Matera is durum semolina and sourdough, baked to keep for a week, and families stamped their loaves for the communal oven.

Stale bread becomes mollica, the topping that replaced cheese; strascinati with cruschi and mollica is the region's signature dish.
