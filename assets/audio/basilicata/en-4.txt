Matera: the cave city.

A doctor from Turin came into Matera, on the eastern edge of Basilicata, with a permit to have stamped and a guidebook promising a picturesque town, a museum and some curious cave dwellings. She looked around and there was no town: a bare plateau, a few government palaces standing in the dust, some of them unfinished. She walked until she reached a street with houses along one side and a drop along the other, and the city was in the drop.

Luisa Levi was a doctor, and her brother Carlo had been confined in a Lucanian village by the fascist government since 1935. He would set her account down ten years later, in Cristo si è fermato a Eboli. The ravine was two half-funnels side by side, and the districts in them are called the Sassi, the stones. They had, she wrote, the shape in which she had imagined Dante's hell at school. She went down by a mule track, circle by circle, and the streets she walked on were the roofs of the houses below.

A family generally had one cave for everything, and slept in it with its animals. Twenty thousand people lived like that, she reckoned. Malaria was endemic in the gorge, and children sat in the doorways with their eyes half shut and swollen red, and the flies settled on them, and it was trachoma. A crowd of them followed her down, and she handed out her small change so they could buy sweets. That was not what they wanted. What they were calling for, in a dialect she could not follow until she stood still, was dammi 'u chinì — give me the quinine.

A house here is a room cut horizontally into the rock with a façade built in front, so from the street it is a house and from inside it is a cave. The stone is calcarenite, soft enough to cut by hand and firm enough to hold a vault. The town calls it tufo, which it is not. There is no water in the gorge, so house after house had its own cistern, fed by channels that gathered rain off the roofs and filtered it through sand.

The largest lies under the main square. Workmen rebuilding the piazza in 1991 broke into a void, and Enzo Viti, a draughtsman who had spent years mapping underground Matera, crossed it in a rubber dinghy. It had been out of use since the aqueduct reached the city in 1927. Above ground the houses opened onto shared courtyards, the vicinato, each with an oven and a well. Anthropologists surveying the Sassi in the 1950s concluded that the courtyard, not the family, was the unit people lived in.

Cut into the ravine walls and the plateau opposite are 155 rock churches, dug and painted between the eighth century and the thirteenth. They were counted by nineteen young Materani who formed a circle in 1959 and went looking along the cliffs. In 1963 they found the Cripta del Peccato Originale abandoned in a cave, its frescoes five hundred years older than Giotto.

Levi's book came out in 1945 and was read across Italy. It was Palmiro Togliatti, visiting in 1948, who called the Sassi la vergogna nazionale, the national disgrace. Two years later De Gasperi came to look, and set a Lucanian minister, Emilio Colombo, to draft a law that would clear them. Law 619 passed on 17 May 1952. Between fifteen and seventeen thousand people lived there, somewhere between half and two-thirds of the town. The law paid to seal the condemned dwellings permanently shut, and gave a family one month to move into the house allotted to it or lose it. The last one left in 1968.

Whether that was a deportation or a deliverance is still argued in the town. A commission funded by American relief money put anthropologists into the Sassi from 1951, and the museum of peasant civilisation reads their field notes as a record of people asking to be moved. A father of seven told one of them that his house was damp, that the nine of them slept in one room, and that there was no lavatory and no window. It should not be used even for animals, he said.

They were rehoused with some ambition. Borgo La Martella, built a few kilometres out between 1951 and 1954, was promoted by Adriano Olivetti and designed under Ludovico Quaroni. Its plan is an attempt to rebuild the vicinato the clearance had just destroyed. The old city stayed empty for a generation. A law of 1986 declared the recovery a national interest and paid for it. The mechanism it set up is what refilled the place: the Sassi belong to the state, the comune leases them on, and anyone who undertakes the restoration can take a house. Artists went first, then hotels, then everyone. Film crews came for a city that could play Jerusalem, Gibson's Passion in 2004 and a Bond film opening in the streets in 2021, and in 2019 Matera was European Capital of Culture.

Fifty-five kilometres inland stands Craco, on a spur above the upper Cavone among the calanchi, clay badlands eroded into blades and gullies. A landslide in 1963 began the evacuation, a flood in 1972 ended any hope of the old centre, and the earthquake of 1980 brought the final order. Pietro Turzio stayed until 1995, because the house the state gave him did not suit his elderly father.

In the south the Pollino is Italy's largest national park, and it holds the loricate pine, which grows wild in Italy only here and on the massif next door. In 2018 one of them, named Italus, was dated to a first ring in AD 789.

On the thirty kilometres of Tyrrhenian coast, Maratea has a Christ twenty-one metres high above the town, put up in the 1960s by Bruno Innocenti. It is cement faced with chips of Carrara marble, and it faces inland. The count who commissioned it told the sculptor that from a distance it should seem to look out to sea, but that it must in fact turn to the land and the church behind it.

Back in the gorge, the Sassi that held fifteen to twenty thousand people now hold somewhere between fifteen hundred and three thousand. Property there has risen about tenfold since the listing, and some of those who came back in the 1990s have gone again, priced out of the houses their grandparents were moved out of.
