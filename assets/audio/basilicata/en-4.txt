Matera: the cave city.

A city carved out of a ravine, inhabited for nine thousand years, emptied by law in 1952 as a national disgrace, and now a World Heritage site and a film set. How Matera fell and came back.

Matera is built into a limestone gorge on the eastern edge of Basilicata, and for most of its history it was not built at all but dug. The two districts called the Sassi, the stones, are a warren of houses, churches, stables and cisterns cut into the soft rock of a ravine, stacked in a dozen levels connected by stairs and shared courtyards. People have lived here since the Palaeolithic, which makes Matera one of the oldest continuously inhabited places on earth. In 1952 the Italian state emptied it by law, and forty years later UNESCO listed it. That reversal is the story worth telling.

How the city works.

A house in the Sassi is a room cut horizontally into the rock, with a built façade added in front of it, so that from outside it looks like an ordinary street and from inside it is a cave. The rock is calcarenite, soft enough to cut with hand tools and hard enough to hold a vault. There is no natural water in the gorge, so every house had a cistern beneath it, fed by channels that collected rain from the roofs and the street, filtered through sand. The largest of them, the Palombaro Lungo under the main square, is fifteen metres deep and was only rediscovered in the 1990s. Houses opened onto a shared courtyard, the vicinato, with a communal oven and a well, and the courtyard rather than the family was the social unit.

Cut into the ravine walls and the plateau across the gorge are more than a hundred rock churches, dug and frescoed between the eighth and thirteenth centuries by Byzantine and Benedictine monks. Some are simple caves with a painted apse; some reproduce a full basilica, columns and all, entirely in the rock. The frescoes in the Crypt of Original Sin, in a cave outside the town, are ninth-century and were only cleaned in this century.

1952: the city is emptied.

By the 1940s the Sassi held about 15,000 people, roughly half the town, in conditions that had stopped being tolerable. Families lived in single caves with their animals; there was no sewerage; infant mortality was around four times the national average, and malaria was endemic. Carlo Levi, exiled to Basilicata by the fascist government in 1935 and writing about it in Cristo si è fermato a Eboli in 1945, put a description of the Sassi in the mouth of his sister, who had visited: a gorge of caves in which children with the faces of the old asked her for quinine. The book was read across Italy and the Sassi became a national embarrassment.

The response was Law 619 of 17 May 1952, proposed by the local deputy Emilio Colombo and passed without opposition: the Sassi were to be evacuated and new districts built on the plateau above. Between 1952 and the mid-1960s about 15,000 people were moved out, and the old city was left empty, locked and crumbling for a generation.

And back again.

The return began quietly. A law of 1986 funded restoration and allowed people to take on the empty houses; artists, then hotels, then everyone else followed. UNESCO listed the Sassi and the rock churches in 1993, calling them an exceptional record of how people lived with a difficult landscape over nine millennia. Film crews arrived, because Matera looks like Jerusalem and costs less: Pasolini shot his gospel here in 1964, Mel Gibson filmed the Passion in 2004, and a James Bond film opened in the streets in 2021. In 2019 Matera was European Capital of Culture. The town that was cleared as a disgrace now has a housing shortage.

Craco, 40 km south, was abandoned after landslides from 1963, a flood in 1972 and the 1980 earthquake, and stands empty on its cliff.

The rest of the region.

Basilicata is worth more than one town. Craco, on a ridge above the Cavone valley, was abandoned in stages after landslides began in 1963 and stands roofless and empty; it has been used as a film set for the same reasons Matera has. The calanchi, clay badlands eroded into blades and gullies, run for kilometres around it. In the south the Pollino, Italy's largest national park, holds the loricate pines that grow nowhere else in the country and survive for a thousand years on bare rock. And on the short Tyrrhenian coast, Maratea has a marble Christ twenty-one metres tall standing on the mountain above the sea, facing inland rather than out, which the town will explain to you at length.
