Volcanic soils and ancient grapes.

Campania grows grapes the Greeks and Romans knew, on soils made of ash and lava. Why volcanic ground matters, and what Aglianico, Falanghina, Fiano and Greco actually are.

Campania is the oldest wine country in Italy. The Greeks who founded Cumae and Naples in the eighth century BC brought vines with them and called the whole peninsula Oenotria, the land of trained vines. The Romans made Falernian here, the most famous wine of the ancient world. Then came phylloxera, war and industry, and by 1970 the region's wine was mostly anonymous bulk. What has happened since is one of Italy's real revivals, and it rests on two things: old grape varieties nobody else has, and soil made of fire.

What the volcano gives.

Three volcanic systems shape the region. Vesuvius, still active, last erupted in 1944 and covers the coast around Naples in layers of ash, pumice and lava. West of the city the Campi Flegrei, the burning fields, are a collapsed caldera of forty vents where the ground still steams at Solfatara. Inland, ancient eruptions dropped ash over the hills of Irpinia, where it weathered into the yellow tuff that gives the village of Tufo its name.

Volcanic soil is not magic, but it does specific things. It drains fast and holds heat, it is rich in potassium and poor in organic matter, and it stresses the vine just enough to make small, concentrated berries. It is also loose and sandy, which is why phylloxera, the root louse that destroyed European vineyards after 1870, could never move through it. Vines on Vesuvius and in the Campi Flegrei still grow ungrafted, on their own roots, some of them well over a century old, which is rare almost everywhere else in Europe.

The red grape: Aglianico.

Aglianico is one of Italy's three great red grapes, alongside Nebbiolo and Sangiovese, and the least known abroad. The name was long explained as a corruption of Hellenic, suggesting a Greek origin, though the linguistics are now doubted and the vine may simply be native. It ripens very late, often into November, keeps ferocious acidity and tannin, and needs a warm site and a patient winemaker. Young Aglianico can be brutal: black fruit behind a wall of tannin. With ten years it becomes something else, savoury and smoky, with sour cherry, tobacco, iron and ash.

Key facts.

Campania has four DOCG wines: Taurasi, Fiano di Avellino, Greco di Tufo and Aglianico del Taburno.

Vesuvius last erupted in March 1944, destroying vineyards and two villages; the mountain is monitored constantly and about 700,000 people live in the red zone.

Ungrafted vines survive in the sandy volcanic soils of Vesuvius, the Campi Flegrei and the island of Ischia.

Falernian, the Romans' most prized wine, came from the slopes of Monte Massico in the north of the region; the modern Falerno del Massico DOC revives the name.

The white grapes.

Campania's whites are its surprise. Most of southern Italy makes soft, low-acid whites for drinking young; these do the opposite.

Fiano is waxy and nutty, with hazelnut, pear and honey, a texture like lanolin and enough acidity to age ten years. Pliny may have known it as vitis apiana, the vine of the bees, for its sweet ripe grapes. Greco, brought from Thessaly according to tradition, is tighter and more mineral, with green apple, peach skin and a faintly bitter, smoky finish from the sulphurous tuff it grows in. Falanghina is the everyday one, floral and citrus-fresh, grown around the Campi Flegrei and in the Sannio hills, and it is the wine Naples drinks with fried food and pizza. Coda di Volpe, "fox's tail", named for the curved shape of its bunch, is the traditional white of Vesuvius.

Lacryma Christi and the islands.

The wine of Vesuvius carries the best name in Italian wine: Lacryma Christi del Vesuvio, the tears of Christ, from a legend that Christ wept over the lost beauty of the bay and vines grew where the tears fell. The white, from Coda di Volpe and Verdeca, is saline and smoky; the red, from Piedirosso and Aglianico, is light and savoury and should be served cool. Piedirosso, locally per' e palummo, "pigeon's foot", for the red stalks of its bunch, is the other Vesuvian red, low in tannin and easy to drink young. Out in the bay, Ischia got one of Italy's first DOCs in 1966, and the terraces of the Amalfi coast make small amounts of wine from grapes found nowhere else, grown on pergolas above the sea.

In the glass: Lacryma Christi del Vesuvio Rosso.

 Colour: Light to medium ruby.

 Nose: Red cherry, pomegranate, dried herbs, a smoky mineral note.

 Palate: Medium body, fresh acidity, soft tannin, savoury and saline finish.

 Alcohol: 12.5–13.5%.

 Serve: 14–16 °C, slightly cool; drink within five years.

 At the table: Pizza, pasta with tomato, grilled fish, salumi.

Before you move on.

Volcanic, sandy soils resisted phylloxera, so Campania still has ungrafted vines a century old.

Aglianico is the great red: late-ripening, high in acid and tannin, superb with age.

Fiano is waxy and nutty, Greco tighter and mineral, Falanghina the fresh everyday white.
