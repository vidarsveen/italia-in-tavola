Volcanic soils and ancient grapes.

Growers met at Bacoli, on the bay west of Naples, over three days in January 2025 to compare notes on vines that nobody had ever grafted. They farm the black ground under Vesuvius and around the Campi Flegrei, where the root louse that emptied Europe's vineyards never got through. Everyone assumes the volcano did that. It was the sand.

Phylloxera is an aphid that feeds on vine roots, and it has to reach them. It works through the soil from one root to the next, which takes ground that will hold the shape of a tunnel. Sand will not. The galleries collapse behind the insect and it dries out, and the life cycle breaks. The thresholds quoted in the literature are less than about three per cent clay, or more than about sixty-five per cent sand. Ash and pumice lie that loose.

So the vines under Vesuvius and around the Campi Flegrei were never grafted onto American roots. Neither were the vines at Colares, on the Atlantic coast of Portugal, or those in the sand of the Camargue, where there is no volcano to thank. The louse crossed into Italy in 1879 and worked south province by province. No record says when it arrived in Campania, or how far into the region it got.

What the volcanic ground does do is drain fast and hold the day's heat. Growers will add that it is poor and rich in potassium, and that this stresses the vine into small, concentrated berries. The vines themselves can be counted. One Vesuvian estate farms twelve hectares of ungrafted vines between two hundred and three hundred and fifty metres, and there are vines a century old in the Campi Flegrei. The oldest claims in the region are not on a volcano at all. On the terraces at Tramonti, above Amalfi, stumps of Tintore are said to be three to five hundred years old, on the estate's own account.

Vesuvius last erupted on 18 March 1944, over about ten days. Lava took the edges of Massa di Somma and San Sebastiano al Vesuvio and reached Cercola, and ten to twelve thousand people were moved out, most of them to Portici. The twenty-six who died were not killed by lava. They died under roofs that gave way beneath the weight of fallen ash, at Terzigno, at Pagani and elsewhere. That ash is the loose ground the vines root in.

West of the city the Campi Flegrei, the burning fields, are a caldera twelve or thirteen kilometres across with at least twenty-four craters and structures in it, some of them under the sea. The last eruption lasted a week in September 1538 and left a cone a hundred and thirty metres high, Monte Nuovo. The floor still vents steam and sulphur at Solfatara, and the whole caldera rises and falls by measurable amounts.

Inland is where the description usually slips. Irpinia, the province of Avellino, gets called volcanic because ash fell on it and because the village of Tufo is named for the yellow stone under it. The growers' own consortium describes something else: calcareous clay with volcanic deposits lying on the surface. An hour east, the Fiano grows on clay and limestone.

Everyone's story about Aglianico is that it is Greek, and that the name is a worn-down hellenica. The name is not recorded at all before the fifteenth century, and it first appears in print, as aglianiche, in 1520, by which time the south was under Spanish rule. DNA work has since found little relation between it and Greek varieties. What is certain is the ripening. Aglianico is picked from the second half of October into November, and it keeps acidity and tannin that a southern summer ought to have burned away. Young, it is black fruit behind a wall. After ten years it turns savoury and smoky, with sour cherry, tobacco and iron.

The white grown on Vesuvius was sold and described as Coda di Volpe for a century, fox's tail, for the long curved shape of its bunch. Pliny would have recognised the name: he lists a cauda vulpium among the vines fit for a pergola. It was the wrong name. Morphological work and then DNA separated the Vesuvian vine from Coda di Volpe and disproved the kinship outright, and Caprettone entered the national register of varieties on 20 October 2014. It grows only in the province of Naples. In 2020 the Vesuvio rules gained a Caprettone of their own. The rule for Lacryma Christi Bianco still calls the grape Coda di Volpe.

The red of the mountain is Piedirosso, which Naples calls per' e palummo, pigeon's foot, for the red stalks its bunches hang from. The dialect name is the old one. The Italian name is barely a century old: the ampelographer Michele Carlucci coined Piedirosso in 1909.

The everyday white of Naples, drunk with fried fish and pizza, is Falanghina, and it is two grapes rather than one. Falanghina Flegrea grows on volcanic sand west of the city. Falanghina Beneventana grows inland, on clay and limestone. DNA work in the 2000s found them to be distinct varieties rather than clones, differing in most of the characters an ampelographer counts. A wine list gives both the same single word. Fiano and Greco belong to the hills inland, where the ground is not volcanic and the nights are cold.

The wine of the mountain carries the best name in Italian wine. In one version Lucifer, thrown out of Paradise, tore a piece of it away and dragged it down to the Gulf of Naples. Christ saw Paradise in the bay, wept, and vines grew where the tears fell. In the other, Christ came to the slopes in disguise and asked a hermit for water, then turned what he was given into wine. No document dates the name Lacryma Christi. The earliest custodians anyone can name are the Capuchin friars of Torre del Greco. The white is built on Caprettone, with Verdeca permitted alongside it; the red is light, savoury and better served cool.

Out in the bay, Ischia was given one of Italy's first DOCs in March 1966, for wines made from Forastera, Biancolella and Guarnaccia. On the Amalfi terraces, under seventy hectares of them, the old Tintore stumps still carry fruit on pergolas above the sea. The vines will go on being ungrafted either way, for a reason that has nothing to do with the mountain.
