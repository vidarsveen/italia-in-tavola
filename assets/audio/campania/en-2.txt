Taurasi, Fiano and Greco.

Forty kilometres inland the coast turns into mountains, and three small zones make wines that can stand beside Barolo. The rules of Taurasi, and the two white DOCGs beside it.

Drive east from Naples and within an hour the sea is gone. The province of Avellino, known by its old name Irpinia, is a landscape of steep green hills between 400 and 700 metres, cold in winter, snowy some years, with a continental swing between day and night temperatures that no coastal vineyard has. Three of Campania's four DOCG wines come from these hills, and they are the reason the region is taken seriously.

Taurasi, the Barolo of the south.

Taurasi was made DOCG in 1993, the first red in southern Italy to be so classified. It is at least 85% Aglianico, grown in seventeen communes along the Calore valley, and it must age three years before release, four for Riserva, with at least one year in wood. In practice the good ones need ten years more. The nickname "the Barolo of the south" is a lazy comparison, but it points at something real: high acidity, drying tannin, a savoury rather than fruity profile, and a wine built to last decades rather than please immediately.

The revival has a name attached to it. Antonio Mastroberardino kept working with Aglianico, Fiano and Greco through the decades when everyone else was pulling them out for higher-yielding international varieties, and his 1968 Taurasi Riserva showed what the grape could do. After the earthquake of 1980, which devastated Irpinia, the family replanted rather than leave. The same winery later helped replant ancient varieties inside the walls of Pompeii.

Campania's four DOCGs.

Taurasi: min. 85% Aglianico, three years' ageing (four for Riserva), at least one in wood.

Fiano di Avellino: min. 85% Fiano, from 26 communes around Avellino; DOCG since 2003.

Greco di Tufo: min. 85% Greco, from eight communes around Tufo; DOCG since 2003, and made as a sparkling wine too.

Aglianico del Taburno: from the Taburno massif in Benevento province; DOCG since 2011, softer and earlier-drinking than Taurasi.

Fiano di Avellino.

The white that ages. Fiano grown at 400 to 600 metres on clay and limestone gives a wine that is almost closed when young, all wax and green almond, and opens after three to five years into hazelnut, honey, dried herbs and smoke. It is one of very few Italian whites outside Friuli and Alto Adige that genuinely improves in bottle for a decade. Serve it too cold and you taste nothing; 12 °C is better than 8.

Greco di Tufo.

The village of Tufo sits on a hill of the yellow volcanic stone it is named after, and under it are the sulphur mines that worked from 1866 until 1982. The Greco grown on that ground makes a firmer, more angular wine than Fiano: green apple, peach skin, crushed stone, a bitter almond twist and a smoky, sulphurous edge that regulars look for. It is thick-skinned and prone to rot, so it is picked with care, and it takes well to a second fermentation, which is why good metodo classico sparkling wine is now made from it.

In the glass: Taurasi Riserva.

 Colour: Deep ruby turning garnet, brick at the rim with age.

 Nose: Sour cherry, plum, leather, tobacco, wood smoke, iron.

 Palate: Full body, very high acidity, firm drying tannin, long savoury finish.

 Alcohol: 13.5–14.5%.

 Serve: 18 °C, decanted two hours; best from ten years, good ones live thirty.

 At the table: Ragù napoletano, lamb, game, aged caciocavallo and pecorino.

The rest of the map.

North of Naples the Sannio hills around Benevento are the volume producers, and the source of most Falanghina. Falerno del Massico revives the Roman name on the slopes near the border with Lazio. The Cilento, the wild coast south of Salerno, makes Aglianico and Fiano at a lower price. And the Costa d'Amalfi DOC covers a handful of hectares of terraces held up by dry stone walls above the sea, where the grapes have names like Tintore, Ginestra and Pepella and the work is done by hand and by mule.

Before you move on.

Irpinia, the hills inland of Naples, is high, cool and continental; it holds three of Campania's four DOCGs.

Taurasi is at least 85% Aglianico, aged three years, and needs another ten in bottle.

Fiano di Avellino ages and deepens; Greco di Tufo is firmer, smokier and makes fine sparkling wine.
