Taurasi, Fiano and Greco.

A woman outside Tufo set a stone alight, and it went on burning. Francesco di Marzo, riding past in 1866, stopped to look. Stones do not burn. That one did. The ground under the village of Tufo is full of sulphur, and within a few years the land between Tufo and Altavilla Irpina carried one of the largest industrial works in southern Italy.

At its height the district put about thirteen hundred men underground. The money built a village with a railway station, and the station is what changed the wine. The di Marzo family, growing a white grape on the hill above since at least the 1830s, now had a way to move it. They sent Greco north to France in quantity, and the house says the idea of trying the French bottle fermentation came out of that trade. Tufo has made a metodo classico sparkling wine ever since.

American sulphur undercut the mines after the war, and sulphur recovered as a by-product from oil refineries undercut them again. Extraction stopped around 1972. The works closed about 1983 with seven men left, after roughly a hundred and eighteen years. Some of the vineyards sit directly above the abandoned galleries.

Tasters reach for sulphur and smoke when they describe Greco di Tufo, and the temptation to join those two facts is considerable. Nobody has shown they are connected. The fullest account of the village sets the mines and the wine down one after the other, first the one and then the other, and declines to draw a line between them.

Greco has a second life on the flat land between Naples and Caserta, where it goes by the name Asprinio and where the DNA says it is the same grape. There it is not grown on a hill at all. It is married to poplars and climbs them, fifteen metres up and twenty in places, with no wires and no posts. One mother plant can cover two hundred square metres and carry two hundred and twenty kilos of grapes. Pruning, tying and picking are all done from a narrow ladder called a scalillo, light enough for one man to carry.

In the 1960s this system covered something like sixteen thousand hectares between the two provinces. Today it is under two hundred, and the whole territory of the Aversa DOC amounts to about thirty-two. The figures come from two press accounts that share a phrasing. The reason for the collapse is not mysterious. Nothing about a vine fifteen metres in the air can be mechanised, and the land underneath it was wanted for houses.

These hills are cold in winter and swing hard between day and night, which no vineyard on the coast does, and three of Campania's four DOCG wines come off them. Taurasi is at least 85% Aglianico, from seventeen communes along the Calore valley, and it was raised to DOCG in 1993, having been a DOC since 1970. It is usually called the first red of the Italian south to be promoted. The zone is far smaller than the reputation, at about 472 hectares and something over six thousand hectolitres a year.

The vineyards standing in Irpinia now were rebuilt in a countryside the earthquake helped to empty. The quake of 23 November 1980 killed 2,914 people and damaged 688 comuni, half of them losing their housing stock entirely. The reconstruction that followed pushed more people off the land and out of farming.

Through much of the post-war period Mastroberardino made more than half of all the DOC wine in Campania. The firm was founded at Avellino in 1878. Antonio Mastroberardino, the tenth generation, stayed with Aglianico, Fiano and Greco when the money was in Cabernet and Chardonnay. The 1968 Taurasi Riserva he made with his brother Walter was named one of Wine Spectator's twelve wines of the century in 1999. He died on 28 January 2014. The brothers had divided the business in 1994. Antonio kept the name and the winery, and Walter took much of the vineyard and founded Terredora.

In 1996 grape pips came out of the excavation beside the Villa dei Misteri at Pompeii, and the laboratory handling them, under Anna Maria Ciarallo, called the family in. Plaster casts of the voids in the ash showed where the vine stakes had stood two thousand years earlier. Chestnut poles went back into the same holes, in rows running north to south, on the ground level of AD 79. The first harvest came in September 1999, and the 2001 vintage made 1,721 bottles.

Fiano grows between four and six hundred metres on clay and limestone, and it gives almost nothing away when young: wax, green almond, and not much besides. Three to five years in bottle bring hazelnut, honey, dried herbs and smoke. The rules recognise the keeping, and allow a Riserva with a minimum year's ageing. Serve it too cold and you taste none of it; twelve degrees is better than eight. The Romans had a vine they called apiana, the vine of the bees, for grapes sweet enough to draw them, and whether Fiano descends from it is a hypothesis rather than a finding. The hypothesis has a legal existence all the same: the DOCG permits the word Apianum on the label, in letters no more than half the height of the denomination.

Greco makes a firmer, more angular wine than Fiano, with green apple, peach skin, crushed stone and a twist of bitter almond. It is also the largest of the three by a distance, at six hundred-odd hectares and around thirty-five thousand hectolitres, which is more wine than Taurasi and Fiano di Avellino together. The grape is thick-skinned and ripens in late September or early October, before Aglianico. What troubles it is mildew, and flowers that drop or set shot berries, so the crop is unreliable rather than delicate. It takes a second fermentation well, which is where the sparkling wine comes from.

Inland to the north-east, the province of Benevento — the Sannio on the label — has led Campania in vineyard area and in volume for twenty years, and most Falanghina comes from there. That is the cheap end of the region, and so, more quietly, is the Cilento south of Salerno. The opposite case is the Costa d'Amalfi, fewer than seventy hectares worked by hand on terraces above the sea, where growers still pick a red called Tintore that grows almost nowhere else.

At the northern edge of the region, on the slopes of Monte Massico, a DOC carries the most famous name in Roman wine. Falernian was white, it was kept fifteen or twenty years until it turned amber, and a bar in Pompeii charged four asses for it when ordinary wine cost one. Which grape it was made from, nobody can say. When Falerno del Massico was drawn up in 1989, two traditions claimed the name, one built on Aglianico and one on Primitivo, and the decree did not choose between them. It wrote both into the rules.
