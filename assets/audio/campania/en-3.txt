Pizza, mozzarella and the Neapolitan table.

The most copied dish in the world was street food for the poor of Naples. What makes a real Neapolitan pizza, why the mozzarella comes from buffalo, and what else the city eats.

Naples eats better than almost anywhere in Italy on almost no money. This is a city that has been crowded, poor and inventive for four hundred years, and its cooking is built from flour, tomatoes, cheese, fish and vegetables, made brilliant by technique and by ingredients that happen to be the best in the world at what they do. Its most famous product is a disc of dough that has conquered the planet and is still, at home, a two-euro lunch eaten folded in four.

What a Neapolitan pizza is.

The rules are written down. The Associazione Verace Pizza Napoletana, founded in 1984, and the EU's Traditional Speciality Guaranteed status of 2010 define pizza napoletana: soft wheat flour, water, salt and yeast only; dough left to rise at least eight hours; shaped by hand, never with a rolling pin; a rim of one to two centimetres; a wood-fired dome oven at about 485 °C; sixty to ninety seconds of baking. What comes out is soft, foldable and blistered black in spots, not crisp. It is eaten with a knife and fork on a plate, or folded into a portafoglio, a wallet, and eaten walking.

There are two classic toppings and everything else is a variation. Marinara: tomato, garlic, oregano, oil, no cheese, and the older of the two. Margherita: tomato, mozzarella, basil, oil. The story is that Raffaele Esposito made it for Queen Margherita of Savoy on her visit in 1889 and named it for her, the colours matching the new Italian flag. The letter of thanks from the royal household exists; historians doubt the rest, and pizza with those toppings was certainly sold in Naples before that. Either way the name stuck.

Key facts.

Tomatoes reached Europe from the Americas in the sixteenth century and were thought poisonous; Naples was among the first places to eat them, and pizza as we know it dates from the late eighteenth century.

San Marzano tomatoes, grown in the volcanic soil of the Sarno valley, are long, low in seeds and acid, and are the DOP tomato for pizza and sauce.

Pizza fritta, deep-fried filled dough, was the wartime pizza, sold when there was no fuel for the ovens.

Naples has around 8,000 pizzerias; Da Michele, opened in 1870, serves only marinara and margherita.

Mozzarella di bufala.

Water buffalo have been kept in the marshy plains around Caserta and Salerno since at least the twelfth century, and their milk makes a cheese that cow's milk cannot imitate: Mozzarella di Bufala Campana DOP, white, glossy, elastic outside and soft inside, releasing sweet whey when cut. The name comes from mozzare, to cut off, the twist of the hand that separates each ball from the rope of stretched curd. It is a fresh cheese and it is at its best within a day, kept in its own whey at room temperature, never in the fridge. Buffalo milk has roughly twice the fat of cow's milk, which is why the cheese tastes as it does.

Pasta, the sea and the Sunday sauce.

Naples industrialised dried pasta. The town of Gragnano, on a windy slope above the coast, had the right climate to dry it and by 1800 had eighty pasta workshops; its bronze-drawn, slow-dried pasta is an IGP product today. The classic dishes are cheap and precise. Spaghetti alle vongole, with the small clams of the bay, garlic, oil and parsley, and no cheese. Pasta e patate con la provola, pasta and potatoes with smoked cheese melting through it, the definition of comfort. Spaghetti alla puttanesca, with tomato, olives, capers and anchovy. Pasta e fagioli. Genovese, which despite its name is Neapolitan: beef cooked for hours under a mountain of onions until both collapse into a sweet brown sauce.

And the ragù napoletano, which is not the Bolognese kind. Whole cuts of beef and pork, sometimes rolled and tied as braciole, are browned and then simmered in tomato for four to six hours until the sauce is dark and the meat falls apart. The sauce dresses the pasta as a first course and the meat follows as the second. It is the Sunday dish, started early in the morning, and Neapolitans argue about whether it should be stirred with a wooden spoon and how slowly it should pippiare, mutter, at the edge of a simmer.

Coffee, pastry and lemons.

Neapolitan coffee is roasted dark, ground fine and pulled short and thick, and it is drunk standing at the bar, often already sugared. The tradition of the caffè sospeso, paying for a second coffee for whoever needs one, comes from here. The pastries are among Italy's best: sfogliatella, either riccia, a ribbed shell of hundreds of crisp leaves, or frolla, the same semolina and ricotta filling in shortcrust; babà, a yeast cake soaked in rum, brought by French chefs in the eighteenth century and now more Neapolitan than French; and pastiera, the Easter tart of wheat berries, ricotta and orange flower water. From the Amalfi coast and Sorrento come the great lemons, thick-skinned and sweet-scented, eaten in salads, made into limoncello and used in the delizia al limone.

Before you move on.

Real pizza napoletana is soft and foldable: eight hours' rise, hand-shaped, 485 °C, ninety seconds.

Mozzarella di bufala is a fresh buffalo-milk cheese, eaten within a day and never refrigerated.

Ragù napoletano is whole cuts of meat simmered for hours; the sauce dresses the pasta, the meat follows.
