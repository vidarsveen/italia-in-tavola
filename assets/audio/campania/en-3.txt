Pizza, mozzarella and the Neapolitan table.

The pizzeria had copper pans, an oven never allowed to go out and every kind on offer, and it was nowhere near Naples. A Neapolitan businessman had opened it, reasoning that what his own city loved the rest of the world would love too. Matilde Serao, writing in 1884, recorded that it failed. Taken out of its Neapolitan setting, she said, the pizza seemed a false note, and represented an indigestion.

The method travelled where the dish could not. When UNESCO added Neapolitan pizza to its heritage list in December 2017, the entry was the art of the pizzaiuolo. It lists four phases of handling dough, and a rotating movement of the baker's arm at the mouth of a wood oven. The bearers it names are the master pizzaiuolo, the pizzaiuolo and the baker. About three thousand of them work in the city.

The European specification that made pizza napoletana a Traditional Speciality Guaranteed in 2010 is unexpectedly exact. It fixes the salt in the water, the weight of each ball of dough and the hours it must rest. It asks for brewer's yeast by name, three grams of it to nearly two kilos of flour. The disc is four millimetres thick in the middle inside a rim of one to two centimetres, no wider than 35 centimetres, and it bakes for sixty to ninety seconds. It is shaped by hand and never with a rolling pin. The text even sets the temperature the mozzarella must reach, 65 to 70 degrees.

It says nothing at all about the type of flour, and the reason is that Germany objected. A rule admitting only one country's flour, the objection ran, put German wheat at a disadvantage; an agreement was notified in February 2009 and the clause came out. Poland objected separately that the name was not distinctive enough to protect. What survives is a specification with no "00" in it, only a table of numbers a miller would recognise.

The pizzaioli have a rulebook of their own, and it does not agree with the European one. The Associazione Verace Pizza Napoletana, set up in 1984 by seventeen master pizzaioli, requires type 00 or 0 flour and eight to twenty-four hours of fermentation. It puts 485 degrees at the dome of the oven and 380 to 430 on the floor, which is the exact reverse of the regulation. Both numbers are real, and they belong to different documents and different surfaces.

The protection is thinner than it sounds, too. The name was registered without reservation, so anyone anywhere may still call a pizza a pizza napoletana; what is protected is the logo and the claim to have followed the tradition. The finished pizza, the regulation says, must be soft, elastic and easily foldable into four.

Serao's chapter on what Naples eats puts pizza among the things that cost one soldo. The man with a shop worked at night, she wrote, turning out round flatbreads of dense dough that scorched rather than baked, loaded with barely cooked tomato, garlic, pepper and oregano. She lists the four kinds on sale: tomato; mozzarella and cheese; anchovies and oil; oil, oregano and garlic. The slices went out through the alleys cut into one-soldo wedges on a great convex tin shield balanced on a boy's head, and there were two-centesimi pieces for children going to school. Food priced like that is eaten standing, and what is eaten standing has to fold. Naples still eats it so, folded into a portafoglio, a wallet, or flat on a plate with a knife and fork.

It had been that way for a while. Emanuele Rocco, writing in 1858, called the ordinary pizzas the ones with garlic and oil. Others, he said, were covered with grated cheese and lard and a few leaves of basil, others again with thin slices of mozzarella, and sometimes with ham, tomato or clams. Alexandre Dumas, who travelled the coast in 1835, listed much the same range and noticed that the price moved with the market, since yesterday's pizza could not be sold at today's rate.

Which is the difficulty with the story everyone knows. A marinara is tomato, garlic, oregano and oil, with no cheese at all; a margherita adds mozzarella and basil. A letter dated 11 June 1889, signed by Camillo Galli of the royal table service, told Raffaele Esposito that the three kinds of pizza prepared for Queen Margherita of Savoy had been found excellent. That is the whole of it: three pizzas, no mozzarella, no basil, no flag, and nobody named after the queen. The letter hangs in the pizzeria, which trades today as Brandi, and what is disputed is not whether it exists but whether it is genuine. The historian Zachary Nowak has argued that the signature does not match Galli's own of 1891, and that the palace used printed letterhead rather than a handwritten sheet. The seal sits in the wrong place, and the letter is addressed to a surname no Italian man of the period would have used. No newspaper of 1889 mentions any of it, and the story surfaces in the 1930s. Rocco, meanwhile, had mozzarella, basil and tomato among the toppings of Neapolitan pizzas thirty years before the queen arrived.

Water buffalo are in the documents of central and southern Italy by the twelfth century. That is where the record starts, not where the animal arrived. An abbot at Farfa brought buffalo in with his cattle and horses in the 1120s, and an Angevin order of the next century deals with a tamed work buffalo strayed near Salerno. How the animal reached Italy is unsettled: the likeliest route is the Arab one through Sicily, and Norman and Swabian administrators were taxing buffalo herds in Sicily by the twelfth century.

What the milk does is chemistry. Buffalo milk carries at least 7.2 per cent fat against about 3.8 in cow's milk, so roughly twice. The casein and the calcium rise with it, and those are what set a firm curd quickly and let it stretch. The curd ripens under its own whey until it reaches about pH 5.2. At that point enough calcium has been drawn out of the protein network for it to pull into fibres, and boiling water and a pair of hands turn those fibres into a rope. Mozzare is to cut off, and the name is the cut: each ball is severed from the rope. The whey that weeps out when you slice one is written into the DOP as a quality and not a fault.

The cheese itself, Mozzarella di Bufala Campana DOP, is white and glossy, elastic outside and soft within. Eat it the day it is made if you can. The consortium keeps it in its liquid and says it is still good three or four days later, fridge and all. What matters is bringing it back to eighteen or twenty degrees before it reaches the table.

The town of Gragnano, on a slope above the coast, rebuilt itself around drying pasta. Its registered specification records something unusual. In the middle of the nineteenth century the width of the streets and the height of the buildings were planned so the wind would flow through and help the drying. By tradition there were about seventy workshops at the start of that century and more than a hundred by the 1850s, employing most of the working town. The IGP covers one comune only, requires bronze dies, which leave a rough surface, and allows four to sixty hours of drying between forty and eighty-five degrees.

Cavalcanti has spaghetti alle vongole in print by 1837. He opens the clams in water, strains the liquor back into the pan with garlic and parsley, and finishes the pasta in it, and he already distinguishes the small lupino from the larger verace. Genovese is beef cooked for hours under two to four times its weight in onions until both collapse into a sweet brown sauce, and nobody knows why a Neapolitan dish carries a Ligurian name. The oldest text that bears on it is the Liber de coquina, compiled in Naples, where an onion stew dresses tria ianuensis. There it is the pasta, not the sauce, that is called Genoese. Pasta e patate con la provola, pasta e fagioli and spaghetti alla puttanesca are the everyday dishes beside it, and none of them needs anything expensive.

The ragù napoletano is not the Bolognese kind. Whole cuts of beef and pork, sometimes rolled and tied as braciole, are browned and then simmered in tomato for four to six hours until the sauce is dark and the meat falls apart. The sauce dresses the pasta as a first course and the meat follows as the second. It is the Sunday dish, started early in the morning. The verb for what it does on the heat is pippiare, a Neapolitan word for a simmer so slow it releases one bubble at a time, like someone drawing on a pipe. Eduardo De Filippo built a poem on it, in which a husband tells his wife, carefully, that what she has made is not ragù. It is meat with tomato on it.

Neapolitan coffee is roasted dark, ground fine and pulled short and thick, and it is drunk standing at the bar, often already sugared. The caffè sospeso, paying for a second coffee for whoever needs one, belongs to the city's account of itself. A newspaper went looking for it in 2008 and found it unobserved in the bars it visited. It came back that year through a book, and in 2010 through a café's anniversary.

The city's pastry is the sfogliatella, either riccia, a ribbed shell of hundreds of crisp leaves, or frolla, the same filling in shortcrust. The filling is semolina cooked in milk with ricotta, candied citrus and cinnamon. The convent story attached to it — Santa Rosa at Conca dei Marini, and a pastry-cook called Pintauro who brought it to via Toledo in 1818 — rests on documents nobody has produced. The shop itself trades under the date 1785. The babà, a yeast cake soaked in rum, is French by origin and Neapolitan by adoption. Pastiera is the Easter tart of wheat, ricotta and orange flower water, though the oldest printed recipe, Latini's of the 1690s, seasons it with Parmesan and pepper.
