Vesuvius, Pompeii and the Amalfi coast.

The harvest was in. At Oplontis there were pomegranates; in the houses along the bay, chestnuts and walnuts and dried figs put by. Braziers stood lit in rooms people had walked out of. The dead were found in heavy wool. In the cellars the jars of grape juice had been closed only after some ten days of open fermentation. Everything in those kitchens says autumn. Pliny's letter says the twenty-fourth of August.

For a few days in 2018 it looked as though the argument had been settled. A charcoal scrawl came out of a room under renovation in Regio V, and the director of the site read it as a date in the middle of October. The history books might be being rewritten, the culture minister said. Three days later the park published a second transcription, by a palaeographer from Pisa: the same marks, she argued, say that they took it in the oil pantry. Only the fragment that looks like a date is agreed on. The note carries no year, a workman left it on a wall about to be plastered, and an experiment has since shown that charcoal writing survives ten months unaltered. The most recent volcanology declines to name a day and counts from the first day of the eruption instead.

On the first morning the eruption column went up past thirty kilometres, and it stood for about eighteen hours. Pumice fell on Pompeii at twelve to fifteen centimetres an hour, so by dawn there was a metre and a half of it on the roofs. Of the 1,044 dead recovered from the town, 394 were already dead before any hot cloud arrived, nine in ten of them under roofs and floors that gave way. Earthquakes during the collapse of the summit brought walls down on people who had survived the stones.

Then the column failed and six surges came down the mountain. The first three stopped short of the north-west wall. The fourth came in and killed, in minutes; whether by heat or by ash in the lungs, two teams of scientists are still arguing. Herculaneum, a smaller and richer town closer to the mountain, went under more than twenty metres. There the heat carbonised what it touched instead of burning it, so doors, beds, roof beams and food came through.

Pompeii was a working town of perhaps eleven or twelve thousand people, and the ash kept the ordinary things. Election slogans and insults stayed painted on the walls, price lists stayed up in the brothels, and the frescoes held their colours because no light or air reached them. The bodies decayed and left voids in the hardened ash. Giuseppe Fiorelli, who ran the excavation from 1860, filled four of them with plaster on 3 February 1863. Others had tried it in 1831 and in 1861, and he made it work. A little over a hundred casts have been made since, against the remains of more than a thousand victims.

In 2024 a team took ancient DNA from fourteen casts that were under restoration. The adult wearing a golden bracelet with a child on its lap, read for a century as a mother and her child, was an unrelated adult male. The pair in the House of the Cryptoporticus, long taken for two sisters, included at least one genetic male, and those two were not related either. Jewellery had been read as femininity and closeness as family, and neither inference survived the test.

Bread came from bakeries, more than thirty of them, each milling its own grain on basalt stones turned by donkeys, because the poorer citizens had no oven and in some cases no kitchen either. One bakery was found with eighty-one loaves still inside and an iron bar across the oven door. The standard loaf was scored before baking into wedges, and one of them, carbonised at Herculaneum, carries a stamp naming Celer, slave of Quintus Granius Verus. That is an owner's mark, not a baker's brand.

What the work was like came out of the ground in 2023. In a house under renovation in Regio IX, the milling room has no door to the street. The only way into the rest of the house is through the atrium, and its windows are set high in the wall and barred. The basalt floor around four millstones is cut with semicircular grooves, to guide the animals round and stop them slipping. Apuleius describes the same room, with blindfolded donkeys and enslaved workers at the mills.

The man who sold the fish sauce put his own name on his floor. Aulus Umbricius Scaurus had the mosaic in his atrium laid with pictures of his own jars and their labels: the flower of garum, made of mackerel, from the shop of Scaurus. His name is on nearly a third of the fish-sauce jars found in the two towns. In a converted house six great jars stood sunk in the garden, five of them still holding dried sauce packed with the vertebrae of anchovies. When they were opened in 1960, the smell of fish was reported to be fresh.

The best evidence of ordinary eating is less dignified than any of that. Two hundred and twenty litres of soil came out of the sewer under a block of flats and shops at Herculaneum. It held a hundred and ninety-four kinds of plant and animal, a hundred and thirteen of them edible. There were figs, olives, grapes, carrot, chicory, garlic and sea urchin, and black pepper carried from India into a building nobody would call grand. The fish were mostly small and young, sea bream and sardines and horse mackerel, netted in the shallows and sold cheap. Seafood was a regular part of the diet there, and a minor one.

South of the bay the Sorrentine peninsula falls into the sea in cliffs, and the Costiera Amalfitana runs along its southern side. Amalfi declared itself independent of Byzantium in 839 and took a duke of its own in 958. For two centuries it carried timber and weapons east and brought spices back, and struck its own coin to do it.

A marine inundation on 25 November 1343 wrecked the port. It is usually given as the end of Amalfi, though the Normans had taken the town in 1073 and Pisa had sacked it twice by 1137. The wave has a better explanation than a storm: a submarine landslide, set off by a flank collapse of Stromboli.

What the coast has instead is a landscape built by hand and held up by dry stone. The walls are called macere, they stand three to seven metres, and nobody has ever measured them all. The one proper survey, of twenty-three square kilometres behind Amalfi and Ravello, found a hundred and sixty-three kilometres of terracing in that stretch alone. When a wall stops being maintained the terrace above it slides, and abandoned terracing is the documented cause of the landslides.

The lemon grown on them is the Sfusato Amalfitano: spindle-shaped, at least a hundred grams, with a thick sweet pith under a rind heavy in oil. The rules require its juice to be both plentiful and sharp. The trees are trained flat under chestnut posts at least a hundred and eighty centimetres high, and covered with mats. The cover slows the fruit down, so picking runs from February into August, by which time the field lemons of Sicily are long finished. Crates come down by electric pulley where a grower could afford to put one in, and on people's backs where nobody could, in baskets of up to seventy kilos. The fruit goes into salads and pasta, into limoncello, and into the lemon cakes of every bar on the coast.

Inland at Paestum, on the plain south of Salerno, three Greek temples stand nearly whole. One of Hera dates from about 550 BC, one of Athena from about 510, and a second of Hera from about 460, the best preserved of the three. Colonists from Sybaris founded the city around 600 BC and called it Poseidonia. It survived because the plain around it went bad. The drainage failed, the water stood, malaria came, the bishopric was suppressed in 1100, and for six hundred years almost nobody had reason to go there and carry the stone away. The marsh that emptied the plain is why the temples are still standing, and the buffalo whose milk becomes mozzarella graze the ground it left behind.
