Vesuvius, Pompeii and the Amalfi coast.

In AD 79 a mountain buried two cities and kept them. What the eruption left, what it tells us about how Romans ate, and the coast of lemons and terraces beyond it.

On an autumn day in AD 79, probably in late October, Vesuvius blew a column of ash and pumice twenty kilometres into the sky and held it there for about eighteen hours. Pumice fell on Pompeii until roofs collapsed. Then the column failed, and surges of gas and rock at several hundred degrees ran down the mountain, killing everyone still alive in Pompeii and burying Herculaneum under twenty metres of volcanic material. The disaster is the reason we know more about daily life in these two towns than in Rome itself.

What the ash kept.

Pompeii was a working town of perhaps eleven thousand people. Under the ash the archaeologists found streets with stepping stones and wheel ruts, shops with counters, a bakery with carbonised loaves still in the oven, election slogans and insults painted on walls, brothels with price lists, gardens whose root cavities allowed the planting to be reconstructed, and frescoes whose colours survived because no light or air reached them. The bodies decayed but left voids in the hardened ash; from 1863 Giuseppe Fiorelli filled these with plaster and produced the casts of the dead that are the site's most disturbing exhibit.

Herculaneum, a smaller and richer seaside town, was buried differently. The hot surges carbonised rather than burned, so wooden doors, beds, roof beams and even food survived, along with a library of about 1,800 papyrus scrolls in the Villa of the Papyri, charred solid and now being read by X-ray scanning and machine learning without unrolling them.

Roman food, recovered.

The sites are also the best evidence we have of what ordinary Romans ate. Pompeii had around eighty thermopolia, counter-service bars with sunken jars in a masonry counter, selling hot food to people whose flats had no kitchen; a fully painted one excavated in 2020 still had duck, pig, goat, fish and snails in its jars. Bread was baked in communal ovens and stamped with the baker's mark. Garum, the fermented fish sauce that flavoured nearly everything, was made in Pompeii and shipped in jars marked with the maker's name. Wine amphorae, wine bars and painted signs are everywhere, and the vineyards inside the walls have been replanted with old Campanian varieties and now make wine again.

Key facts.

Pliny the Younger, watching from Misenum, wrote the only eyewitness account; his uncle Pliny the Elder died leading a rescue by sea. Eruptions of this type are called Plinian.

Pompeii was rediscovered in 1748; roughly a third of the site is still unexcavated and deliberately left so.

Vesuvius has erupted more than fifty times since; the 1631 eruption killed several thousand people, and the last was in March 1944.

Naples' National Archaeological Museum holds the finds: the mosaics, the frescoes, the Farnese sculptures.

The Amalfi coast.

South of the bay the Sorrentine peninsula falls into the sea in cliffs, and along them runs the Costiera Amalfitana, a UNESCO World Heritage site since 1997. Amalfi itself was an independent maritime republic before Venice or Genoa amounted to anything, trading with Byzantium and North Africa in the tenth and eleventh centuries, and its Tavole Amalfitane were the maritime law of the Mediterranean for centuries. The town was largely destroyed by a storm and landslide in 1343 and never recovered its power.

What is left is a landscape built by hand. The terraces on these slopes are held by dry stone walls that run for hundreds of kilometres in total, and they grow lemons: the Sfusato Amalfitano, long, thick-skinned, with a sweet fragrant zest and little bitterness, carried up and down the steps in baskets because nothing else can reach them. They become limoncello, salads, pasta sauces and the lemon cakes of every bar on the coast. Inland at Paestum, on the plain south of Salerno, three Greek temples from the sixth and fifth centuries BC stand almost intact among the buffalo farms, which is a fair summary of Campania: the ancient world, still standing, in the middle of a working landscape.

Before you move on.

The AD 79 eruption buried Pompeii in pumice and Herculaneum in hot surges, preserving both in different ways.

The sites show ordinary Roman eating: counter bars, communal ovens, garum, wine shops, vineyards inside the walls.

Amalfi was a maritime republic; its terraces grow the Sfusato lemon, and Paestum's Greek temples stand on the plain to the south.
