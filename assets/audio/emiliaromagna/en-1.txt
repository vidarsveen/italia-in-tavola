Lambrusco, properly understood.

The frothy red of Emilia was Italy's most exported wine and its most mocked. What the grapes are, how the wine is made, and why the dry version belongs on the table with everything the region cures and fries.

Lambrusco has a reputation problem, and it earned it honestly. In the 1970s and 1980s sweet, fizzy Lambrusco was the best-selling imported wine in the United States, thirteen million cases in 1985 alone, sold cold as a soft drink with alcohol. What the plain of Emilia actually drinks is something else: a dry, tart, deep-purple sparkling red with a violet foam, served cold in a tumbler with cured pork and fried dough. It is the only wine in Italy that was designed for its food rather than the other way round, and it is one of the few reds that gets better with a chill.

Not one grape but a family.

Lambrusco is a group of related grapes, all native to Emilia, and the name is probably from the Latin labrusca, the wild vine Virgil and Pliny wrote about. Three matter most. Lambrusco di Sorbara grows on the sandy plain north of Modena between the rivers Secchia and Panaro. It is the palest, most acidic and most perfumed, tasting of sour cherry and violets, and it has a quirk: its flowers often fail to set fruit, so yields are low and it is usually blended with a little Salamino to fill the bunches. Lambrusco Grasparossa di Castelvetro grows in the foothills south of Modena and is the opposite, dark, tannic, full and blackberry-scented, the Lambrusco that behaves most like a red wine. Lambrusco Salamino di Santa Croce, named for bunches shaped like small salami, is the most planted, and sits between the two. Around Reggio Emilia and Parma the Maestri, Marani and Montericco varieties fill out the blends, and across the Po the Mantovano version belongs to Lombardy.

Key facts.

Main DOCs: Lambrusco di Sorbara, Lambrusco Grasparossa di Castelvetro and Lambrusco Salamino di Santa Croce, all created in 1970, plus Lambrusco di Modena, Reggiano and Colli di Parma.

Styles: secco (dry), semisecco, amabile and dolce; and by pressure, frizzante (lightly sparkling, the traditional form) or spumante.

Most Lambrusco gets its bubbles from a second fermentation in a pressurised tank (the Charmat or Martinotti method). A growing minority is refermented in the bottle and left on its sediment, the old farmhouse method, and a few are made like Champagne.

Serve at 10–12 °C. Alcohol is low, usually 10.5–12%.

How the fizz gets in.

Before the tank, Lambrusco was made the way every farm made it: fermentation stopped by the winter cold, wine bottled in spring with a little sugar left, and the warmth of May finishing the job in the bottle. The result was cloudy, dry, alive, and drunk within the year. The Charmat method, in which the second fermentation happens in a sealed steel tank and the wine is filtered and bottled under pressure, made Lambrusco clean, cheap and consistent, and made the export boom possible. It is still how most is made, and done well it preserves the grape's fruit and colour. The revival of the last twenty years has gone back the other way: producers like Cantina della Volta, Paltrinieri, Bellei and the old house of Cleto Chiarli, founded in 1860, make refermented-in-bottle and traditional-method Lambrusco, pale rosé Sorbara that could be mistaken for a sparkling wine from somewhere grander, and Grasparossa with real grip.

In the glass: Lambrusco di Sorbara secco.

 Colour: Pale ruby to bright pink, with a violet-tinged foam that fades quickly.

 Nose: Sour cherry, redcurrant, violets, orange peel, a yeasty note if refermented in bottle.

 Palate: Bone dry, sharp acidity, light body, faint tannin, clean and thirst-quenching.

 Alcohol: 11–12%.

 Serve: 10–12 °C, from a tumbler or a white-wine glass; drink within two years.

 At the table: Gnocco fritto and salumi, tortellini, zampone and cotechino, bollito misto, pizza.

Why it works at the table.

The cooking of Emilia is built on pork fat, butter, egg pasta and Parmigiano, and it needs a wine that can cut through all of it. Dry Lambrusco does that three ways at once: with acidity, with cold, and with carbon dioxide, which scrubs the palate between mouthfuls. It is what the region drinks with gnocco fritto, pillows of fried dough that are torn open and stuffed with prosciutto or salame; with tigelle, the small flatbreads of the Modena hills; with the great winter dish of zampone and cotechino, pork sausage stuffed into a trotter or a skin, boiled for hours and served with lentils; and with bollito misto, the trolley of boiled meats. Grasparossa handles roasts and aged cheese. Sorbara is the aperitivo, and it drinks well with fish, which no other red does.

Reading the label.

Look for secco, and for one of the three named DOCs rather than a bare "Lambrusco dell'Emilia", which is the catch-all. "Rifermentato in bottiglia" or "metodo ancestrale" means the cloudy traditional style. A Sorbara labelled rosato is not a novelty; the grape is naturally that pale. And do not age it. Lambrusco is a wine of the year, sold cheap because it is made to be, and at its best it is the most food-friendly sparkling wine in Italy for the price of a sandwich.

Before you move on.

Lambrusco is a family of Emilian grapes: pale, acidic Sorbara, dark, tannic Grasparossa and the widely planted Salamino.

Most is made dry and frizzante by tank fermentation; the revival is in bottle-refermented and traditional-method wines from Modena and Reggio.

Served cold, dry Lambrusco cuts pork fat, egg pasta and cheese, which is why it exists.
