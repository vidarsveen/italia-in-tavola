Lambrusco, the wine that was two wines.

In 1985 one brand of Lambrusco, Riunite, sold eleven and a half million cases in the United States. It was sweet, it was poured over ice, and it came with a jingle. Britain and the rest of northern Europe were drinking the same thing under other labels. For most of the world Lambrusco has meant that ever since: a soft drink with alcohol in it, and a name that makes a sommelier wince. In Modena that same year the bars were pouring something else. What came out of the fridge was dry, dark purple and foaming violet, and it arrived in a tumbler beside a piece of fried dough. It carried the same name, and it was a different wine.

Before there were steel tanks, every farm on the plain made Lambrusco the same way, and how it turned out depended on the weather. A farmer pressed his grapes in October. The cold of November stopped the fermentation before all the sugar had turned to alcohol, and in spring he bottled what he had, with some of that sugar still in it. In May the cellar warmed up, the yeast started again inside the bottle, and the gas it made could not get out. By June every farmhouse between Parma and Bologna had a cloudy, dry, sparkling red, and none of them had set out to make one. It was drunk within the year, because it would not keep.

The steel tank changed what the wine cost to make. In the tank method, called Charmat abroad and Martinotti in Italy, the second fermentation happens in a sealed steel tank instead of in the bottle. The wine is filtered and bottled under pressure, so it comes out clear, clean and the same every time, at a fraction of the cost of handling bottles one by one. Why did that matter so much? Because the fermentation could now be stopped whenever the sugar was right, so the wine could be made sweet, and because it could be made by the million. So the tank made Riunite possible, and Riunite made the joke. Most Lambrusco is still made in a tank, and when it is made dry the method serves it well, because it keeps the fruit and the colour. The problem was never the tank. It was the sugar.

Lambrusco is a family of grapes, all native to Emilia, and the two that matter most grow on opposite sides of the city. Drive north out of Modena onto the sandy plain between the rivers Secchia and Panaro and the vine is Lambrusco di Sorbara. It is the pale one. Its flowers often fail to set fruit, so growers plant a second Lambrusco, Salamino, among the rows to fill the bunches. The wine it gives is nearly a rosé, sharp with acidity and smelling of sour cherry and violets. Turn south instead, into the foothills around Castelvetro, and the vine is Grasparossa, which is dark, tannic and full and tastes of blackberry. Of the whole family it is the one that behaves most like a red wine. Salamino, named for bunches shaped like small salami, sits between the two in taste and is planted more than either of them.

A gnocco fritto is a pillow of dough dropped into hot fat, and it comes to the table puffed and blistered. You tear it open, the steam comes out, a slice of prosciutto or salame goes in and softens in the heat, and beside it stands a tumbler of Sorbara cold enough to mist the glass. Everything Emilia cooks is built like that, on pork fat, on butter, on egg pasta and on Parmigiano, and it needs a wine that can cut through. Dry Lambrusco does it three ways at once. The acidity cuts the fat, the cold makes the mouth want the next bite, and the bubbles scrub the palate between them. That is why the region drinks it with tigelle, the small flatbreads of the hills above Modena. At New Year it goes with zampone and cotechino, pork sausage stuffed into a trotter or a skin, boiled for hours and served over lentils. It is what goes with the trolley of boiled meats called bollito misto. Grasparossa handles the roast and the aged Parmigiano. Sorbara is the aperitivo, and it drinks well with fish, which few red wines manage.

The last twenty years have run the other way. Cleto Chiarli kept an osteria in Modena and made Lambrusco for his own tables, and in 1860 he turned that into a wine house, the oldest in Emilia-Romagna still working. The firm that carries his name now sells a Sorbara refermented in the bottle and named after him, the Fondatore. Smaller producers around it, Cantina della Volta and Paltrinieri among them, have gone back to the same old method. The second fermentation happens in the bottle, the wine stays on its sediment, and what you pour is faintly cloudy, as the farmhouse wine was. A few have gone the whole way to the Champagne method, with the yeast removed and the bottle topped up. After that the old joke is hard to keep up. A pale, bottle-fermented Sorbara can be mistaken for a sparkling wine from somewhere grander and more expensive. A Grasparossa made this way has real grip, and asks for a plate of meat.

None of it is meant to last. Even the best bottle-fermented Sorbara is made in the autumn to be finished by the next one. It is drunk cold, from a tumbler, at a table with something fried on it, by people who have never once thought of it as a wine that needed defending. They would be puzzled to learn that anyone had laughed.
