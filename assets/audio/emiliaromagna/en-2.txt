Sangiovese on the Romagna side.

East of Bologna the region changes name, dialect and grape. Romagna makes Sangiovese that is rounder than Tuscany's, on hills that may be where the grape was born. The sub-zones, the tannic white Albana, and Artusi's kitchen.

Emilia-Romagna is two regions joined by a hyphen. Emilia, from Piacenza to Bologna, is the plain of the Via Emilia, pork, butter and Lambrusco. Romagna, from Imola to the Adriatic, is hills, olive oil as well as butter, grilled meat, flatbread and a red grape that Tuscany considers its own. Sangiovese was first written down in Tuscany in 1590, but one of the stories of its name puts its birthplace on Monte Giove, above Santarcangelo di Romagna, where monks are said to have called their wine sanguis Jovis, the blood of Jupiter. Whichever side is right, Romagna grows more Sangiovese than any region except Tuscany, and for a long time it made it badly.

The hills behind the Via Emilia.

South of the Roman road the plain lifts into a band of foothills, twenty kilometres deep, running from Imola to Rimini. The soil changes as you go: yellow sandstone and shell-rich spungone at Bertinoro, clay and limestone at Predappio, gypsum ridges and chalk at Brisighella, and forest and cool air at Modigliana, where the vineyards reach 500 metres. The Adriatic is never far, and its breeze and the fog of the plain make Romagna warmer and more humid than Chianti. That gives a rounder, riper Sangiovese: black cherry rather than sour cherry, violets, softer tannin, less bite. For decades it was sold in bulk to bottlers or drunk from the demijohn. The turn came in the 1990s, when growers in Predappio and Modigliana started bottling single hillsides, and in 2011 the old Sangiovese di Romagna DOC of 1967 was rewritten as Romagna DOC with named sub-zones.

Key facts.

Romagna Sangiovese: at least 85% Sangiovese. Superiore needs 12.5% alcohol; Riserva needs 13% and two years of ageing after the harvest.

Twelve sub-zones were named in 2011, among them Bertinoro, Brisighella, Castrocaro, Cesena, Longiano, Meldola, Modigliana, Oriolo, Predappio and Serra; more have been added since. Bertinoro is Riserva only.

Albana di Romagna became Italy's first white DOCG in 1987, to some scorn at the time. It is a tannic, golden white, and its passito is the region's great sweet wine.

Pagadebit, "pays the debts", is the local name for Bombino Bianco, a white so reliable that it covered the grower's bills in bad years.

The names to know.

Predappio is the historic cru. The old vineyards of Predappio Alta, on the steep slope above the town Mussolini was born in, were planted a century ago, and estates like Nicolucci and Chiara Condello make wines here that are compared to good Chianti Classico. Modigliana, high and wooded, gives the finest, most perfumed Sangiovese in Romagna, elegant rather than powerful; Villa Papiano and Mutiliana are the reference. Bertinoro is structure and salt from its shell-bearing sandstone; Fattoria Paradiso has been bottling it since the 1950s. Brisighella, on chalk and gypsum, is the newest to attract attention and shares its hills with olive groves that make Romagna's best oil. None of these is expensive yet, which is the point.

In the glass: Romagna Sangiovese Superiore.

 Colour: Medium ruby, deeper than Chianti of the same age.

 Nose: Black cherry, plum, violet, dried herbs, a warm earthy note.

 Palate: Medium to full body, fresh but not sharp acidity, round tannin, a savoury finish.

 Alcohol: 13–14%.

 Serve: 16–18 °C; Superiore from two years, Riserva from four, the best keep ten.

 At the table: Grilled meat, cappelletti in broth, piadina with squacquerone and prosciutto, pecorino di fossa.

Albana and the whites.

Romagna's white is Albana, a grape with thick, tannic skins that gives a golden wine with apricot, almond and a grip in the mouth more like a red. Dry Albana is an acquired taste and a good one; the passito, from grapes dried on the vine or on mats, is honeyed, orange-scented and one of Italy's serious sweet wines. Trebbiano Romagnolo makes the everyday white by the carafe, and Pagadebit is the one you take to the beach. The coast from Ravenna to Rimini drinks all three with the Adriatic's fish.

Artusi's kitchen.

Romagna's table is simpler than Emilia's and more Tuscan in spirit. Piadina, the flatbread cooked on a hot plate and folded around prosciutto, rocket and the soft, sour squacquerone cheese, is eaten standing at kiosks along the coast. Cappelletti, little hats of egg pasta filled with cheese, come in capon broth at Christmas; passatelli, strands of breadcrumb, Parmigiano and egg pressed through a tool into broth, are the poor man's pasta. Meat is grilled over embers, castrato (mutton) at country feasts, and brodetto, the Adriatic fish stew, is argued over from port to port. The region's great cook, Pellegrino Artusi, was born at Forlimpopoli in 1820, and his 1891 book Science in the Kitchen and the Art of Eating Well was the first to treat Italian cooking as one cuisine. His house is now a cookery school, Casa Artusi, where the sfogline still teach the rolling pin.

Before you move on.

Romagna is the hilly, eastern half of the region; its Sangiovese is rounder and riper than Tuscany's, with black cherry and soft tannin.

Romagna DOC (2011) names sub-zones such as Predappio, Modigliana, Bertinoro and Brisighella; Riserva needs 13% and two years.

Albana was Italy's first white DOCG (1987), tannic and golden; piadina, cappelletti and Artusi's book define the Romagna table.
