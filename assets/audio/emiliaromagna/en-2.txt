Sangiovese on the Romagna side.

Pellegrino Artusi was seventy-one, a retired silk merchant living in Florence, when in 1891 he paid a printer to run off a thousand copies of a cookbook that no publisher had wanted. It took him four years to sell them. By the time he died in 1911 the book had sold more than two hundred thousand copies. It had done something no book had done before: it treated the cooking of the whole peninsula as one cuisine. Artusi was born at Forlimpopoli, on the Roman road east of Bologna, and the hills behind his town grow the grape the rest of Italy thinks of as Tuscan.

Emilia-Romagna is two regions joined by a hyphen. Emilia runs from Piacenza to Bologna along the old Roman road, and it is the plain: pork, butter and Lambrusco. Romagna runs from Imola to the Adriatic, and it is hills, with olive oil beside the butter, meat grilled over embers, a flatbread, and a red grape that Tuscany considers its own. The grape was first written down in Tuscany, in 1590. But one story of its name puts its birthplace on Monte Giove, above Santarcangelo di Romagna, where monks are said to have called their wine sanguis Jovis, the blood of Jupiter. Nobody can prove either claim. Romagna grows more Sangiovese than any region except Tuscany, and for decades it made the wine badly, sold it in bulk to bottlers, or drank it from the demijohn.

Drive south off the Via Emilia and the plain lifts into a band of foothills twenty kilometres deep, running from Imola to Rimini. The soil changes as you go, and so does the wine. At Bertinoro the vines stand on a yellow sandstone full of shells, called spungone, and the wine has structure and a salty edge. At Modigliana they climb to 500 metres into forest and cool air, and the wine is the finest and most perfumed of the hills, elegant rather than powerful. Predappio, on clay over limestone, is the historic one, with vineyards planted a century ago at Predappio Alta, on the steep slope above the town. The Adriatic is never far, and its breeze and the fog off the plain make all these hills warmer and more humid than Chianti. So the grape ripens further, and the wine comes out rounder: black cherry rather than sour cherry, violets, softer tannin, less bite. The turn came in the 1990s, when growers at Predappio and Modigliana began bottling single hillsides under their own names. In 2011 the appellation followed them: it became Romagna DOC, with the hillsides named as sub-zones.

Romagna's white is Albana, and it behaves like a red. The grape has thick, tannic skins, so the wine comes out golden, tasting of apricot and almond, with a grip in the mouth that a white is not supposed to have. In 1987 it became Italy's first white DOCG, to some scorn at the time. Dry Albana is an acquired taste and a good one. The passito, made from grapes dried on the vine or on mats, is honeyed and orange-scented, and it is one of the country's serious sweet wines. Trebbiano Romagnolo fills the carafe on an everyday table. A third white is called Pagadebit, pays the debts, because it was so reliable that it covered the grower's bills in a bad year, and it is the one you take to the beach. The coast from Ravenna to Rimini drinks all three with the Adriatic's fish.

The table Artusi came from is simpler than Emilia's and closer to Tuscany's. Piadina is a flatbread cooked on a hot plate and folded round prosciutto, rocket and squacquerone, a soft, sour cheese, and it is eaten standing at kiosks along the coast. At Christmas the capon broth carries cappelletti, little hats of egg pasta filled with cheese. Passatelli, strands of breadcrumb, Parmigiano and egg pressed through a tool straight into the broth, were the poor family's pasta. At country feasts the meat over the embers is castrato, which is mutton, and along the coast brodetto, the fish stew, is argued over from port to port. Artusi's house at Forlimpopoli is now a cookery school, Casa Artusi, and in it the sfogline still teach the rolling pin.

He called the book Science in the Kitchen and the Art of Eating Well, and he went on adding to it for the rest of his life. The first edition had four hundred and seventy-five recipes. The last one he saw had seven hundred and ninety, and the hills of Romagna had a book that the whole country cooked from.
