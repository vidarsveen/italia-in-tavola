Parmigiano, prosciutto and balsamic: three protected treasures.

In a store in the hills between Parma and Modena the shelves run to the roof. Every shelf carries wheels of cheese, row upon row of them, each stamped with the number of its dairy and the month it was made. At twelve months a man walks the rows with a small hammer. He taps each wheel all over and listens for a hollow or a crack. A wheel that passes is branded with the oval mark of the consortium, in fire. A wheel that fails has its rind scored off and goes out as plain table cheese. He is called the battitore, the beater, and the ham and the vinegar of the same plain are judged the same way, by hand, at the end of a long wait.

The three are Parmigiano Reggiano, Prosciutto di Parma and Aceto Balsamico Tradizionale. They are made within fifty kilometres of one another, and they began as the same problem. Milk, fresh pork and grape juice all spoil quickly, and a farm on this plain needed all three to last the winter. Each of the three is an answer: take the water out, add salt or time or both, and wait. All of them are now protected designations of origin, which fixes the place, the ingredients and the method by law, because all of them are copied all over the world.

The cheese has been made the same way since the monks of the abbeys on the Po plain worked it out in the twelfth century, and the rules are short. Raw milk, from cows fed on local grass and hay, in five provinces only. The evening milk is left overnight so the cream rises, and is skimmed. In the morning it is mixed with the whole milk of that day in a copper vat, warmed, and set with calf rennet and a little whey from the day before. The cheesemaker breaks the curd into grains the size of rice, cooks it, and lifts it out in a cloth as one mass, which he cuts in two. Two wheels a vat, about 45 kilos each before they dry. They sit in brine for three weeks and then on wooden shelves for at least a year, which is when the hammer comes. Most Parmigiano is sold at 24 months, when it has the crystals and the sweetness people expect. At 36 it is drier and sharper, and the dairies keep some wheels to 60 months and beyond. In 1348 Boccaccio imagined a mountain of the grated cheese with people rolling macaroni down it. A wheel keeps well enough, and is worth enough, that a local bank still takes wheels as security for a loan and stores them in its own vault.

The ham needs three things: a heavy pig, a mild climate and a dry wind. The pigs are raised across northern Italy on a diet that includes the whey left over from the cheese, and they reach about 160 kilos. Only the hind leg is used. In the hills south of Parma, above all around Langhirano, the legs are rubbed with sea salt and rested in cold rooms, then washed and hung in long halls with tall windows. The windows are opened when the wind blows down the Val Parma from the sea, and nothing else is added, no smoke, no spice, no nitrate. After several months the exposed meat is covered with a paste of lard and salt, the sugnatura, to slow the drying, and the ham finishes in cellars for at least fourteen months, often two years. Then the inspector comes with a needle of horse bone, pushes it into the ham at five points, and smells it each time. A ham that passes is branded with the five-pointed ducal crown.

Its rarer cousin is made on the foggy flats of the lower Po around Zibello and Busseto, Verdi's country. Culatello di Zibello is only the heart of the leg, boned, salted, sewn into a pig's bladder, tied in a net and hung in a damp cellar. The fog gets into the cellar, and the fog ages it. It is smaller, darker and sweeter than prosciutto, and it costs three times as much.

The vinegar starts as grape must, mostly Trebbiano and Lambrusco, boiled slowly in an open pan until it has lost a third or half of its volume and turned to a dark syrup. It goes into the top of a batteria, a row of five or more barrels of decreasing size, each of a different wood: oak, chestnut, cherry, mulberry, ash or juniper. The barrels live in the attic, because the summer heat up there drives the fermentation and the winter cold stops it. Every year the smallest barrel gives up a little vinegar and is topped up from the next one along, and that one from the next. So young must goes in at one end, and vinegar decades old comes out at the other. Nothing is ever added. After twelve years at the least, a sample goes to a panel of tasters. Only what they approve may be sold, in a sealed bottle of 100 millilitres, as Aceto Balsamico Tradizionale di Modena or di Reggio Emilia. It is thick, sweet, sour and complex, and it is used by the drop, on a piece of Parmigiano, on strawberries, on a risotto, and not on a salad.

Families in Modena keep a batteria of their own in the attic, and give one to a daughter when she marries. The bottle marked Aceto Balsamico di Modena IGP on the supermarket shelf is a different thing altogether: wine vinegar with some cooked must, sometimes coloured with caramel, aged for as little as two months. It is a good vinegar for a salad. It is not what is in the attic.

The hammer, the needle and the tasters all come at the end of the same kind of wait. Twelve months for the cheese, fourteen for the ham, twelve years for the vinegar, and a row of barrels in the attic for the daughter who will one day marry.
