Parmigiano, prosciutto and balsamic: three protected treasures.

Three products from within fifty kilometres of each other are copied all over the world and made properly only here. How a wheel of cheese, a leg of pork and a barrel of cooked grape must are made, aged and judged.

Between Parma and Modena, a strip of land fifty kilometres wide produces three foods that the rest of the world imitates and cannot match: Parmigiano Reggiano, Prosciutto di Parma and Aceto Balsamico Tradizionale. All three are protected designations of origin, which means the place, the ingredients and the method are fixed by law. All three are the result of doing one slow thing for a very long time, and all three were invented as ways of keeping something, milk, pork and grape juice, through the winter.

Parmigiano Reggiano: milk, fire and a hammer.

The cheese has been made the same way since the monks of the Po valley abbeys worked it out in the twelfth century; the first written record is from 1254, and Boccaccio in 1348 imagined a mountain of grated Parmigiano with people rolling macaroni down it. The rules: raw milk from cows fed on local grass and hay, in five provinces only, Parma, Reggio Emilia, Modena, and parts of Bologna and Mantova. The evening milk is left overnight to let the cream rise and is skimmed; it is mixed with the whole milk of the morning in a copper vat holding 1,100 litres, warmed, and set with calf rennet and a whey starter from the day before. The curd is broken into grains the size of rice, cooked, and lifted out in a cloth as a single mass that is cut in two: two wheels per vat, about 45 kilos each before they lose water. The wheels sit in brine for three weeks and then on wooden shelves for at least twelve months.

At twelve months every wheel is tested by a battitore, who taps it all over with a small hammer and listens for hollows and cracks. Wheels that pass are fire-branded with the oval mark of the consortium; those that fail have their rind scored off and are sold as plain table cheese. Most Parmigiano is sold at 24 months, when it has the crystals and the sweetness people expect; 36 months is drier and sharper; the dairies go to 60 months and beyond for enthusiasts. Around four million wheels are made a year, from nearly a fifth of Italy's milk. Wheels are accepted as collateral by a local bank, which keeps them in its own vault.

Key facts.

Parmigiano Reggiano: five provinces, raw milk, calf rennet and salt only, 1,100 litres of milk for two wheels, minimum 12 months; the consortium dates from 1934 and European protection from 1996. Grana Padano is the cousin made over a far wider area with fewer restrictions.

Prosciutto di Parma: hind legs of heavy pigs raised in northern Italy, sea salt only, minimum 14 months of curing, branded with the five-pointed ducal crown. About eight million hams a year, most from the hills around Langhirano.

Aceto Balsamico Tradizionale di Modena (and di Reggio Emilia): cooked grape must only, aged in a set of barrels for at least 12 years (affinato) or 25 years (extravecchio), sold in a sealed 100 ml bottle. Protected since 2000.

Aceto Balsamico di Modena IGP is a different product: wine vinegar and must, aged at least 60 days, made by the hundred million litres.

Prosciutto di Parma: salt and the wind.

The ham needs a heavy pig, a mild climate and a dry breeze. The pigs, raised across northern Italy on a diet that includes the whey left over from the cheese, reach about 160 kilos; only the hind leg is used. In the hills south of Parma, above all around Langhirano, the legs are rubbed with sea salt, rested in cold rooms, washed, and hung in long halls with tall windows that are opened when the wind blows down the Val Parma from the sea. Nothing else is added: no nitrates, no smoke, no spices. After several months the exposed meat is covered with a paste of lard and salt, the sugnatura, to slow the drying, and the ham finishes in cellars for a total of at least fourteen months, often two years. An inspector tests each one by pushing a needle of horse bone into five points and smelling it; the ham that passes is branded with the ducal crown.

Its rarer cousin is Culatello di Zibello, made not in the hills but on the foggy flats of the lower Po around Zibello and Busseto, Verdi's country. Only the heart of the leg is used, boned, salted, sewn into a pig's bladder and tied in a net, and hung in damp cellars where the fog does the ageing. It is smaller, darker and sweeter than prosciutto and costs three times as much.

Balsamic: a barrel in the attic.

Traditional balsamic vinegar starts as grape must, mostly Trebbiano and Lambrusco, boiled slowly in an open pan until it loses a third or half of its volume and turns to a dark syrup. It goes into the top of a batteria, a row of five or more barrels of decreasing size, each of a different wood, oak, chestnut, cherry, mulberry, ash or juniper, kept in the attic where summer heat drives the fermentation and winter cold stops it. Every year the smallest barrel gives up a little vinegar, and is topped up from the next largest, and so on down the line, so that the youngest must enters at one end and vinegar decades old leaves the other. Nothing is added, ever. After at least twelve years a sample goes to a panel of tasters, and only what they approve may be sold, in a sealed 100 ml bottle, Modena's designed by Giugiaro, as Aceto Balsamico Tradizionale di Modena or di Reggio Emilia. It is thick, sweet, sour and complex, and it is used by the drop, on Parmigiano, on strawberries, on a risotto, not in a salad.

The bottle labelled Aceto Balsamico di Modena IGP on the supermarket shelf is a different thing: wine vinegar mixed with some cooked must, sometimes coloured with caramel, aged for as little as two months. It is a good salad vinegar. It is not what the families of Modena keep in the attic and give a daughter when she marries.

Before you move on.

Parmigiano Reggiano is raw milk, rennet and salt from five provinces, two wheels per 1,100-litre vat, at least 12 months, tested by hammer and fire-branded.

Prosciutto di Parma is hind leg and sea salt cured for at least 14 months around Langhirano; Culatello di Zibello is the heart of the leg aged in Po valley fog.

Traditional balsamic is cooked must aged at least 12 or 25 years through a battery of barrels; the IGP supermarket version is a different, quick product.
