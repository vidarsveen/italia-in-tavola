Bologna la Grassa and the sfoglia.

Europe's oldest university, 62 kilometres of porticoes, two leaning towers, and a sheet of egg pasta rolled so thin you can see the table through it. Bologna the learned, the red and the fat.

Bologna has three nicknames and lives up to all of them. La Dotta, the learned, for the university founded in 1088, the oldest in the world still running. La Rossa, the red, for its terracotta roofs and its politics. La Grassa, the fat, for a kitchen that regards butter, pork and egg pasta as the food groups. It is a city of arcades, so that you can cross it in the rain without an umbrella, of two medieval towers that lean at alarming angles, and of a piece of egg dough rolled by hand into a sheet called the sfoglia, from which almost everything the city eats is cut.

Towers and porticoes.

In the twelfth century Bologna bristled with towers, perhaps a hundred of them, built by families as fortresses and as boasts. Two survive at the centre of the city. The Asinelli, 97 metres, was built around 1110 and can be climbed by 498 wooden steps; the Garisenda beside it, 48 metres, began to sink while it was being built, leans four degrees, and was mentioned by Dante, who compared it to a giant bending over him. In 2023 it was fenced off for fear it would fall. The porticoes are the city's other signature: 62 kilometres of covered walkways, begun in the Middle Ages to gain living space above the street and required by law from 1288, and listed by UNESCO in 2021. The longest runs 3.8 kilometres and 666 arches uphill from the city gate to the sanctuary of San Luca.

Piazza Maggiore, the main square, is closed on one side by San Petronio, begun in 1390 and never finished; the marble facing stops halfway up and the brick shows above it; the story the city tells is that the Pope would not allow a church larger than Saint Peter's. Around the corner is the Quadrilatero, the grid of narrow market streets that has sold food since Roman times, where the stalls of Via Pescherie Vecchie and the delicatessens of Via Drogheria set the city's table.

Key facts.

University of Bologna founded 1088; Asinelli tower about 1110, 97 m; Garisenda 48 m, leaning about four degrees; porticoes 62 km, UNESCO World Heritage 2021.

The sfoglia is flour and eggs only, one egg to 100 g of flour, rolled with a long pin called a mattarello.

The city's Chamber of Commerce holds the official recipes: the tagliatella (1972), whose cooked width is fixed at 8 mm, said to be the 12,270th part of the Asinelli tower; the tortellino (1974); and ragù alla bolognese (1982).

Mortadella Bologna IGP is finely ground pork studded with cubes of fat, cooked slowly in a huge casing; a city ordinance regulated it as early as 1661.

The sfoglia.

Everything begins with the sheet. Flour is heaped on a wooden board, eggs are broken into a well in the middle, and the dough is kneaded and then rolled out with a mattarello a metre long, turning and stretching, until it is thin enough to read a newspaper through and covers the whole board. The women who do this, the sfogline, are a Bolognese institution, and a good one can roll a sheet a metre across in twenty minutes. From the sheet come tagliatelle, cut into ribbons and served with ragù, the slow sauce of beef, pancetta, soffritto, a little tomato, wine and milk that the rest of the world calls bolognese and puts on spaghetti, which Bologna does not do. From it come lasagne verdi, the sheet coloured with spinach, layered with ragù and béchamel; and tortellini, thumbnail-sized parcels of pork loin, prosciutto, mortadella and Parmigiano, folded around a finger and served in capon broth, never with cream if you are in Bologna.

The tortellino has a legend, contested between Bologna and Modena, in which an innkeeper at Castelfranco Emilia, midway between the two, peeps through a keyhole at Venus asleep and, overcome, rushes to the kitchen to reproduce her navel in pasta. The two cities also disagree about the filling and the size, and the 1974 recipe deposited at the Bologna Chamber of Commerce was an attempt to settle it.

The rest of the fat city.

Mortadella is Bologna's sausage, pale pink, silky, scented with pepper and sometimes pistachio, sliced thin or cut into cubes for an aperitivo; it is the ancestor of the "bologna" of American lunch boxes and bears no resemblance to it. Cotoletta alla bolognese takes a breaded veal cutlet and covers it with prosciutto and Parmigiano before finishing it in broth. Friggione, onions and tomato cooked for hours, sits beside boiled meats. In winter there is certosino, a dense spiced cake with candied fruit, and at any time the crescentine and tigelle, fried and griddled breads, to wrap around salumi. The wine is Lambrusco, or the frothy white Pignoletto from the hills just south of the city.

The best place to understand the city is an osteria. The Osteria del Sole, off the Quadrilatero, has served wine since 1465 and no food at all: you bring your own from the market stalls outside, spread it on the long tables, and order a glass. It is the oldest expression of the Bolognese idea that eating is something you do together, slowly, and with the best ingredients in Italy a few metres away.

Before you move on.

Bologna is la Dotta (university 1088), la Rossa and la Grassa; its towers date from around 1110 and its 62 km of porticoes are World Heritage.

The sfoglia, eggs and flour rolled by hand, becomes tagliatelle al ragù, lasagne verdi and tortellini in brodo; the official recipes are held by the Chamber of Commerce.

Mortadella, cotoletta alla bolognese and the osteria table complete the picture; spaghetti bolognese does not exist here.
