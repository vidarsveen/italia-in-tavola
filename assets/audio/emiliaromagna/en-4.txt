Bologna la Grassa and the sfoglia.

Early in the morning, in a kitchen off the market streets of Bologna, a woman heaps flour on a wooden board. She breaks eggs into a well in the middle, one egg for every hundred grams of flour. She kneads, and then she rolls, with a pin a metre long called a mattarello. She turns the dough and stretches it until the sheet covers the whole board and the grain of the wood shows through it. A good sfoglina can roll a sheet a metre across in twenty minutes. The sheet is the sfoglia, and almost everything the city eats is cut from it. Bologna has three nicknames. It is la Dotta, the learned, for a university founded in 1088 and still running, and la Rossa, the red, for its roofs and its politics. The kitchen she works in is the third: la Grassa, the fat, where butter, pork and egg pasta count as the food groups.

Cut the sheet into ribbons eight millimetres wide and you have tagliatelle, which are dressed with ragù, the slow sauce of beef, pancetta, soffritto, a little tomato, wine and milk. The rest of the world calls that sauce bolognese and puts it on spaghetti, which Bologna does not do. Colour the sheet with spinach, layer it with ragù and béchamel, and it is lasagne verdi. Cut it into small squares, put a dab of pork loin, prosciutto, mortadella and Parmigiano on each, and fold it round a finger, and it is a tortellino. Tortellini are served in capon broth at Christmas, and never, in Bologna, with cream. The city takes these seriously enough to have deposited the recipes with its Chamber of Commerce: the tagliatella in 1972, the tortellino in 1974, the ragù in 1982. The width of a cooked tagliatella was fixed at eight millimetres, which is said to be the 12,270th part of the Asinelli tower.

The tortellino has a legend, and Bologna and Modena both claim it. An innkeeper at Castelfranco Emilia, which lies halfway between the two cities, peeped through a keyhole at Venus asleep in one of his rooms. He was so taken with her navel that he ran to the kitchen to make it in pasta. Nobody believes it and everybody tells it. What the two cities argue about in earnest is the filling and the size, and the recipe deposited in 1974 was an attempt to settle that.

In the twelfth century Bologna bristled with towers, perhaps a hundred of them, built by families as fortresses and as boasts. Two survive at the centre. The Garisenda began to sink while it was being built and leans four degrees. Dante compared it to a giant bending over him, and in 2023 it was fenced off for fear it would fall. The Asinelli beside it stands 97 metres and leans a little itself. The city's other signature is the portico. Porticoes began in the Middle Ages as a way of gaining living space above the street, and from 1288 the law required them, so the result is 62 kilometres of covered walkway. You can cross Bologna in the rain without an umbrella. The longest portico climbs 3.8 kilometres and 666 arches from the city gate to the sanctuary of San Luca on its hill.

In the main square, Piazza Maggiore, the church of San Petronio has stood unfinished since it was begun in 1390. The marble facing stops halfway up and the brick shows above it, and the story the city tells is that the Pope would not allow a church larger than Saint Peter's. Round the corner is the Quadrilatero, the grid of narrow streets that has sold food since Roman times. The stalls of Via Pescherie Vecchie and the delicatessens of Via Drogheria set the city's table.

Bologna's sausage is mortadella: pork ground smooth and pale pink, silky, studded with cubes of neck fat and whole peppercorns, sometimes pistachio, and cooked slowly in a huge casing. It is sliced thin, or cubed for an aperitivo. It is the ancestor of the bologna in American lunch boxes and bears no resemblance to it. Cotoletta alla bolognese takes a breaded veal cutlet, covers it with prosciutto and Parmigiano and finishes it in broth.

The Osteria del Sole, off the Quadrilatero, has served wine since 1465 and no food at all. You buy what you want from the stalls outside, mortadella, a piece of Parmigiano, bread, a tray of tortellini from the pasta shop, and spread it on the long tables. Then you order a glass of Pignoletto, the frothy white from the hills just south of the city, or a Lambrusco. Eating here is something you do together, slowly, with the stalls that sold you the food outside the door. The sheet that was rolled at dawn a few streets off is on the table by noon.
