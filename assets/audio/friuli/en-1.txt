Collio and the great Italian whites.

Mario Schiopetto grew up in his parents' inn in Udine, the Osteria ai Pompieri, across the road from the fire station, and as a boy he helped choose the wine it served. As a young man he drove lorries for an international haulier, which took him to Alsace and Burgundy and then to the Rhineland. At Bad Kreuznach he made friends with an expert in cellar equipment, who told him to give up cement and wood and use stainless steel. In 1965 Schiopetto made a Tocai entirely in steel. It came out clean and dry and tasted of its grape, at a time when many Italian whites were oxidised and tired. He went on to rent vineyards from the Church around the archbishop's palace at Capriva, in the hills of the Collio.

A white wine fermented warm, in a vat of wood or cement, loses much of the scent of its grape. In a steel tank the juice can be kept cool while it ferments, and the tank is closed and easy to clean, so the smell of the fresh fruit stays in the wine. Schiopetto's whites were bottled young, to be drunk within a few years. He named them after their grapes, and other estates in the hills took up the method.

Livio Felluga had come from Istria, across the Gulf of Trieste. He was born in 1914 at Isola d'Istria, into a family that had made Refosco and Malvasia for five generations, and he settled in the hills around Rosazzo. After the war, when Friuli still made mostly red wine, he bought hillside vineyards that had been abandoned and replanted them. In 1956, before any Friulian wine had an appellation, he put a map of the hills on his label, so that each bottle showed where it came from. He helped turn the region from red wine to white, and by 2022 white wine made up 86 per cent of Friuli's production, the highest share of any Italian region.

Most of Friuli is flat. The Tagliamento, the Meduna and the Cellina carried gravel down from the mountains and spread it across the plain of the Grave. The hill wines come from a band along the Slovenian border. The Collio is eight communes around Gorizia, between the Isonzo and the Judrio, with the border along the ridge to the north, and across it the same hills go on as the Goriška Brda. The Colli Orientali del Friuli, the eastern hills, run north from Manzano past Cividale. Both zones stand on ponca, the local word for flysch. It is a rock of thin layers of marl and sandstone, laid down on the floor of an ancient sea, and it crumbles into soil. The sandstone lets water drain away and the marl holds some of it, and the soil is poor, so the vine has to root deep. The Alps shelter the hills from the north, and the Adriatic, less than forty kilometres away, sends warmth up the valleys. In September the days are warm and the nights cool, which keeps both scent and acidity in the grapes.

Most Friulian wines are still named after one grape, but the most famous whites are blends. A Collio Bianco may be made from any of the permitted white grapes, and each estate has its own mix, often aged in old wood. The two blends that made Friulian whites famous carry other names. The Jermann estate made its first Vintage Tunina in 1975, and Livio Felluga made his first Terre Alte, from his vineyards at Rosazzo, in 1981. Rosazzo became a DOCG of its own in 2011, for a blend led by Friulano.

Above Trieste the hills give way to the Carso, a limestone plateau of bare rock and red soil, where the bora blows hard and many vines grow in hollows in the rock. Its white grape, Vitovska, stands up to the wind and the drought, and its red, Terrano, is a Refosco that suits the red earth. Malvasia Istriana grows there too, and some growers leave their white grapes on the skins while they ferment.

Friuli sold white wine abroad long before anyone used steel. Picolit is a white grape whose flowers mostly fail, so a bunch carries only a few berries, and it makes a sweet wine in small amounts. From about 1762 Count Fabio Asquini grew it on a large scale at Fagagna, north-west of Udine. He sold it in small bottles of glass blown on Murano, made to his own specification. It went as far as Russia and England, and to several of the Italian states. A letter of 1765 records that some of those bottles went to the Pope.
