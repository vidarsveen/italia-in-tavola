Collio and the great Italian whites.

In the far north-east corner of Italy, a crescent of hills on the Slovenian border makes the country's most serious dry white wines. The soil that does it, the zones, and how Friuli got there.

Friuli-Venezia Giulia is the corner of Italy that most Italians have never visited: a region wedged between the Alps, the Adriatic, Austria and Slovenia, with its own language, Friulian, and a history of belonging to Venice, then Austria, then Italy, then almost Yugoslavia. Its wine is the reason to know it. On the hills that curve around Gorizia, and again north of Udine, Friuli makes dry white wines of a weight, precision and ageing ability that the rest of Italy took decades to match, and from grapes, some of them, that grow nowhere else.

The hills and the ponca.

Most of Friuli is flat, the gravel plain of the Grave that the rivers Tagliamento and Meduna spread on their way to the sea, and it grows a great deal of sound, inexpensive Pinot Grigio and Merlot. The great wines come from two bands of hills against the mountains. The Collio is a half-moon of slopes around Gorizia, eight communes between the Isonzo and Judrio rivers, with the border with Slovenia running along the ridge and the same hills continuing on the other side as the Goriška Brda. The Colli Orientali del Friuli, the "eastern hills", run north from Cormons past Cividale towards the Alps. Both stand on ponca, the local name for flysch, layered marl and sandstone laid down under the sea and crumbling now into a soil that drains fast, holds little water and forces the vine to dig. The Alps to the north keep out the cold, the Adriatic forty kilometres away sends warmth up the valleys, and the difference between day and night in September gives the grapes aroma and acidity together.

Key facts.

Collio DOC (1968): the hills around Gorizia, about 1,500 hectares; the Colli Orientali del Friuli and Friuli Grave DOCs followed in 1970. Roughly 60% of the region's wine is white, the highest share in Italy after Alto Adige.

Soil: ponca, a flysch of marl and sandstone, on both hill zones; gravel on the Grave plain; limestone on the Carso above Trieste.

Two sweet DOCGs: Ramandolo (2001), Verduzzo from the hills above Nimis, and Colli Orientali del Friuli Picolit (2006), from a grape whose flowers mostly fail to set fruit.

The region's white revolution began in the 1960s with Mario Schiopetto and Livio Felluga, who introduced cold fermentation in steel and bottling by the estate.

How the whites got serious.

Until the 1960s Friuli's wine was sold in bulk and drunk locally, mostly as light red. Two men changed that. Mario Schiopetto, an innkeeper's son from Capriva, went to Germany, learned about cold fermentation and steel tanks, and came home to make white wines that were clean, dry and intensely varietal at a time when Italian whites were oxidised and tired. Livio Felluga, from a family of Istrian winemakers, bought land at Rosazzo in the Colli Orientali and put a map of the hills on his label. Between them they invented the modern Italian white: fermented cold, bottled young, drunk within a few years, and named by grape. The formula spread to the whole country, and Friuli kept the edge that comes from the hills and the ponca. Today the names to know are Schiopetto, Felluga, Jermann, Gravner, Radikon, Vie di Romans, Venica, Russiz Superiore, Volpe Pasini and Ronco del Gnemiz, most of them family estates of a few dozen hectares.

In the glass: Collio Bianco.

 Colour: Pale to medium straw, sometimes gold with age.

 Nose: White peach, pear, almond, chamomile, a stony mineral note; herbs if Sauvignon is in the blend.

 Palate: Dry, full for a white, medium-plus acidity, textured and long, often a faint bitterness at the end.

 Alcohol: 13–14%.

 Serve: 10–12 °C, in a large glass; from two years, and the best keep ten.

 At the table: Prosciutto di San Daniele, frico, risotto, Adriatic fish, roast chicken; the region's whites go with meat.

Blends and the Carso.

Friuli names most of its wines by grape, but its most ambitious whites are blends. Collio Bianco may be made from any of the permitted white grapes, and each estate has its own formula, Friulano with Ribolla and Malvasia, or Chardonnay, Sauvignon and Pinot Bianco, aged in old wood; Jermann's Vintage Tunina and Felluga's Terre Alte are the two that made the category famous. One more hill zone belongs in the picture: the Carso, the limestone plateau above Trieste, thin red soil over white rock, where the wind blows the bora and the vines grow in walled pockets. It has its own grapes, the white Vitovska and the red Terrano, and its own tradition of skin-contact whites, which the next reading is about.

Before you move on.

Friuli's best wines are dry whites from two hill zones on the Slovenian border, the Collio and the Colli Orientali, on crumbling flysch called ponca.

Schiopetto and Felluga invented the modern Italian white here in the 1960s: cold fermentation, steel, bottled young, named by grape.

Collio Bianco blends and the estates' single-vineyard whites age for a decade; Ramandolo and Picolit are the sweet DOCGs.
