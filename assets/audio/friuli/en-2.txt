Friulano, Ribolla and orange wine.

Hail destroyed ninety-five per cent of Josko Gravner's crop at Oslavia, above Gorizia, in 1996. He divided the Ribolla Gialla that survived between four small barrels. Two fermented with their skins and two without, and in each pair one had yeast added and one did not. By the spring of 1997 one barrel stood out, the Ribolla fermented on its skins with only its own yeast.

Gravner had doubted the clean wines of steel since 1987, when he tasted about a thousand wines in California and decided that so much work in the cellar was a mistake. His neighbour Stanko Radikon had already gone back to the skins in 1995, giving his Ribolla seven days on them, as his grandfather had done. At the 1997 harvest Gravner left his Ribolla on its skins in open wooden vats, as if it were a red. Then he tried a clay amphora of 250 litres from Spain, and it leaked, because it had no lining of beeswax. In 2000 he went to Georgia, where people were making wine in clay jars about eight thousand years ago, and ordered eleven of the jars they call qvevri. Nine arrived broken. From the 2001 vintage his wines fermented in Georgian qvevri buried to the rim in the floor of his cellar.

The skins give a white wine colour and tannin. Wines made this way come out amber, with a grip like a light red, and they taste of dried apricot, tea, nuts and honey while staying dry. In 2004 David Harvey, a British wine merchant working in a cellar on Etna, called them orange wine. "I didn't set out to invent a word," he said later. "I just used it naturally and it stuck."

Ribolla Gialla is a golden grape with thick skins, and it has grown in these hills for a long time; a dispute of 1296 already names it, as Rabola. Made in steel, it gives a lean white with lemon and green apple. Its thick skins give a long maceration plenty to draw on. In 2010 the growers of Oslavia founded an association of the producers of Ribolla di Oslavia, and Gravner joined it in 2020.

The white most often poured in a Friulian osteria, with a plate of ham, had to change its name. Friulano is the grape known elsewhere as Sauvignonasse, which came from the Gironde, and it has nothing to do with the grapes of Hungary's Tokaji. It reached Friuli in the nineteenth century, and by the early twentieth its name was spelled Tocai. The name that the law protected was Hungary's. Under an agreement between the European Community and Hungary in 1993, no Italian wine could be labelled Tocai after March 2007. The director of the cooperative at Cormons said they would go on calling Tocai Tocai until there was a final ruling. The region passed a law to keep the name for wine sold in Italy, and in 2008 the Constitutional Court struck it down. The older generation still says Tocai.

Friulano is dry and medium-bodied, with pear and chamomile, more texture than scent and a bitter-almond finish. It is the usual wine of the tajut, the glass of wine taken in an osteria before lunch or dinner. In the sixteenth century the council of Udine was already tasting the wine its osterie sold, in the square then called Piazza del Vino, now Piazza Libertà.

Refosco dal Peduncolo Rosso, named for the red stalks of its bunches, makes a dark, tart, plummy red with a bitter-cherry finish. Other native reds came close to disappearing. Pignolo, a very tannic red, was down to its last two vines at the Abbey of Rosazzo, and in 1979 they were saved. In 1970 Paolo and Dina Rapuzzi wanted to replant Schioppettino at Cialla, near Prepotto, and the regional office told them it was illegal, because the variety was not on the official register. They planted it anyway, grafting cuttings from the last surviving vines. In 1973 the grappa distillers Benito and Giannola Nonino distilled the pomace of Picolit on its own, and the family counts it as the first single-variety grappa. In 1975 they founded a prize, the Risit d'Aur, for growers who saved Friuli's dying vines. One of the first, in 1976, went to the Rapuzzis for Schioppettino, "of which absurd laws had decreed the extinction".
