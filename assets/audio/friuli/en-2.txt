Friulano, Ribolla and orange wine.

A grape that lost its name in a fight with Hungary, a golden grape that has been here since the 1200s, and a village where the whites are fermented on their skins like reds. The grapes of Friuli, and the wine that came back from the Caucasus.

Friuli's grapes are its own. Some came with the Romans, some with the Venetians, some over the ridge from Slovenia, and several exist only here, in a few hundred hectares of hills. The white that Friulians drink most, and pour in every osteria with a plate of ham, spent the twentieth century called Tocai and was forced to change its name; the one that grows highest on the ponca, Ribolla Gialla, is the base of a style of wine that was ancient, then forgotten, then, from one village on the Gorizia border, reinvented for the whole world.

Friulano, formerly Tocai.

Friulano is the region's everyday white and its most planted native grape. It is genetically the same as Sauvignonasse, an old Bordeaux variety, and has nothing to do with Hungary's Tokaji, but it was called Tocai Friulano for centuries, and when Hungary joined the European Union it won the argument over the name. From 2007 the wine has been labelled Friulano, and the older generation still says Tocai. It makes a dry, medium-bodied white with pear, chamomile and a distinctive bitter-almond finish, low in aroma and high in texture, and it is the wine for the tajut, the small glass drunk standing at the bar at eleven in the morning. Its companions are Malvasia Istriana, the salty, aromatic white of the coast and the Carso; Verduzzo, tannic and golden, the grape of Ramandolo; and Picolit, whose flowers drop before they set, so that a vine gives a handful of grapes and a sweet wine that Habsburg courts paid for in gold.

Key facts.

Friulano: dry, textured, bitter-almond finish; called Tocai until the EU ruling took effect in 2007. Ribolla Gialla: recorded in the Collio in the 13th century, high acid, citrus and green apple, the base of the orange wines.

Native reds: Refosco dal Peduncolo Rosso (dark, plummy, tart), Schioppettino from Prepotto (peppery, Syrah-like, rescued from a few vines in the 1970s), Pignolo (tannic, rare), Terrano on the Carso.

Orange wine: white grapes fermented on their skins for days or months, giving amber colour, tannin and a dry, nutty, tea-like taste. Josko Gravner at Oslavia adopted buried Georgian clay amphorae in 2001; his neighbour Stanko Radikon made skin-contact Ribolla from 1995.

The Collio's Slovenian side, the Goriška Brda, and the Carso across the border continue the same styles; the frontier runs through vineyards.

Ribolla and the village of Oslavia.

Ribolla Gialla is the Collio's oldest grape, a golden, thick-skinned white that gives a lean, high-acid wine with lemon and green apple when it is made the modern way. It is also the grape of Oslavia, a village on the ridge above Gorizia that was destroyed in the First World War and rebuilt, where a handful of growers made the region's most radical wines. In the 1990s Stanko Radikon and Josko Gravner, dissatisfied with the clean steel-tank whites they had helped to perfect, went back to what their grandfathers had done: crushing the white grapes and leaving the juice on the skins, as if making a red, for weeks or months. Gravner went further, to Georgia, where wine has been made in buried clay jars for eight thousand years, and from 2001 he fermented everything in qvevri dug into the floor of his cellar. The wines came out amber, tannic, dry, tasting of dried apricot, tea, nuts and honey without sweetness, and they divided the wine world for a decade.

The style has a name now, orange wine, and is made from Georgia to California, but its modern home is this ridge. Radikon, Gravner, Dario Prinčič, La Castellada and Primosic in Oslavia, Vodopivec and Zidarich on the Carso, and Movia on the Slovenian side make the reference wines. They are not for everyone, and they are not meant to be drunk cold from a flute. Poured at cellar temperature with a plate of San Daniele, or with the cheese-and-potato crust of frico, they make a kind of sense that no other white does.

In the glass: Ribolla Gialla, skin-contact.

 Colour: Amber to deep orange, often hazy.

 Nose: Dried apricot, orange peel, black tea, walnut, honey, a cidery note.

 Palate: Dry, with tannin like a light red, firm acidity, savoury and long; no fruit sweetness.

 Alcohol: 12.5–14%.

 Serve: 14–16 °C, in a red-wine glass, opened an hour ahead; ages for twenty years.

 At the table: Prosciutto, frico, roast pork, mushrooms, aged Montasio, spiced food; anything that would defeat a normal white.

The reds.

Friuli's reds are a minority and a good one. Refosco dal Peduncolo Rosso, named for its red stalks, is the everyday red, dark, tart and plummy, with a bitter cherry finish. Schioppettino, from the village of Prepotto on the border, was down to a few vines in the 1970s and was saved by one family, the Rapuzzi; it is peppery and fragrant, the closest thing Italy has to a cool-climate Syrah. Pignolo, so tannic that it needs a decade, is rarer still. And the Merlot of the Grave and the Isonzo, planted since the Habsburgs, makes soft, generous wine that the region drinks with its winter stews and does not export.
