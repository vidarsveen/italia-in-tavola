San Daniele, frico and the Carnic kitchen.

A ham cured in the wind between the Alps and the sea, a cheese fried into a crust, sweet-and-savoury dumplings from the mountains, and a soup that tastes of Austria. What Friuli eats, from the plain to Carnia.

Friuli's cooking is the food of a poor, cold, border country that was ruled by Venice and Austria and fed its soldiers on polenta. It is heavier than the rest of Italy's, built on pork, cheese, beans, cabbage and maize, with butter as often as oil, and it changes as you climb: the plain eats prosciutto and frico, the mountains of Carnia eat dumplings filled with things that would seem odd anywhere else, and Trieste eats like Vienna with a sea view. The one thing the whole region agrees on is a glass of white wine at any hour, the tajut, and the bar where you drink it.

Prosciutto di San Daniele.

San Daniele del Friuli is a small town on a hill above the Tagliamento, where the air that comes down from the Alps meets the air that comes up from the Adriatic, and the two keep the town dry and breezy all year. That is why the ham is here. The legs come from heavy pigs raised in northern Italy; they are salted with sea salt only, no spices, no nitrates, pressed flat under weights into the guitar shape that marks San Daniele, and hung in the wind for at least thirteen months, usually longer. The trotter is left on, by tradition and because it helps the ham dry evenly. The result is sweeter and more delicate than Parma, with a faint sweetness at the end, and it is sliced by hand thicker than a machine would cut it. Some thirty producers in the town make around 2.7 million hams a year, protected since 1996, and the town's festival every June is the region's biggest party.

Key facts.

Prosciutto di San Daniele DOP (1996): pork leg and sea salt, cured at least 13 months in San Daniele del Friuli, trotter left on. About 2.7 million hams a year.

Montasio DOP (1996): a cow's-milk cheese first made by the monks of Moggio in the 13th century; sold fresco from two months, mezzano from five, stagionato from ten, stravecchio from eighteen.

Frico: Montasio fried, either with potato and onion into a soft cake, or alone into a thin crisp wafer.

Brovada DOP: turnips fermented for weeks in grape pomace, then grated and stewed; served with musetto, a coarse pork sausage. Cjarsons: the sweet-and-savoury filled pasta of Carnia.

Frico and Montasio.

Montasio is the cheese of the whole region, made since the thirteenth century when the monks of the abbey of Moggio in Carnia organised the mountain dairies: a firm, pale cow's-milk cheese, mild when young, sharp and crystalline at two years. Its purpose in life is frico. In the soft version, grated Montasio is fried slowly with sliced potato and onion in a pan until it forms a golden cake, crisp outside and melting inside, and served with polenta; it was the dish that shepherds and woodcutters carried into the mountains, and it is now on every menu in Udine. In the crisp version, a handful of grated cheese is fried alone into a lace wafer, and eaten as a snack with the aperitivo. Every trattoria claims its own proportion of cheese to potato, and the argument is as fierce as any about ragù in Bologna.

Carnia and the cjarsons.

Carnia is the mountainous north-west of the region, a knot of valleys under the Carnic Alps, poor, Alpine and Friulian-speaking, where the men left every spring for centuries to work as pedlars in Austria and Germany and came back in autumn with spices and raisins. That history is in the cjarsons, the valley's ravioli: a potato or bread dough filled with a mixture that changes from village to village and can include spinach, ricotta, raisins, cocoa, cinnamon, apricot jam, mint and lemon, boiled and dressed with melted butter and smoked ricotta. They are sweet and savoury at once and unlike any pasta south of the mountains. Carnia also makes polenta from its own maize, toc' in braide, a soft polenta with cheese and butter, and the smoked ricotta, ricotta affumicata, that is grated over everything.

Turnips, sausage and the sweet things.

The winter table is Austrian in spirit. Brovada is the region's sauerkraut: turnips buried in grape pomace after the harvest, left to ferment for a month or more, then grated and stewed, sour and winey, and served with musetto, a coarse boiled sausage made with the meat of the pig's snout, on Christmas Eve and New Year. Jota, the soup of Trieste and the Karst, is beans, sauerkraut, potato and smoked pork, thick enough to stand a spoon in. There is goulash in the trattorie of Gorizia and Trieste, polenta at every meal, and the game and mushrooms of the hills in autumn. Sweet things come from the Slovene border: the gubana of the Natisone valleys, a spiral of yeast dough rolled around walnuts, raisins, pine nuts and grappa, and the strucolo, Trieste's strudel. The digestivo is grappa, which the region distils in quantity, and the coffee, of which more in the next reading, is the best in Italy.

Before you move on.

Prosciutto di San Daniele is pork and sea salt cured at least 13 months in the breeze between the Alps and the Adriatic, trotter on, pressed flat.

Montasio, the monks' cheese of Carnia, is fried with potato into frico, the region's signature dish.

Cjarsons, brovada with musetto, jota and gubana show the Alpine, Austrian and Slovene sides of the Friulian table.
