San Daniele, frico and the Carnic kitchen.

Every autumn men walked out of the mountain valleys of Carnia, in the north-west of Friuli, with a wooden chest of drawers strapped to their backs. The chest was called a crassigne, and its drawers held spices and medicines. The men were cramârs, from the German Krämer, a shopkeeper. At the height of the trade, from the seventeenth century to the early nineteenth, they carried their goods over the Alps as far as Hungary and Transylvania, and they came home in late spring. The museum at Tolmezzo has a portrait of one of them, Pietro Gerino of Frassenetto, with his box of spices.

In Carnia people say that the spices and raisins left over in the crassigne went into cjarsons, the valley's filled pasta. No document shows it; the museum's records have the cramârs bringing home church silver from Augsburg. The spices are in the filling all the same. There are more than fifty versions. The dough is made of flour or of potato, and the filling can mix raisins, cocoa and cinnamon with spinach and ricotta, and sometimes jam or mint. At Cleulis, near Paluzza, it has dried figs, cloves and lemon. The cjarsons are boiled and dressed with melted butter and grated smoked ricotta, so every mouthful is sweet and salty at once. They were a dish for weddings and feast days until the chef Gianni Cosetti brought them into restaurants in the 1970s.

The cheese of these valleys and of the plain below is Montasio, a firm cow's-milk cheese. Its consortium says the Benedictine monks of the abbey of Moggio, in Carnia, refined it from the thirteenth century, but no document from that time survives. The first written mention is a price list from Udine of about 1773, kept in the Guarneriana library at San Daniele, and on it Montasio costs well above the other cheeses. At two months it is mild, and after a year and a half it is sharp and crystalline.

Friuli fries it. For frico, grated Montasio goes into a pan with sliced potato and onion and cooks slowly into a cake, crisp outside and soft inside, which is eaten with polenta. Fried alone, a handful of the cheese becomes a thin, crisp wafer to eat with the aperitivo. Cheese fried in a pan is an old dish. In the 1460s Maestro Martino, cook to the patriarch of Aquileia, wrote a recipe for slices of cheese cooked in butter in small pans, turned and served with sugar and cinnamon. He was cooking in Rome, though, and nobody can show that frico descends from it.

San Daniele del Friuli stands on a hill of glacial rubble above the Tagliamento. Its ham makers say that cold air from the Alps meets warmer air from the Adriatic there, and keeps the town dry and breezy. The legs come from heavy pigs raised in ten regions of northern and central Italy. Each leg is covered in sea salt, and nothing else is added, no spice and no nitrite. It is then pressed, which gives San Daniele its flat shape, like a guitar. The trotter stays on, because it lets moisture drain out as the ham ages. The hams hang in rooms whose many windows are opened to let that mountain and sea air through. An inspector pushes a sliver of horse bone into the hams at set points and smells the bone. After at least four hundred days from the salting, the mark with the letters SD is burned into the skin.

After the vintage, white turnips with a violet collar are laid in vats between layers of marc, the pressed skins of red grapes, and covered with water. A little sea salt, red wine or red wine vinegar may go in too. The turnips ferment for at least twenty-five days, and they come out cream, pink or red, depending on the marc. This is brovada. It is peeled, cut into long strips and stewed slowly with bay and pork fat, and it tastes sour and of wine. On New Year's Eve and New Year's Day it is eaten with musetto, a sausage of the pig's snout and rind, cooked like a cotechino.

In Trieste and on the Karst the winter soup is jota. It is beans, sauerkraut and potato with caraway and bay, usually cooked with smoked pork, and thick enough to stand a spoon in. Goulash, which arrived under the Habsburgs, is eaten in the trattorie of Gorizia and Trieste, and Maria Stelvio gave a recipe for it in her book of Trieste cooking in 1927.

The cake of the Natisone valleys, near the Slovene border above Cividale, is gubana, a spiral of yeast dough rolled round walnuts, raisins, pine nuts and grappa. A story puts it on the table of Pope Gregory XII at a banquet in Cividale in 1409, and the first document to name the cake dates from 1576. Cut into slices, it shows the spiral of dough and filling, turn after turn.
