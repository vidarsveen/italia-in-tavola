Trieste: coffee, Habsburgs and the bora.

A city that was Austria's port for two hundred years and has never quite decided to be Italian: its coffee, its cafés, the wind that knocks people over, and the older Friuli behind it, Roman Aquileia, Lombard Cividale and Venetian Udine.

Trieste is the regional capital and it does not belong to the region. It sits at the far end, on a strip of coast under the limestone plateau of the Karst, a city of grand nineteenth-century streets, a Greek church, a Serbian church, a synagogue, and a piazza that opens straight onto the sea. For two centuries it was the only port of the Habsburg empire, the harbour through which Vienna traded with the world, and it grew rich, cosmopolitan and coffee-scented. It became Italian in 1918, was occupied and fought over after 1945, and only rejoined Italy for good in 1954. Its writers, Italo Svevo, Umberto Saba and their guest James Joyce, wrote about a city always looking over its shoulder. Its cafés still do.

The Habsburg port.

In 1719 the emperor Charles VI declared Trieste a free port, exempt from customs duties, and merchants came from every corner of the empire and beyond: Greeks, Serbs, Jews, Germans, Armenians, Slovenes and Italians, who built the neoclassical quarter around the Grand Canal and the insurance companies, Lloyd and Generali, that still bear the city's name. Piazza Unità d'Italia, laid out as the empire's window on the sea, is among the largest squares in Europe to open directly onto the water. Up the coast, on a promontory over the bay, the archduke Maximilian built the white castle of Miramare between 1856 and 1860, and left it to be emperor of Mexico, where he was shot in 1867; his widow never recovered, and the castle stands as he left it, with his gardens. The Karst plateau behind the city is a wilderness of white limestone, sinkholes and the Grotta Gigante, a single cave chamber a hundred metres high.

Key facts.

Trieste was Habsburg from 1382 to 1918, a free port from 1719, Italian from 1918, a Free Territory under Allied administration 1947–54, and Italian again from 1954. Slovenia is ten kilometres away.

The bora is a dry, cold north-east wind that falls off the Karst; gusts above 150 km/h have been recorded, and ropes are strung along streets for pedestrians.

Coffee: Trieste has been the empire's coffee port since the 1700s; Illy was founded here in 1933; Caffè Tommaseo (1830), Caffè degli Specchi (1839) and Caffè San Marco (1914) are the historic cafés.

Elsewhere in the region: Aquileia, a Roman city with a fourth-century mosaic floor, UNESCO 1998; Cividale, the first Lombard capital in Italy, with the eighth-century Tempietto, UNESCO 2011; Udine, the Venetian capital of Friuli.

Coffee and the bora.

Coffee came to Trieste in Habsburg ships and never left. The city roasts, trades and drinks more of it per head than any in Italy, and has its own vocabulary: a nero is an espresso, a capo is a macchiato, a capo in b is the same in a glass, and ordering a cappuccino gets you something small. Illy, founded by a Hungarian officer who stayed after the war, invented the pressurised coffee machine here in 1935. The cafés are the city's living rooms, Viennese in style and habit: Caffè San Marco, opened in 1914 with its Secession decor and a bookshop inside, where Saba wrote and Claudio Magris still does; Caffè Tommaseo on the waterfront, the oldest, from 1830; Caffè degli Specchi on the piazza. And then the bora, the wind that defines the city as much as the coffee: a cold, dry blast off the Karst that arrives in winter without warning, blows for days at a hundred kilometres an hour and more, throws spray over the Molo Audace and cars across the road, and has ropes and chains fixed along the streets for people to hold. Triestini claim it clears the head.

The older Friuli.

Trieste is the region's newest city; the rest is much older. Aquileia, on the plain near the lagoon, was one of the great cities of the Roman empire, a port and military base of a hundred thousand people, destroyed by Attila in 452; its basilica stands on a floor of fourth-century mosaic, 760 square metres of it, the largest of its age in the west, with fishing scenes, animals and the story of Jonah. Cividale del Friuli, on the gorge of the Natisone, was the first capital of the Lombards when they invaded Italy in 568, and keeps the Tempietto Longobardo, a small eighth-century chapel with stucco saints of a delicacy nothing else from that century matches. Udine, the region's real centre, is a Venetian city: a castle on a mound, a loggia copied from the Doge's palace, Tiepolo's first great frescoes, and the osterie where the tajut was invented. Between them, on the hills, Gorizia was one city until 1947 and is two, Gorizia and Nova Gorica, with the border running across the station square; in 2025 the pair were Europe's capital of culture together.

Before you move on.

Trieste was the Habsburg empire's port from 1719 to 1918, Italian from 1918, disputed until 1954; Miramare was Maximilian's castle.

Coffee is the city's trade and culture, with Illy and the Viennese cafés; the bora is the wind off the Karst that blows at over 100 km/h.

Aquileia's Roman mosaics, Cividale's Lombard Tempietto and Venetian Udine are the older Friuli behind the port.
