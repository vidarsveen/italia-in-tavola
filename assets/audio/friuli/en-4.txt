Trieste: coffee, Habsburgs and the bora.

In a Trieste bar an espresso is a nero. A capo is an espresso with a little milk in a cup; ask for it in b, for bicchiere, and it comes in a small glass. The coffee with plenty of milk that the rest of Italy calls a cappuccino is a caffelatte here. The beans in those cups came in through the port. About a fifth of the green coffee that Italy imports is unloaded at Trieste, and much of it is graded and roasted in the city.

On 18 March 1719 the emperor Charles VI made Trieste a free port. Goods could be landed there without customs duty, so merchants came to trade them, Greeks and Serbs, Jews and Armenians among them. The first coffee house on record opened in 1768, and in 1779 Maria Theresa set duties that taxed spirits heavily and coffee lightly. By the end of the century Trieste was Austria's main port.

The cafés that survive are younger. Caffè Tommaseo, on the waterfront, opened in 1825 or 1830 and claims to be the oldest, and Caffè degli Specchi opened on the great square in 1839. Caffè San Marco opened on 3 January 1914 with Viennese Secession decoration. On 23 May 1915, the day Italy declared war on Austria-Hungary, Austrian soldiers wrecked it. Italo Svevo, Umberto Saba and James Joyce, who taught Svevo English, were regulars there, and it is now a café and bookshop.

Francesco Illy, born at Timișoara, served in the Austro-Hungarian army and settled in Trieste after the war, and in 1933 he founded a coffee company there with Roberto Hausbrandt. Roasted coffee goes stale in air, so in 1934 he packed it in tins filled under pressure with an inert gas. In 1935 he built the Illetta, a machine that measured out the water itself and pushed it through the coffee with compressed air instead of steam.

Up the coast from the city, the archduke Maximilian built the white castle of Miramare between 1856 and 1860. On 14 April 1864 he sailed from there to become emperor of Mexico, and in 1867 he was shot at Querétaro. Much of the furniture inside is still his.

Behind the city rises the Karst, a limestone plateau, and its farmers have their own way of selling wine. In 1784 the emperor Joseph II allowed them to sell their own wine without tax for eight days. The farms that still do it are called osmize, from the Slovene word for eight. A branch of ivy and a red wooden arrow by the lane show that one is open. Inside, Terrano, Vitovska or Malvasia comes by the jug, with hard-boiled eggs, the farm's own cured pork and cheese, and everything served must come from the farm. How many days a farm may open now depends on how much wine it makes.

Inland, on the plain and in the hills, Friuli's monuments are far older. Aquileia, near the lagoon, was a Roman port and military base. Soon after 313 Bishop Theodore laid a mosaic floor in its church, with fishermen among the fish and the story of Jonah. Cividale del Friuli, on the gorge of the Natisone, became the seat of the first Lombard duchy in Italy after the Lombards invaded in 568. Its eighth-century chapel, the Tempietto Longobardo, keeps six female saints in stucco.

In winter the bora falls on Trieste from the Karst, a cold, dry wind from the north-east that can arrive without warning. In 1954 the city's anemometer measured a gust of 171 kilometres an hour, just before the wind broke it. Until about the 1970s ropes were strung along the most exposed streets for people to hold on to, and some rings and chains are still in the walls. The spray blows over the Molo Audace, the pier named after the Italian destroyer Audace, which tied up there on 3 November 1918.

The Audace brought Italian rule, which a treaty confirmed in 1920. In 1897, under the empire, Pepi Klajnsic had opened a buffet by the stock exchange. It still keeps a pot where Kaiserfleisch, cotechino and tongue simmer together, and the meat is cut to order onto bread with grated horseradish and mustard. The house says the only things that ever changed were the coins in the till, from crowns to lire in 1918 and from lire to euros in 2002.
