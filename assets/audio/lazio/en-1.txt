The Castelli Romani and Frascati.

Where Rome has always gone to drink: an extinct volcano, papal villas, tavern wine by the jug, and a white that is finally taken seriously.

Take the old Via Tuscolana south-east out of Rome and within half an hour the flat Campagna tilts upward into a ring of wooded hills. These are the Colli Albani, the Alban Hills, and the towns strung around them are the Castelli Romani: Frascati, Grottaferrata, Marino, Castel Gandolfo, Albano, Ariccia, Genzano, Nemi, Rocca di Papa, Velletri and a handful more. For two thousand years this is where Romans have gone to breathe cooler air and to drink.

A volcano under the vines.

The hills are the rim of a volcanic complex that last erupted tens of thousands of years ago. Two of its craters are now lakes, Albano and Nemi, deep, round and startlingly blue. The eruptions left behind soils of tuff and pozzolana, dark, porous, and rich in potassium, which drain well and give the local whites their slightly savoury, mineral edge. The same rock built Rome: the pozzolana that made Roman concrete set under water was quarried here.

Vines have grown on these slopes since antiquity. In the Renaissance the cardinals of the papal court built their summer palaces above Frascati, the so-called Ville Tuscolane, and planted the surrounding land. Villa Aldobrandini, finished in 1602 for Cardinal Pietro Aldobrandini, still looks down over the town square with its terraced gardens and water theatre.

The wine of the fraschette.

For most of its history Frascati was not a wine for connoisseurs. It was the wine of the fraschetta, the rustic tavern that signalled it had new wine to sell by hanging a leafy branch, a frasca, over the door. You sat on a bench at a paper-covered table, ordered wine by the fojetta (a half-litre jug) and brought or bought your own food: bread, olives, pecorino, a slab of porchetta from Ariccia, the whole herb-stuffed roast pig that is now protected as an IGP product. Frascati was cheap, pale, a little rustic, and consumed young and in quantity. Roman dialect poets wrote about it; nobody laid it down.

Key facts.

Frascati DOC was created in 1966, one of Italy's earliest denominations.

Frascati Superiore DOCG and Cannellino di Frascati DOCG (sweet) were promoted to the top tier in 2011.

Grapes: at least 70% Malvasia (Malvasia bianca di Candia and/or Malvasia del Lazio), with Trebbiano toscano, Bellone, Bombino and Greco allowed.

Neighbouring DOCs: Marino, Colli Albani, Colli Lanuvini, Velletri, Montecompatri-Colonna.

Malvasia del Lazio and the quality turn.

The change came when growers began to favour Malvasia del Lazio, also called Malvasia puntinata for the speckles on its skin, over the more productive Malvasia di Candia. Puntinata gives smaller yields but far more character: aromas of white peach, orange blossom and almond, a fuller texture, and the faintly bitter, salty finish that tastes of the tuff. Lower yields, earlier picking and careful pressing turned a tavern wine into something that can hold its own at the table for two or three years. Frascati Superiore must now reach a higher minimum alcohol and is often the estate's selection from older vines.

Cannellino is the hills' oldest luxury: Malvasia left on the vine into November until the berries shrivel and the sugars concentrate, sometimes helped by noble rot. The result is a golden, gently sweet wine of apricot and honey, a natural partner for the almond biscuits and ricotta tarts of the local pastry shops.

Est! Est!! Est!!! and the north.

Lazio's other famous white is a hundred kilometres north, at Montefiascone above Lake Bolsena. The name, Est! Est!! Est!!!, comes from a story told about a German bishop travelling to Rome in 1111 who sent a servant ahead to chalk Est ("it is", meaning the wine is good) on the doors of inns worth stopping at. At Montefiascone the servant was so impressed he wrote it three times. The wine is a blend of Trebbiano and Malvasia, light and easy; the legend is better than most bottles, but the best producers around the lake now make it with real care.

In the glass: Frascati Superiore.

 Colour: Pale straw with green glints.

 Nose: White flowers, citrus peel, white peach, bitter almond.

 Palate: Dry, medium body, fresh acidity, saline finish.

 Alcohol: 12–13.5%.

 Serve: 10–12 °C, within 2–3 years of the vintage.

 At the table: Fried artichokes, spaghetti alle vongole, porchetta, fritto misto, fresh pecorino.

How to drink it like a Roman.

Order it cold, in a carafe, at lunch on a Sunday, ideally in a fraschetta in Ariccia or Frascati with porchetta and a paper plate of pickled vegetables. Then try a Superiore from a serious estate alongside carciofi alla giudia and notice how the bitterness of the artichoke and the almond finish of the wine meet in the middle. That contrast, cheap jug wine and ambitious bottle from the same hills, is the whole story of Frascati in one afternoon.

Before you move on.

The Castelli Romani sit on an extinct volcano; the soils give the whites a mineral, faintly bitter edge.

Frascati was tavern wine for centuries; Malvasia del Lazio and lower yields made it serious.

Three names to remember: Frascati Superiore (dry), Cannellino (sweet), Est! Est!! Est!!! (Montefiascone).
