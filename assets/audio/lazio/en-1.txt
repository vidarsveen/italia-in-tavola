The Castelli Romani and Frascati.

The wine came down at night. The cart had two tall wheels, a painted canopy hung with fringes and bells, and one horse in the shafts. Under a mat lay the barrels, sixty litres to a barrel, half a botte; Romans called that quantity a carretto, after the cart that brought it. The carter wore velvet breeches, red stockings and shoes with buckles. He came down the Appia or the Tuscolana at four or five kilometres an hour, with a lantern lit. At Porta San Giovanni he paid his toll. Porters carried the barrels into the taverns that had hired him.

Giuseppe Gioachino Belli, who wrote his sonnets in Roman dialect, set down in April 1834 what the city did to its wine. It was mixed, he says, with rings of sulphur, with base coin, with something he calls munizione. Then he remembers Marino, one of the hill towns that Rome calls the Castelli Romani, where the household went every October and stayed until St Martin's day in November. Up there were no doctored wines. You drank a quartarolo, and what came out of the barrel was sincero: honest, unmixed, itself. At an osteria called er Grottino you could buy the right to drink by the hour, six or seven quattrini for as long as you sat there.

A grower with new wine to sell hung a branch over his door, usually laurel. That branch, the frasca, named the place: a fraschetta. Nearly all of them belonged to the growers themselves, which is the whole arrangement in one detail, because the man who made the wine also owned the room you drank it in. There was no kitchen. You brought the food yourself, and the people who did it had a name of their own, the fagottari, the bundle-people, after the bundle of dinner carried from home. What you bought was the wine, and you bought it by the measure.

The measures had names. Two litres was a barzilai, after Salvatore Barzilai, a politician who stood two litres to anyone who voted for him. Half a litre was the fojetta. Pope Sixtus V regulated the glasses in 1588, to stop innkeepers shaving the level. The correct mark was a line called the capello, the hair. Romans still say of a man splitting hairs that he is staring at the capello. Under that law a foglietta held 0.4557 litres. On the street it was a half, and nobody measured.

The hills are the rim of a volcano, and it is sleeping rather than dead. They are the Colli Albani, the Alban Hills, and the towns ringed around them are the Castelli: Frascati, Grottaferrata, Marino, Castel Gandolfo, Ariccia, Genzano, Nemi, Velletri and a handful more. The last eruption was about thirty-six thousand years ago, from the crater that now holds Lake Albano, and the institutes that watch it call the volcano quiescent. Between 1950 and 1993 surveyors measured the ground here rising by more than two metres. Small earthquakes still swarm under the crater, and the soil breathes out carbon dioxide and hydrogen sulphide, which is the hazard anyone living on this volcano actually has to manage.

Albano fills two craters that ran together and goes down a hundred and seventy metres, which makes it the deepest volcanic lake in Italy. Lake Nemi, higher up and far shallower, is two more craters joined where the wall between them collapsed. What the eruptions left on the slopes is tuff and pozzolana, dark, porous and free-draining, and that is the ground the vines stand in.

The same eruptions built the city. The red ash under these hills was thrown out four hundred and fifty-six thousand years ago. From about the time Augustus became emperor it was the aggregate Roman builders mixed into their concrete: the Forum, the markets of Trajan, the great vaults that are still standing. The other Roman concrete, the kind that sets hard under seawater, was made with a different ash altogether, from the Bay of Naples two hundred kilometres south. Vitruvius knew the difference and wrote it down. These hills are in the standing city. The harbours belong to Pozzuoli.

The cardinals of the papal court built their summer villas above Frascati, and the popes followed a generation later. Castel Gandolfo passed to the Holy See in 1604, and from 1626 Urban VIII had a palace built there on the ruins of Domitian's villa, making the first papal stay in May of that year.

Frascati is mostly Malvasia, and which Malvasia decides everything. Malvasia del Lazio is the fine one, called puntinata for the single grey dot at the navel of the berry, where the flower was attached. It gives a smaller crop and a finer wine, it concentrates sugar well, and it catches almost every disease a vine can get. Malvasia bianca di Candia is tougher and plainer, and growers tired of losing crops planted that instead. Then, through the 1960s and 1970s, Frascati went everywhere. Cellars sold to supermarkets and competed on price rather than quality. The vines went up onto tendone, the high overhead pergolas that carry an enormous crop. By the 1980s the quantities were enormous, and in twenty years the name stopped meaning what it had meant for centuries.

The recovery had already started while that was still going on. In 1985 Giulio Santarelli, at the time an undersecretary at the ministry of agriculture, planted thirteen hectares at Grottaferrata and brought in Professor Attilio Scienza to work on them. Over the seven years to 1992 they hunted down Lazio varieties that phylloxera and the international grapes had nearly finished off. They put them back into a working vineyard, a few kilometres from the tendone filling the supermarket shelves. What turned a tavern wine into a bottle that holds up at the table was lower yields, earlier picking and careful pressing.

Cannellino is the same grapes picked late. The bunches hang into November until the berries shrivel and the sugar concentrates, sometimes helped by noble rot, and the rules allow the drying to be finished indoors as well as on the vine. What goes into the bottle must carry at least thirty-five grams of sugar in a litre. It comes out golden and gently sweet, with apricot and honey in it, and the pastry shops of the hills pour it with their almond biscuits and ricotta tarts. The name is usually explained by the cannella, the little tap on the end of a barrel. Sellers will tell you the wine is as old as the hills themselves, and it may be, but what can be shown of it starts in the twentieth century. Everything earlier is asserted rather than documented.

Lazio's other old white grows a hundred kilometres north, at Montefiascone above Lake Bolsena. The story goes that in 1111 a bishop on his way to Rome sent his servant Martino ahead of him. Martino was to chalk Est, meaning there is good wine here, on the door of any inn worth a halt. At Montefiascone he wrote it three times. The bishop stayed, drank, and lies in San Flaviano under an inscription that blames the wine for killing him. The story is old. The doubt is nearly as old. In 1680 a German scholar published a Latin dissertation at Altdorf on the things commonly said about a certain German drinker who died at Montefiascone, and called the whole business a fabula, a fable. By then the tale was already old, already everywhere, and already doubted. No twelfth-century document names the bishop at all.

The wine under the name is Trebbiano toscano, which is called Procanico here, with Trebbiano giallo, which Lazio calls Rossetto, and a little Malvasia. It was recognised on the same day as Frascati, in the same decree, in March 1966. Two of the first four denominations in Italy came from these hills and that lake.

The consortium counts about four hundred growers who claim the Frascati name, and thirty-three cellars that make and bottle the wine. Four hundred people own the vines. Thirty-three own the wine. The carts stopped running long ago, and the arrangement that filled them did not.
