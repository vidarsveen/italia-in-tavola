Cesanese: Lazio's forgotten red.

Above Olevano Romano, in the hills east of Rome, stands a hectare of Cesanese d'Affile that Guido Ciolli planted in 1953 and trained as bushes. He sold the wine in bulk, and so did his son Costantino, and so did most growers at Olevano. None of it carried a name. In 2001 his grandson Damiano bottled the fruit of that hectare with his own name on the label. The vines had not changed. Everything around them had.

Cesanese is Lazio's own red, and it has no known relative among Italian varieties. Where the name came from is not understood either. There are two forms of it, and calling them two grapes, as the books often do, puts the difference too strongly. Cesanese d'Affile and Cesanese comune are biotypes, near-identical in the laboratory and told apart by the look of the vine. What separates them is the berry. Affile's are smaller, with thick skins carrying a heavy load of colour and tannin, while the common type gives a bigger crop of bigger fruit, which is what a grower paid by weight wanted.

Cesanese ripens late, through the second half of October and at Olevano sometimes into November, and it carries its berries in a tight bunch. A tight bunch in a wet autumn rots. So the old answer was to pick before the rot arrived, while the sugar was up. What that gave was a wine that was sweet, lightly sparkling and drunk inside the year. That was not carelessness. It was the sensible response to the risk, and it is still legal: amabile, dolce and dolce frizzante stand in the Olevano rules today beside the dry wine.

The country these villages stand in is called Ciociaria, after the ciocia, a triangle of leather laced up the calf with thongs, which shepherds and farmers here once wore. The word is Rome's, and it was not meant kindly. It began as city dialect for the poor men who came in wearing that footwear, and the district ended up named after the shoes of its poorest visitors.

Three appellations share the grape. Cesanese del Piglio lies on the slopes of the Monti Ernici, in the province of Frosinone. Cesanese di Affile is tiny, and its ground is not volcanic at all. It is terre rosse, the red earth left behind as limestone weathers away, which is worth holding beside the tuff and pozzolana of the Castelli. Cesanese di Olevano Romano faces west and south-west, and its statutes were regulating vineyards there in 1364.

What the growers of the revival changed was the vineyard and the cellar. They replanted Cesanese d'Affile on the best hillside sites, cut their yields, waited for full ripeness, and aged the wine in large old barrels instead of selling it off in bulk. Antonello Coletti Conti bottled his first vintage under his own name at Anagni in 2003, and Marcella Giuliani's estate took its present form in 2002. The appellation followed close behind them. Piglio was promoted in August 2008, and it was the first DOCG in Lazio of any colour; Frascati Superiore and Cannellino followed three years later. The delimited territory runs to more than fifteen thousand hectares, of which about a hundred and forty-four are under vine.

The whole appellation makes something like half a million bottles in a year. Frascati, an hour's drive away, makes ten and a half million.

In 1860 an agronomist named Pasquale Visocchi began carrying French vines into the Valle di Comino, in the far south-east of Lazio: Cabernet Franc, Cabernet Sauvignon, Merlot, Syrah and Gamay. The family winery was working by about 1868, putting four thousand quintals of grapes through it and exporting the wine, and it ran for the better part of a century. It was among the first places in newly unified Italy to grow them, and the wine has carried the Atina name since 1999. In the hills south of the Castelli, at Cori, the Nero Buono grows almost nowhere outside that one commune and makes a dark, plummy wine; where it came from nobody has established. North, above Lake Bolsena, Aleatico di Gradoli is a sweet red made in three villages and a piece of a fourth, traditionally drunk with dry biscuits or on its own after dinner.

When researchers went into the old vineyards of the denomination and tested what was actually growing in them, the vines came back as a single type: Cesanese d'Affile. Most of what grows in those old rows is not the common grape at all. And the ledger of the abbey of Subiaco, which owned most of the land around Piglio, is recorded as setting one word against both of its October harvests in 1838. At Subiaco and at Piglio alike, that word is Cesanese. The grape was never lost. It simply never had its name on a bottle.
