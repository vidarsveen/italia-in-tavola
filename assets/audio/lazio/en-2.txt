Cesanese: Lazio's forgotten red.

A native grape that nearly disappeared, three villages that kept it alive, and the reds that Roman lamb was made for.

Rome is a white-wine city with a red-meat kitchen. Roast lamb, oxtail, tripe, pork jowl: the Roman table calls for a red, and for most of the twentieth century it got one from anywhere but Lazio. The region's own red grape, Cesanese, was grown by farmers who could not be bothered to fight it, drunk sweet and fizzy at village festivals, and quietly forgotten. Its revival over the past thirty years is one of the small good-news stories of Italian wine.

Where it grows.

Cesanese belongs to the hills that rise east of Rome towards the Apennines, the country that Romans call Ciociaria after the ciocie, the leather sandals its shepherds once wore. Three appellations share the grape, each named for a village:

Cesanese del Piglio DOCG, around Piglio, Anagni and Acuto in the province of Frosinone. Promoted to DOCG in 2008, Lazio's first red at that level.

Cesanese di Affile DOC, a tiny mountain zone where the finer biotype of the grape, Cesanese di Affile, is thought to have originated.

Cesanese di Olevano Romano DOC, on the slopes of the Monti Prenestini closer to Rome.

There are two Cesanese vines. Cesanese Comune is the vigorous, large-berried type that farmers planted for volume. Cesanese di Affile has small, loose bunches and thick skins, ripens with more concentration, and is now the grape serious producers choose. Both ripen late, well into October, and both are prone to rot in a wet autumn. That is why past generations often picked early and made the wine off-dry or slightly sparkling, and why the grape got its reputation for being difficult.

What it tastes like.

Made dry and with patience, Cesanese is a medium to full-bodied red with soft tannins and a scent that is unmistakably its own: ripe red cherry and plum, black pepper and cloves, dried violets, sometimes a note of liquorice or tar as it ages. On the palate there is warmth (13.5–15% is common), a savoury, slightly bitter twist at the end, and enough acidity to keep it lively with food. It is not a wine for long ageing, though the best Piglio Superiore Riserva bottlings improve for five to eight years.

In the glass: Cesanese del Piglio.

 Colour: Medium ruby, garnet with age.

 Nose: Red cherry, plum, black pepper, clove, violet.

 Palate: Medium-full, soft tannin, warm, savoury bitter finish.

 Alcohol: 13.5–15%.

 Serve: 16–18 °C; decant young Riserva for an hour.

 At the table: Abbacchio, coda alla vaccinara, amatriciana, porchetta, aged pecorino.

The revival.

The turn came in the 1990s and 2000s, when a handful of growers in Piglio and Olevano replanted Cesanese di Affile on the best hillside sites, cut yields, waited for full ripeness and aged the wine in large old barrels rather than selling it in bulk. Producers such as Damiano Ciolli in Olevano Romano showed that the grape could make a wine of real elegance, and the DOCG status for Piglio in 2008 gave the zone the recognition it needed. Cesanese is still a small production, a few million bottles a year, but it is now on the lists of the better Roman trattorias, which is exactly where it belongs.

The other reds, and a sweet one.

Lazio's other reds are minor but worth knowing. Nero Buono di Cori, from the hills south of the Castelli, is another native grape making dark, plummy wines. Around Atina in the far south-east, French varieties planted in the nineteenth century gave a Cabernet-based DOC. And from the north, above Lake Bolsena, comes Aleatico di Gradoli, a sweet, aromatic red from the Aleatico grape that smells of roses and Muscat and is traditionally drunk with dry biscuits or after dinner on its own.

Pairing at the Roman table.

The classic match is abbacchio alla romana: young lamb roasted with rosemary, garlic and a splash of vinegar, or the same lamb as scottadito, small chops grilled and eaten with the fingers, "burning-finger" style. The peppery warmth of Cesanese and the fat of the lamb are a pairing that predates the DOCG by a few centuries. It also handles the Roman offal dishes that a lighter red would lose to: oxtail stewed with celery and cocoa, or tripe with mint and pecorino.

Before you move on.

Cesanese is Lazio's native red; Cesanese di Affile is the quality biotype.

Three zones: Piglio (DOCG), Affile and Olevano Romano. Late ripening explains its difficult past.

Think cherry, pepper and violet, and pour it with lamb, oxtail or amatriciana.
