The four Roman pastas.

Gricia, cacio e pepe, amatriciana and carbonara share three ingredients and a shepherd's logic. Learn the family tree and the technique behind the sauce.

Ask a Roman to name the city's dishes and four pastas come first, in a family order that everyone agrees on: gricia, cacio e pepe, amatriciana and carbonara. They are variations on a single idea. Start with cured pork jowl and sheep's cheese, add black pepper; take away the pork and you have cacio e pepe; add tomato and you have amatriciana; add egg and you have carbonara. Understand the three ingredients and the technique, and you understand all four.

The pantry of the shepherds.

None of this is city food in origin. The Roman Campagna was for centuries grazing land, and every autumn shepherds drove their flocks down from the Apennine mountains of Abruzzo and the Lazio borderlands to winter on the plain. What they carried had to keep without a kitchen: cured pork, hard cheese, dried pasta, pepper.

Guanciale is pork jowl, cured with salt and black pepper for several weeks. It is fattier than pancetta and its fat renders into a clear, sweet, almost nutty liquid that becomes the base of the sauce. Bacon is smoked and pancetta is belly; neither tastes the same, and Roman cooks are not flexible on the point.

Pecorino Romano is a hard sheep's-milk cheese, salted heavily and aged at least five months, sharp enough to season a dish on its own. Despite the name, most of it is now made in Sardinia, which the DOP allows along with Lazio and the province of Grosseto. Do not use Parmigiano: it is sweeter, softer and made from cow's milk, and the dish becomes something else. Black pepper, freshly cracked and ideally toasted for a moment in the pan, is the third constant.

Gricia, the mother sauce.

Gricia is guanciale, pecorino and pepper, sometimes called amatriciana bianca, white amatriciana, because it is the same dish before tomatoes arrived. Its name is argued over: some derive it from Grisciano, a hamlet a few kilometres from Amatrice, others from the gricio, the old Roman word for the bread and grocery sellers who came from the Swiss Grisons. Either way it is the oldest of the four, and the one to cook first because it teaches the technique: render the guanciale slowly, toss the pasta in the fat with a splash of its cooking water, then the cheese off the heat.

Cacio e pepe, the hardest simple dish.

Cheese and pepper, nothing more, and famously easy to get wrong. The secret is starch. Pasta water, especially from a small pot, carries enough starch to bind grated pecorino into a smooth emulsion; too hot and the cheese seizes into rubbery clumps, too little water and it stays grainy. Roman cooks make a paste of finely grated cheese and lukewarm pasta water in a bowl, toast the pepper, and toss the pasta with the paste off the heat until it turns glossy. Tonnarelli, a square-cut fresh egg spaghetti, is the traditional shape; the rough surface holds the sauce.

Amatriciana, the one with a hometown.

Amatriciana is gricia plus tomato, and it comes from Amatrice, a mountain town in the north-east corner of Lazio (it belonged to Abruzzo until 1927). Tomatoes reached the dish in the eighteenth or nineteenth century; the town's official recipe insists on guanciale, pecorino, tomato, a splash of white wine and a little chilli, and explicitly excludes onion and garlic, which Roman restaurants nonetheless often add. In 2020 the European Union registered Amatriciana tradizionale as a Traditional Speciality Guaranteed, the first pasta sauce with that protection. After the earthquake that destroyed Amatrice in 2016, restaurants across Italy put the dish on their menus to raise money for the town.

Carbonara, the youngest.

Carbonara is gricia plus egg: whole eggs or yolks beaten with pecorino and pepper, poured over the hot pasta and guanciale off the heat so the residual warmth thickens the egg into a sauce without scrambling it. No cream, ever. It is also the youngest of the four. The name suggests the carbonai, the charcoal burners of the Apennines, but the first written recipes appear only after the Second World War, and the most plausible story is that it took shape in Rome in 1944 when American rations of bacon and powdered egg met Roman pasta and pecorino. Whatever its origin, it is now the most cooked Italian dish in the world and the one Italians most love to see done wrong abroad.

The family tree.

Gricia = guanciale + pecorino + pepper.

Cacio e pepe = pecorino + pepper (+ pasta water).

Amatriciana = gricia + tomato (+ white wine, chilli).

Carbonara = gricia + egg.

What to pour.

The white sauces want a white with acidity and a little bitterness to cut the fat: a cold Frascati Superiore is the local answer, and it is right. Amatriciana, with its tomato and chilli, moves you to red: a young Cesanese, or across the border a Montepulciano d'Abruzzo, which is what Amatrice itself would drink. With carbonara, Romans are divided between white and a light red; the safe choice is the Frascati, the interesting one is a sparkling red like a dry Lambrusco.

Before you move on.

Guanciale, Pecorino Romano and black pepper are the three constants; no bacon, no Parmigiano, no cream.

Gricia is the base; cacio e pepe subtracts, amatriciana and carbonara add.

The technique is an emulsion of rendered fat, starchy water and cheese, finished off the heat.
