The four Roman pastas.

In one corner of the rione Ponte the Romans had a square they called the piazza delli Matrigiani. It belonged, in the city's mind, to the mountain people who came down from around Amatrice. They had a lane of their own there, a lodging house, a church, and a market for the produce they carried in. On 22 August 1665 the Maestri di Strade, the officials who kept Rome's streets in order, gave them two days to clear its refuse.

What those migrants did in Rome was feed it. They kept inns, worked as kitchen boys, sold vegetables and hauled sacks, and enough of them ran osterie that matriciano came to mean, in Roman dialect, the keeper of an inn with a kitchen. Alessandro Rufini, writing in 1855 about where Rome's inns got their names, records an Osteria della Matriciana in via della Pilotta that was always run by a woman born at la Matrice. He gives no name for her. He also does not settle which Matrice he means. There is a Matrice near Campobasso in Molise, and Amatrice belonged to the same Kingdom of Naples until 1860, so the word fits both towns and Rufini does not say which he means.

The four dishes are usually told as a family. Gricia comes first, then cacio e pepe, then amatriciana, then carbonara: take out the pork, add the tomato, add the egg. The order is tidy and the documents do not support it. Amatriciana is the oldest of the four on paper. The name turns up in a Roman magazine in 1916, and the first recipe is Ada Boni's, at the end of the 1920s, with minced guanciale browned in lard with onion and then peeled tomatoes. Cacio e pepe is in her La cucina romana, published about 1930. Gricia is the worst documented of all four, because nobody has found a first printed appearance of the word at all. What Boni does give, near her matriciana, is the same sauce without tomato under another name, marchiciana. So gricia is probably the older dish and certainly the later word.

The story that explains all four is better known than any of the recipes, and it rests on nothing. It says the dishes came down with the shepherds who drove their flocks off the Apennines to winter on the Roman plain, carrying what would keep: cured pork, hard cheese, dried pasta, pepper. The transhumance is real, but the pantry in the story is not in the records. What they describe is bread baked hard enough to last for weeks, oil, cheese, onion, garlic, herbs and wine, and dishes made from stale bread and whey. Pepper was an expensive import; in London in the 1430s a master mason needed more than two days' wages for a pound of it. The men in the piazza of 1665 are the better witnesses, because they were in the city and cooking for money.

Guanciale is the pig's cheek, cut as a triangle from the throat, salted and hung to dry. Pepper or chilli goes on the cut face, though no specification demands it. What separates it from pancetta is the cut and the proportion rather than the time: in guanciale the fat predominates over the lean, and pancetta is belly. That fat is the whole point of the cut. It renders into a clear, sweet, almost nutty liquid, and that liquid is the base of three of the four sauces. Guanciale is usually unsmoked, although smoking is permitted around Amatrice.

Pecorino Romano is sheep's milk, salted hard and aged until it is sharp enough to season a dish on its own. The wheels are traditionally salted dry by hand over weeks, though the rules also allow brine. Most of it is no longer made anywhere near Rome. The designation covers all of Sardinia, all of Lazio and the Tuscan province of Grosseto, and Sardinia now makes almost all of it.

Cheese, pepper, and the water the pasta cooked in. It fails more often than it works, and in 2025 a group of physicists published the reason in Physics of Fluids. Grated pecorino in hot water is casein held in micelles, and heat makes the protein clump together. With starch below about one per cent of the weight of the cheese, the sauce seizes into what the authors named the Mozzarella Phase: lumps suspended in a separated liquid. Between two and three per cent it holds, because the starch binds to the cheese proteins and keeps them from finding each other. And the transition runs the opposite way to a cook's instinct. The sauce breaks when it is heated rather than when it cools, and it happens a little above sixty degrees. That is why the cheese goes in with the pan off the heat, and why a small pot of water, carrying more starch, beats a large one. Roman kitchens arrived at the same place without the equations. The cheese is worked to a paste with lukewarm pasta water in a bowl, and the pasta is tossed through it off the heat until it turns glossy; with too little water it stays grainy. The paper won an Ig Nobel prize.

Amatrice stands high in the north-eastern corner of Lazio, and it has only been in Lazio since a decree of 1927 moved its province; before that it was in Aquila degli Abruzzi. The town keeps an official recipe, and the thing it is most specific about is the pan: iron, strictly, with a grudging allowance of steel for restaurants. Guanciale, pecorino, tomato, a splash of dry white wine, a little chilli, and spaghetti rather than bucatini. It says nothing at all about onion. The prohibition everyone quotes belongs to a different document, the European registration of March 2020, which states that the sauce does not use garlic, onion or pork belly. The irony is that the old recipe used onion: Ada Boni browned her guanciale in lard with it in the 1920s, and the rule against it is a later tidying-up. That registration made Amatriciana Tradizionale a Traditional Speciality Guaranteed, the first pasta sauce protected that way, and Italy's third such name after mozzarella and Neapolitan pizza.

Shortly after half past three in the morning on 24 August 2016, an earthquake centred near Accumoli destroyed the town. Two hundred and ninety-nine people died, the great majority of them in Amatrice. That same day a blogger called Paolo Campana started a campaign. Restaurants would add a euro to the price of the dish, match it with a euro of their own, and send the two to the Italian Red Cross. Six hundred restaurants had joined within days.

Carbonara carries the most stories of the four, and mechanically it is the simplest to describe. Guanciale is rendered in its own fat. Eggs beaten with grated pecorino then go through the hot pasta with the pan off the heat, so the warmth left in it thickens them rather than scrambling them. The best-known story says it was invented in 1944, when American rations of bacon and powdered egg met Roman pasta and pecorino. There is a man behind it. Renato Gualandi, a young cook, said he put bacon, cream, cheese and powdered egg yolk together for a dinner of Allied officers at Riccione on 22 September 1944. Black pepper went in at the last moment. He said it in an interview in 2009, at the age of eighty-eight. Between retellings the hotel changes and so do the generals at the table, and no document from 1944 has ever turned up.

In August 1939, five years before that dinner, a Dutch-language daily in the Dutch East Indies ran a Rome correspondent's piece about two trattorie in Trastevere, and listed spaghetti alla carbonara among their dishes. The citation comes from the researchers who found it rather than from the page itself. If it stands, the dish had a name before the Americans arrived. What is certain is that the recipes came later. The first in print is American: Patricia Bronté's 1952 guide to the near north side of Chicago, describing the dish at a restaurant called Armando's, made with tagliarini and Parmesan. The first Italian recipe followed in 1954, in a Milanese magazine answering a reader's request, with pancetta, gruyère and a clove of garlic.

The charcoal burners the name points at have never been tied to the dish by any document. Alberto Capatti reads the name as a description of the food's register rather than a claim about its inventors. It suggests cheap ingredients in no fixed proportion, and the plain tastes of people used to eating out of doors.

The rule that no cream goes anywhere near a carbonara is younger still. Luigi Carnacina's La grande cucina of 1960 is the first Italian recipe to insist on guanciale rather than pancetta, and it is the recipe that fixed the dish in the form Romans now defend. It also calls for a few spoonfuls of very fresh liquid cream.
