Rome: the Colosseum and the eternal table.

A walk from the amphitheatre through the neighbourhoods that made Roman cooking: the Ghetto, Testaccio and the friggitoria on the corner.

Every course about Lazio starts in Rome, and every visit to Rome starts at the same building. The Colosseum is the largest amphitheatre ever built, and after nineteen centuries it is still the thing you see first when you climb out of the metro. It is also, if you walk out from it in any direction, the door to the neighbourhoods where Roman food was invented.

The Flavian Amphitheatre.

The emperor Vespasian began it around AD 72 on the drained lake of Nero's palace grounds, a deliberate gesture returning a private pleasure park to the people. His son Titus opened it in AD 80 with a hundred days of games, and the younger son Domitian added the hypogeum, the maze of tunnels, cages and lifts under the arena floor that you can see today because the floor is gone. The official name was the Flavian Amphitheatre, after the family; "Colosseum" came later, probably from the colossal bronze statue of Nero that stood beside it.

Key facts.

Elliptical, about 189 m long, 156 m wide and 48 m high, in travertine quarried at Tivoli.

80 numbered entrance arches; spectators found their seats by ticket number, as in a modern stadium.

Capacity around 50,000, some estimates higher.

A canvas awning, the velarium, was rigged by sailors from the fleet at Misenum to shade the crowd.

In use for games for roughly 450 years; later a fortress, a quarry, a chapel, and a garden of wild plants.

The three tiers of arches use the three classical orders in sequence, Doric at ground level, Ionic, then Corinthian, with a fourth solid storey on top. Much of the outer ring on the south side fell in medieval earthquakes and its stone went into palaces and churches across the city, which is why the building has its famous broken profile.

Eating in the shadow of the arena.

Romans ate at the games as they eat at football today. Excavations in the drains turned up fig seeds, olive stones, nut shells and the bones of chicken and pork, and outside the arches there would have been stalls selling bread, chickpeas and wine. Across the empire the Roman fast-food counter was the thermopolium, a stone bar with pots sunk into it, dozens of which survive at Pompeii and Ostia. The street-food habit never left the city.

Walk up into Monti, the oldest neighbourhood in Rome just behind the Colosseum, and you find the modern version: the friggitoria and the pizza al taglio counter. The essential order is a supplì, a croquette of risotto rice bound with a little ragù, stuffed with mozzarella, breaded and fried, followed by a square of pizza bianca with mortadella. This is how Romans eat between meals and it is where visitors should start.

The Ghetto and the artichoke.

Fifteen minutes' walk west, past the Capitoline hill, is the Ghetto, the few streets where Rome's Jews were confined by papal decree from 1555 until 1870. Rome's Jewish community is the oldest in Europe, older than Christianity in the city, and its kitchen shaped Roman cooking more than any other single tradition. The signature dish is carciofi alla giudia, "Jewish-style artichokes": the round, thornless Romanesco artichoke is trimmed, flattened, and fried twice in olive oil, once slowly to cook it through and once at high heat to crisp the outer leaves. It is eaten with the fingers, leaf by leaf, and it needs a cold glass of Frascati. Other dishes from the same streets: salt cod fillets fried in batter, aliciotti con l'indivia (anchovies baked with endive), and the ricotta and sour cherry tart of the Ghetto bakeries.

Testaccio and the fifth quarter.

South along the river is Testaccio, built around the city's slaughterhouse, the Mattatoio, which operated from 1890 to 1975. Its workers were partly paid in what the butchers could not sell, the quinto quarto or "fifth quarter" of the animal: tail, tripe, intestines, head, heart. The trattorias that fed them turned this into the dishes that define Roman cooking's deep end: coda alla vaccinara, oxtail braised with celery, tomato and a little cocoa; trippa alla romana with mint and pecorino; pajata, the milk-filled intestines of a suckling calf, in tomato sauce with rigatoni. Testaccio market, rebuilt in 2012, is still the best place to see the raw materials and eat a plate of them at lunch.

Reading the city through its food.

Put the three walks together and you have a map of how Romans think about eating. The Colosseum and the friggitoria stand for the street: quick, fried, eaten standing up. The Ghetto stands for memory, dishes carried through four centuries of confinement. Testaccio stands for thrift, the cuisine of people who wasted nothing. And the four pastas of the previous lesson run through all of it, because they belong to nobody and everybody. Order a supplì by the Colosseum, an artichoke in the Ghetto, and oxtail in Testaccio, and you have eaten Roman history in a day.

Before you move on.

The Colosseum (AD 72–80) held around 50,000 people and is the model of every stadium since.

Roman food has three roots: street food, the Jewish Ghetto, and the "fifth quarter" of Testaccio.

Three dishes to try in order: supplì, carciofi alla giudia, coda alla vaccinara.
