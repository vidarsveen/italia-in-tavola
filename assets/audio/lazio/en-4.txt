Rome: the Colosseum and the eternal table.

A great drain runs south from under the Colosseum. Archaeologists spent 2021 and 2022 clearing it, and what had washed in and stayed there was fruit: fig, grape, olive, peach, cherry, blackberry and melon stones, and nutshells. Then there were bones, from chickens, pigs and small dogs, and from the bears and big cats that died in the arena. With them came fifty-three bronze coins struck between about 250 and 450, dice cut from bone, hobnails, clothing studs and scraps of leather. A drain takes whatever runs into it, from the stands and the arena and the street above, across two centuries. So none of this is anybody's lunch. It is what the building shed.

Vespasian began it in the early seventies AD, on the drained lake of Nero's palace gardens, and its name was the Flavian Amphitheatre, after the family that built it. No ancient writer gives the year, and the archaeological park that runs the building today declines to give one either. Martial supplies the point of the gesture instead: where the mass of the amphitheatre rises, he wrote, were Nero's pools, and a few lines later, Rome has been given back to herself. Titus opened it in the year 80. The hundred days of games everyone quotes come from Cassius Dio, writing a century and a half afterwards, in a text that survives only through an eleventh-century Byzantine abridgement.

The floor Titus opened was wood, and so was everything beneath it. Domitian rebuilt the undercroft in masonry: the corridors, the animal cages, and the lifts whose shafts stand open to the sky now that the floor is gone. At its fullest there were sixty capstans down there, each two storeys tall, each level turned by four men. They worked in the dark under the arena, raising cages and scenery into the light.

Eighty arches ring the ground floor, seventy-six of them numbered for the public, and twenty-nine of those numbers can still be read on the north side. Above the three tiers of arcades sits a fourth storey, solid wall instead of openings. The stone is travertine from the quarries at Barco outside Tivoli, some thirty kilometres away, brought in by ox-cart. The pockmarks across the facade are the holes where its iron cramps were dug out, for scrap. The broken profile has a date: an earthquake in 1349 brought down the outer ring on the south side, which had been built on unstable alluvial ground.

The fourth-century catalogue of the city credits the building with eighty-seven thousand loca. A locus is a foot of bench rather than a person, so that figure measures timber and not a crowd. About forty-five thousand seated, with standing room above, is where the familiar fifty thousand comes from.

Nobody has yet found an ancient description of food being sold at a Roman spectacle. What the sources do describe is giving it away. At Titus's opening games, Dio says, the emperor had little wooden balls thrown down into the seats. Each was inscribed with something: a quantity of food, clothing, a silver vessel or a gold one, horses, pack animals, cattle, slaves. Whoever caught one carried it to the officials and collected the thing it named. Suetonius lists the same practice under Nero, with an inventory that ends in ships, blocks of flats and farms. He lists it again under Domitian, who handed baskets of food to the senators and knights and smaller ones to everybody else, and began eating first himself.

Seneca went to the games once at the wrong hour, a decade or so before this building went up. He arrived at midday expecting wit and some relief for the eyes, and found the executions instead. In the morning, he wrote, men are thrown to lions and bears; at midday they are thrown to their spectators. Around him the crowd was shouting kill him, flog him, burn him, and asking why he met the sword so timidly.

Behind the Colosseum lies Monti, Rome's first rione. What stood here in antiquity was the Suburra, the crowded valley of workshops, shouting traders and prostitutes. Juvenal used it as his measure of the unbearable: he would sooner live on a bare islet off Naples, he says, than in the Suburra.

Roman cities did have counters selling hot food, and Pompeii's are the ones that survive. There are about eighty of them on the archaeological park's count, or a hundred and fifty-eight if every masonry counter is included. They are usually called thermopolia, though Steven Ellis has pointed out that the Latin label was fixed on these rooms by their eighteenth- and nineteenth-century excavators and has no support in the record. The one uncovered in 2020 had a nereid on a sea-horse painted on its counter, two mallards hanging head-down beside a rooster, and a jar of wine with crushed fava beans in it. Apicius used them to lighten the colour.

The fried food is still here, and the shop that sells it is a friggitoria. A supplì is a croquette of risotto rice bound with a little ragù, stuffed with mozzarella, breaded and fried. Pull one apart while it is hot and the cheese draws out into a long thread, which is why the full name is supplì al telefono, from the days when a telephone had a cord. Treccani derives the word, with a perhaps, from the French surprise, and nobody has shown how it travelled from French into Roman dialect. Beside the supplì on the same counter are squares of pizza al taglio, cut and sold by weight, and pizza bianca with mortadella folded into it.

The Ghetto lies west of the Capitoline, on the bank of the Tiber. On 14 July 1555 Paul IV issued a bull that opened by calling it exceedingly absurd for Jews to live among Christians. It ordered them into one quarter beside the Tiber, with a single entrance and a single exit. They were to sell their property, wear a distinguishing mark, and live by the rag trade. The wall came down on 17 April 1848. The obligation to live inside it ended on 20 September 1870, when the Italian army broke through the Porta Pia and the popes lost the city. The quarter itself went last. The demolition was decided in 1885 and carried out from 1888, and the lanes that disappeared included via delle Azzimelle, named for its matzo bakery, and the little piazza del Pancotto.

The community is one of the oldest continuously settled in Europe, and there were Jews in Rome before there were Christians in it. The dish those streets are known for is carciofi alla giudia. The artichoke is a thornless Romanesco, and specifically a cimarolo, the head at the top of the plant, the first and the largest. It is trimmed, opened out and fried twice in olive oil, slowly at first to cook the heart through and then hot to make the leaves brittle. In 2018 the import division of Israel's rabbinate ruled that Roman artichokes could not be kosher, because the heads cannot be cleaned of insects. Rome's chief rabbi issued no rebuttal. He and the president of the Jewish community were filmed cleaning artichokes for Passover. The quarter's bakers also still make a tart of ricotta and sour cherries. The tradition is that the ricotta was hidden between two layers of pastry in the years when papal edicts barred Jews from dealing in dairy.

South along the river, Testaccio was built around the city's slaughterhouse, which opened in 1891 to Gioacchino Ersoch's design and killed its last animal in July 1975. The quarter's cooking is the slaughterhouse's cooking, and its name is the quinto quarto, the fifth quarter. A carcass divides into four, and what is left over is the head, the tail, the feet and the offal. Romans say the workers were paid partly in it. No wage agreement, contract or guild rule says so, and payment in kind was ordinary enough in the trade to make the story plausible and leave it unproven.

What the trattorias made of it is still on their menus. Coda alla vaccinara is oxtail, with cheek in Ada Boni's version, cooked long with tomato and celery, and the celery matters enough to have got into the name of the dish in some books. Some Roman kitchens finish it with bitter cocoa, pine nuts and raisins and others call that a restaurant elaboration; both are written down. Trippa alla romana is tripe in tomato with mentuccia, the small Roman mint, and a great deal of pecorino. Saturday is its day, and giovedì gnocchi, venerdì pesce, sabato trippa was in print long before food writers got to it and is still painted on trattoria signs. Pajata is the jejunum of a milk-fed calf with its chyme still in it, cooked in tomato with rigatoni. A European regulation of 2001 put calf intestine on the list of specified risk material after BSE, and for fourteen years the dish was made with lamb instead. The rule was narrowed in 2015 and the calf came back.

The vaccinari the oxtail takes its name from were not only beef butchers. In Rome the word covered the men who worked hides, the tanners and flayers, and a street in the Regola still carries their name.

The quarter shares its name with the hill at its edge, and the hill is made of jars. Monte Testaccio stands about thirty-five metres above the street and runs nearly a kilometre around. It is built from the broken remains of oil amphorae, Dressel 20s, the big globular jars that brought olive oil up from southern Spain, with smaller numbers from Libya and Tunisia. Estimates of how many run from twenty-five to fifty-three million, and none of them is a count. They were dumped rather than reused because the clay was unglazed inside, so the oil went rancid in the fabric, and because that shape broke into large curved pieces that would not reduce to shards. Many of them still carry their paperwork, painted on the side: what was in the jar, what it weighed, who weighed it, and the district it came from.
