Vines on the cliffs.

At harvest in Volastra, the hamlet on the hill above Manarola, the grapes used to leave the vineyards in baskets carried on the shoulder. In May 1980 two small rack railways from Switzerland, the first of their kind in Italy, were fixed to the slope there. A professor named Stelvio Celsi had heard about them two years earlier at a conference in Saint-Vincent. More than fifty of these monorails now run through the vineyards of the Cinque Terre. Each carries up to three hundred kilos of grapes on a single rail laid up the slope, more slowly than a person walks.

Liguria is the arc of coast between France and Tuscany, some 350 kilometres long and in places only a few kilometres wide. Along almost all of it the Maritime Alps and the Apennines come straight down to the sea. Genoa sits halfway along the arc. West of the city is the Riviera di Ponente, the coast of the setting sun. It is broader and gentler, with the olive groves of Imperia and the flower fields and vineyards of the plain at Albenga. East of Genoa is the Riviera di Levante, the coast of the rising sun, and beyond Portofino and the Gulf of Tigullio the mountains fall directly into the water. The Cinque Terre are five villages on that stretch, from Monterosso to Riomaggiore. Each one fills a ravine between two headlands, and until the railway came they could be reached only by boat or on mule tracks. There is almost no flat land on this coast, and whatever grows here, vines, olives or basil, grows on a terrace.

The terraces were built by people who had no other land to farm. The national park dates the first of them to around the year 1000. UNESCO puts most of them in the twelfth century, after the Saracen raids from the sea had come to an end. Either way the work went on for centuries, one wall at a time, with the stone carried up the slope and the soil carried up behind it. By the park's estimate the dry-stone walls of the Cinque Terre add up to some 6,700 kilometres. A terrace is often only a couple of metres wide, with room for two rows of vines. The vines were traditionally trained on a low pergola. It is a frame about half a metre high that runs from the edge of the terrace to the foot of the wall above, and under it the rows form low tunnels. Pruning, tying and picking are all done by hand, and that makes the wine expensive to produce.

Besides holding up the soil, the wall drains the winter rain, gives back to the vines at night the heat it took in by day, and keeps the hillside from sliding towards the sea. When a terrace is abandoned, its wall falls within a few decades, the slope goes with it, and the village below lies in its path. Walter De Battè has grown vines at Riomaggiore since the early 1990s, on slopes that rise seven metres for every ten. He has rebuilt more than three hundred metres of wall, and some of it stands two or three metres high.

On 25 October 2011 more than half a metre of rain fell on the hills behind the coast in six hours. Mud and stones came down the ravines, and the streets of Vernazza and Monterosso were buried. Four people died in the two villages, and thirteen across the whole storm, which also struck the Val di Vara inland and the town of Aulla over the Tuscan border. Accounts of the disaster name the long abandonment of the terraces among the reasons the slopes gave way. Since then the rebuilding has had public money behind it. The park gives grants for new walls, and its call for 2025 alone covered about four kilometres of them. A foundation set up at Manarola in 2014 clears abandoned terraces, rebuilds their walls and leases them to farms that will work them again.

The everyday wine of the terraces is Cinque Terre DOC. It is a white made from Bosco, a grape grown almost nowhere outside eastern Liguria, together with Albarola and Vermentino. It is pale, saline and faintly bitter, and it smells of the herbs on the hillside. In 1973 the growers of the five villages formed a cooperative, the Cantina Cinque Terre, and since 1982 its cellar has stood at Groppo, high above Manarola. About two hundred and twenty growers belong to it, and a dozen others make their own wine. In the villages the wine is poured with the salted anchovies of Monterosso.

The same grapes make the wine the coast is famous for. For Sciacchetrà the bunches stay on the vine until September, and then they are laid on racks in airy rooms to dry. By November a bunch has lost about half its weight, and the rules do not allow the wine to be made before the first of that month. By then the grapes must hold enough sugar for at least seventeen per cent of alcohol. The must ferments slowly, part of the sugar stays in the wine, and what comes out is sweet and amber, with apricot, honey and dried fig and an edge of salt. A hundred kilos of grapes give only about twenty-five litres. In 2020 fifteen producers set out some thirty-five tonnes of grapes to dry, which is enough for about nine thousand litres from the whole of the Cinque Terre. The name is usually explained as two dialect verbs, sciaccà, to crush, and trà, to draw off.

That name does not appear in writing until the late nineteenth century, but the wine of these slopes was known long before. Petrarch praised the vineyards of Corniglia in his Latin poem Africa, and Boccaccio made the wine part of a cure. In one story of the Decameron the Abbot of Cluny is on his way to the baths of Siena for his stomach when the outlaw Ghino di Tacco takes him prisoner. For several days Ghino gives him nothing but two slices of toasted bread and a large glass of vernaccia from Corniglia, taken from the abbot's own supply. At the end of it the abbot says that he wants nothing so much as to eat, so well has the treatment cured him.
