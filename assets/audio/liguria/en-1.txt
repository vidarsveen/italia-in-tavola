Vines on the cliffs.

Liguria is a thin crescent of mountain falling into the sea, and its vineyards hang on terraces built by hand over a thousand years. The Cinque Terre, the wine called Sciacchetrà, and why the walls matter more than the grapes.

Liguria is the arc of coast between France and Tuscany, three hundred and fifty kilometres long and in places only a few kilometres wide, where the Apennines and the Maritime Alps meet the sea without bothering to leave a plain in between. There is almost no flat land. Everything the region grows, olives, basil, vines, grows on terraces, narrow steps of soil held up by dry-stone walls, cut into slopes that a walker needs both hands for. The vineyards are the steepest in Italy and among the steepest in the world, and the wine they make is small in quantity, expensive to produce and mostly drunk within sight of where it grew.

Two rivieras.

Genoa sits in the middle of the arc and divides it. West of the city is the Riviera di Ponente, the coast of the setting sun, broader and gentler, with the olive groves of Imperia and the flower fields and vineyards of the Albenga plain; the next reading is about its wines. East is the Riviera di Levante, the coast of the rising sun, where the mountains come straight down into the water: Portofino, the Gulf of Tigullio, and then the stretch of coast that has come to stand for the whole region, the Cinque Terre. Five villages, Monterosso, Vernazza, Corniglia, Manarola and Riomaggiore, wedged into ravines between headlands, reachable until the railway came only by boat or by mule track, and surrounded on every side by walls.

Key facts.

Cinque Terre DOC (1973): a dry white from at least 40% Bosco with Albarola and Vermentino; three named slopes above Riomaggiore, Costa de Sera, Costa de Campu and Costa da Posa, may appear on the label.

Sciacchetrà: the same grapes dried on racks for weeks, fermented to at least 17% alcohol, sweet, amber, released the November after the vintage; Riserva after three years. Production is a few thousand litres a year.

The terraces are held by dry-stone walls whose total length is put by local estimates in the thousands of kilometres; the Cinque Terre, Porto Venere and the islands were listed by UNESCO in 1997, and a national park followed in 1999.

In October 2011 floods and mudslides, made worse by abandoned terraces, killed nine people and buried Vernazza and Monterosso in mud.

The wall is the vineyard.

The terraces were built between the eleventh and the nineteenth centuries by people with no other land, one wall at a time, carrying the stone up and the soil up behind it. A terrace two metres wide holds two rows of vines, trained low on a pergola so that the sea wind passes over them, and everything on it is done by hand: pruning, tying, harvesting into baskets that were once carried down on shoulders and now ride on small rack-and-pinion monorails bolted to the slope. The wall does more than hold the soil. It drains the winter rain, stores the sun's heat for the vine at night, and keeps the hillside from sliding into the sea. When a terrace is abandoned, the wall falls within a few decades, the slope goes with it, and the village below is the next thing downhill. The floods of 2011 made that clear, and since then the national park has paid for walls to be rebuilt and land to be farmed again, vineyard by vineyard.

The wines: Cinque Terre and Sciacchetrà.

The dry white is Cinque Terre DOC, from the local Bosco grape, which grows nowhere else, with Albarola and Vermentino: pale, saline, faintly bitter, smelling of the herbs of the hillside, made in tiny quantities by the cooperative at Riomaggiore and a dozen growers, and best drunk on a terrace with the anchovies of Monterosso. The famous wine is Sciacchetrà, the name probably from the dialect for "crush and pull", made from the same grapes left on the vine until September and then dried on racks in airy rooms until November, when a bunch has lost half its weight. Pressed, the must ferments slowly to a golden, sweet wine of at least 17% alcohol, with apricot, honey, dried fig and a salt edge from the sea. Petrarch and Boccaccio praised the wine of this coast, and the Republic of Genoa sent it as a gift to popes. It is rare, a few thousand litres a year, and priced like it; a half-bottle from Walter De Battè, Possa or the cooperative is the true souvenir of the Cinque Terre.

In the glass: Cinque Terre DOC.

 Colour: Pale straw with green glints.

 Nose: Sea air, thyme and rosemary, green apple, white flowers, a hint of bitter almond.

 Palate: Dry, light, saline, fresh acidity, a faint bitterness on the finish.

 Alcohol: 12–13%.

 Serve: 10–12 °C, within two years.

 At the table: Salted anchovies, fried fish, trofie al pesto, vegetable pies.

The Colli di Luni and the Tuscan border.

East of La Spezia, where Liguria runs out at the marble mountains of Carrara, the hills of the Colli di Luni make the region's roundest white, a Vermentino grown on gentler slopes on both sides of the Tuscan border, named for the Roman port of Luni that silted up and vanished. It is the Ligurian white most often found outside the region, and the estate of Lunae Bosoni at Castelnuovo Magra has put it on lists across Italy. Between the Colli di Luni and the Cinque Terre lies the Gulf of La Spezia, the Gulf of Poets, where Shelley drowned and Byron swam, and where the pine-shaded villages of Lerici and Tellaro keep the Levante's last vineyards.

Before you move on.

Liguria has almost no flat land; its vines, olives and basil grow on terraces held by dry-stone walls that also hold the hillside up.

The Cinque Terre make a saline dry white from Bosco, Albarola and Vermentino, and the rare sweet Sciacchetrà from dried grapes at 17% or more.

Abandoned walls mean landslides, as the floods of 2011 showed; the national park now pays to rebuild them.
