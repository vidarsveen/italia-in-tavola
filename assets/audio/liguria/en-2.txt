Vermentino, Pigato and the sea.

Two names for one grape, a red that smells of roses in a valley behind the French border, and a coast that drinks white with everything. The wines of the western Riviera and how to read them.

Ligurian wine is white, and most of it is one grape with two names. Along the eastern Riviera and the Tuscan border it is called Vermentino; on the western Riviera, around Albenga and Imperia, the same vine is called Pigato, and the two make different wines from the same DNA. Behind them stand a handful of reds that hardly leave the region, a red from Dolceacqua that smells of roses and a mountain Dolcetto grown at 700 metres, and a wine culture that is entirely about what comes out of the sea and the vegetable garden.

One grape, two names.

Vermentino came to Liguria by sea, probably from Spain through Corsica and Sardinia, where it is also at home, and in Provence it is grown as Rolle. It likes slopes that face the sea and take the light reflected off the water, and it makes a white that is dry, medium-bodied, with citrus, white peach, thyme and a saline, bitter-almond finish. Pigato is genetically the same grape, and takes its name from the freckled skin of its berries, pigau in dialect, spotted. It grows on the plain of Albenga and in the valleys behind it, around Ortovero and Ranzo, and it gives a fuller, rounder, more aromatic wine than Vermentino from the same coast, with ripe apricot and a resinous edge. The difference is partly the clone, partly the soil and partly habit: a Ligurian orders Pigato with fish in Albenga and Vermentino with fish in Sestri Levante, and would not dream of swapping them.

Key facts.

Riviera Ligure di Ponente DOC (1988): the western coast from Genoa to the French border, with Pigato, Vermentino, Rossese, Granaccia and Ormeasco named on the label.

Rossese di Dolceacqua DOC (1972) was Liguria's first appellation: a pale, perfumed red from the Nervia and Verbone valleys behind Ventimiglia, at least 95% Rossese.

Ormeasco di Pornassio DOC is Dolcetto grown at up to 700 metres in the mountains behind Imperia; Granaccia is Grenache, kept alive at Quiliano near Savona.

Colli di Luni DOC (1989) at the Tuscan border makes the region's most exported Vermentino; Bianchetta Genovese and Lumassina are the local whites around Genoa and Savona.

Rossese: roses behind Ventimiglia.

The one red that matters grows in the last valley before France. Rossese di Dolceacqua comes from steep terraces in the Nervia valley, above the medieval village of Dolceacqua with its humpbacked bridge that Monet painted, and it is a red that behaves like the whites around it: pale, light, high in acid, with wild strawberry, rose and Mediterranean herbs, and a soft tannin that makes it drinkable at cellar temperature with fish. It was Liguria's first DOC in 1972, the old vineyards are named cru by cru on the labels of growers like Terre Bianche and Ka' Manciné, and Napoleon is said to have liked it, which every wine region claims. Further east, at Pornassio, the Dolcetto grape of Piedmont crosses the mountains and becomes Ormeasco, a purple, cherry-scented red made at altitudes where the vines see snow, and its rosé, Sciac-trà, is a pun on the sweet wine of the other Riviera.

In the glass: Riviera Ligure di Ponente Pigato.

 Colour: Straw yellow, deeper than Vermentino.

 Nose: Apricot, yellow peach, sage, pine resin, honey, sea spray.

 Palate: Dry, medium to full body, soft acidity, a saline, faintly bitter finish.

 Alcohol: 12.5–13.5%.

 Serve: 10–12 °C; within three years, the best keep five.

 At the table: Grilled fish, seafood pasta, rabbit with olives, torta pasqualina, pesto.

What the coast drinks.

The Ligurian table is fish, vegetables and herbs, and the wine follows. A dry white goes with almost everything from the anchovies of the aperitivo through the pesto to the fried fish, and the reds are light enough to sit alongside. There is no big red, no oak, no wine of ceremony; what the region has instead is one of the highest proportions in Italy of small growers making a few thousand bottles from terraces they work themselves. Production is tiny, about 0.2% of Italy's wine, less than a single large estate in Puglia. The wines are best drunk young, at the water's edge, with the meal the next reading describes. Names worth knowing on the Ponente: Bruna, Durin, Lupi, Claudio Vio and the cooperative of Ranzo for Pigato; Terre Bianche, Ka' Manciné and Maccario Dringenberg for Rossese.

Before you move on.

Vermentino and Pigato are the same grape; Pigato from Albenga is fuller and more aromatic, Vermentino from the Levante and Colli di Luni leaner and more saline.

Rossese di Dolceacqua (DOC 1972) is Liguria's light, rose-scented red from the valleys behind Ventimiglia; Ormeasco is mountain Dolcetto.

Ligurian wine is 0.2% of Italy's, made by small growers on terraces and drunk young with fish, vegetables and pesto.
