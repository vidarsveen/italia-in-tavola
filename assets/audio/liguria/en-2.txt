Vermentino, Pigato and the sea.

Francesca Bruna makes Pigato at Ranzo, in the Arroscia valley behind Albenga, and she says the grape loses its character when it is planted anywhere else. The laboratories say it is Vermentino. It has the same DNA as the white grape of the other end of the Ligurian coast, of Sardinia and Corsica, and of Provence, where it is called Rolle. The difference you can see is on the skin. As the berries ripen they are covered in small rust-coloured freckles, pighe in dialect, and that is where the name Pigato comes from.

Growers put the difference down to the clone, the site and the cellar. Pigato is a strain of the vine that has mutated, and the spotted skin is one sign of the mutation. It grows on the plain of Albenga and in the valleys behind it, around Ortovero and Ranzo. It does best on the higher ground, where hot days are followed by cool nights, on soils that run from red, iron-rich earth to white clay. In the cellar some growers leave the juice on the skins for a while or age the wine in wood. The Pigato that comes out is fuller, rounder and more aromatic than Vermentino, with ripe apricot and a resinous edge. Vermentino from the eastern Riviera is planted on slopes that face the sea and catch the light reflected off the water. It is leaner and more saline, and it tastes of citrus, white peach and thyme, with a bitter-almond finish.

At the far eastern end of Liguria the coast runs out at the marble mountains of Carrara, and Vermentino grows there on gentler hills that cross the border into Tuscany. They are the Colli di Luni, named after Luna, a Roman colony at the mouth of the river Magra. Its port shipped the marble of the Apuan mountains until the harbour silted up and the town was abandoned. The Vermentino of these hills is the Ligurian white most often found outside the region. The estate of Lunae Bosoni at Castelnuovo Magra has put it on wine lists across Italy.

In February 1884 Claude Monet was painting at Bordighera, and he went up the Nervia valley, the last valley before France, to the village of Dolceacqua. He wrote that its bridge was a jewel of lightness, and he painted the bridge and the castle above it. The Doria family held that castle. They are said to have had Napoleon as a guest and to have sent barrels of the local wine after him to Paris, though it is a story the village tells rather than a record. The vines on the terraces around the village are Rossese, and in 1972 Rossese di Dolceacqua became Liguria's first DOC. It is a red that behaves like the whites around it. It is pale, light and high in acid, it smells of wild strawberry, roses and Mediterranean herbs, and its tannin is soft enough for it to be drunk cool with fish. In Provence the same grape is called Tibouren.

Further east, in the mountains behind Imperia, the Dolcetto grape of Piemonte has crossed the watershed. At Pornassio it is called Ormeasco, and it grows at up to about eight hundred metres, where the vines see snow. The wine is purple and smells of cherries. Its rosé is called Sciac-trà, a pun on the sweet wine of the other Riviera.

Liguria makes very little wine. Its vineyards are split among thousands of farms, on holdings that average well under half a hectare, and nearly half of those farms bottle wine under their own name. In a normal year the region makes about one bottle in every thousand produced in Italy. Most of it is drunk young, close to where it grew, and the reds hardly leave the region at all. It goes with a table of fish, vegetables and herbs. A dry white does for almost all of it, from the anchovies before dinner to the pesto and the fried fish, and the reds are light enough to stand beside them.

In Albenga a plate of fried fish comes with a glass of Pigato. In Sestri Levante, on the other Riviera, the same plate comes with Vermentino, and in neither town would anyone call the two the same wine.
