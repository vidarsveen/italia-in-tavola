Pesto, focaccia and the olive.

A green sauce that conquered the world, a bread eaten for breakfast dipped in cappuccino, a small black olive and the oil it gives. The cooking of a coast that lived on herbs, vegetables and what sailors longed for at sea.

Ligurian cooking is sometimes called the cucina del ritorno, the cooking of the return: what sailors dreamed of on months of salt beef and biscuit, and what they were given when they came home. That means fresh herbs, green vegetables, oil, and pastry with things folded inside it. It is a cuisine of the garden rather than the sea, oddly for a coast, and it is built on three things that are protected by law: the basil of Genoa, the olive of Taggia, and the bread that Genoa eats at every hour of the day.

Pesto.

Pesto alla genovese is seven ingredients and a mortar. The basil is Basilico Genovese DOP, protected since 2005, the small-leaved, sweet variety grown in greenhouses on the terraces at Pra', west of Genoa, and picked young, before the leaves take on the mint flavour that basil from anywhere else has. It is pounded in a marble mortar with a wooden pestle, first a clove of garlic and a pinch of coarse salt, then pine nuts, then the leaves in handfuls, crushed against the sides with a circular motion until they give up a green cream, then grated Parmigiano and aged pecorino, and finally Ligurian olive oil stirred in until the sauce holds together. Nothing is cooked. The first written recipe is in Giovanni Battista Ratto's La Cuciniera Genovese of 1863, the city holds a world championship every two years in which entrants are judged on their mortar technique, and a blender, the purists say, bruises the leaves and turns the sauce black. The sauce goes on trofie, small twisted curls of pasta from Recco, on trenette cooked in the same pot as potatoes and green beans, or on mandilli de sæa, the silk handkerchiefs of thin egg pasta, always loosened with a spoonful of the pasta water.

Key facts.

Basilico Genovese DOP (2005): grown on the terraces of Pra' and the Ligurian coast; the pesto recipe is basil, garlic, pine nuts, Parmigiano, pecorino, extra-virgin olive oil and salt, pounded in a mortar.

Focaccia genovese: a centimetre thick, dimpled, brushed with oil and brine; eaten from breakfast on, dipped in cappuccino. Focaccia di Recco col formaggio IGP (2015): two paper-thin unleavened sheets around crescenza cheese.

Taggiasca olive: small, dark, sweet, from Taggia near Imperia; Riviera Ligure DOP oil (1997) is the mildest of Italy's great oils.

Farinata: chickpea flour, water, oil and salt baked in a wide copper pan in a wood oven, sold by the slice from the bakery window.

Focaccia and farinata.

Genoa's bread is focaccia, fügassa in dialect, a flat slab of yeasted dough about a centimetre thick, its surface pressed with fingertips into dimples that fill with olive oil and salty brine before it goes into the oven. It comes out gold, crisp underneath and soft inside, and it is sold by weight from every bakery from seven in the morning; the Genoese eat it for breakfast, dipped in a cappuccino, which visitors find hard to believe until they try it. The other focaccia is from Recco, a town east of Genoa, and it is a different thing: two sheets of unleavened dough stretched as thin as paper, with fresh crescenza cheese between them, baked in a very hot oven until the top blisters. It has had protected status since 2015 and can only be made in Recco and three neighbouring towns. Farinata is older than both: a batter of chickpea flour, water and oil poured into a wide copper pan and baked in a wood oven until the top is crisp and the inside custardy, eaten hot with pepper. The Genoese say it was invented in 1284, when the sacks of chickpeas in the hold of a galley returning from the battle of Meloria got soaked in seawater and the crew dried the paste in the sun.

The Taggiasca and its oil.

The olive of Liguria is the Taggiasca, named for the town of Taggia near Imperia where, the story goes, Benedictine monks planted it in the Middle Ages. It is a small olive, purple-black when ripe, and it gives an oil that is the opposite of Puglia's or Tuscany's: pale gold, delicate, sweet, almost without bitterness, tasting of almond and ripe fruit. Ligurian cooks use it the way others use butter, generously and without letting it dominate, and it is the oil for pesto because it does not fight the basil. The olive itself, cured in brine, goes into most of the region's stews: coniglio alla ligure, rabbit braised with olives, pine nuts and white wine; stoccafisso accomodato, Norwegian stockfish stewed with potatoes, olives and tomatoes, a dish Genoa's ships brought home from the north in the sixteenth century; and the whole tribe of vegetable pies, torta pasqualina with chard and eggs under thirty-three layers of pastry, one for each year of Christ's life, and pansoti, pasta parcels filled with wild greens and served with a walnut sauce.

Herbs and the garden.

What holds it together is the preboggion, the mixed wild herbs of the hills, borage, chard, dandelion, chervil and a dozen others, gathered in spring and folded into pies, pasta fillings and frittatas. Marjoram rather than oregano, pine nuts rather than cream, a sweet-and-sour touch in the cima alla genovese, a breast of veal stuffed with vegetables, eggs and cheese, boiled and sliced cold. It is a frugal, precise and vegetable-led cooking that has survived tourism better than most, because the terraces still grow the basil and the sea still delivers the anchovies.

Before you move on.

Pesto is Genoese basil DOP, garlic, pine nuts, two cheeses, oil and salt, pounded in a mortar and never cooked, served on trofie or trenette.

Focaccia genovese is dimpled, oily and eaten for breakfast; focaccia di Recco is thin dough around crescenza; farinata is a chickpea pancake from a copper pan.

The Taggiasca olive gives a mild, sweet oil and goes into rabbit and stockfish stews; wild herbs fill the pies and the pansoti.
