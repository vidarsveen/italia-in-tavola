Pesto, focaccia and the olive.

In March 2026 a hundred people sat at marble mortars in the great council hall of the Palazzo Ducale in Genoa, and each of them had forty minutes to make pesto by hand. A jury of thirty judged the sauces on their colour, their fineness, their consistency and the balance of their flavours, and on how each competitor had used the pestle. It was the eleventh World Pesto Championship, and the winner took home a golden pestle.

The basil is the reason the sauce belongs to Genoa. Basilico Genovese DOP, protected since 2005, may be grown anywhere along the Ligurian coast, and the best known comes from the greenhouses at Pra', west of the city. It is pulled up young, roots and all, when the plants are ten or twelve centimetres tall. The small, pale leaves are sweet, and they have none of the minty note that most basil carries. Pounding keeps that flavour whole. A pestle crushes the leaf against the stone and presses its aromatic oil out. A blade cuts the leaf instead, and the broken cells turn brown as soon as air reaches them, while the motor warms whatever it is cutting. That is what purists mean when they say a blender bruises the leaves and turns the sauce black.

The recipe has seven ingredients. Garlic and coarse salt go into the mortar first, then pine nuts. The basil follows a handful at a time, worked round the sides of the mortar with a wooden pestle until the leaves give up a green cream. Grated Parmigiano and an aged pecorino follow, and last comes Ligurian olive oil, stirred in until the sauce holds together. Nothing is cooked. The list of seven took a long time to settle. The first written recipe, in Giovanni Battista Ratto's La Cuciniera Genovese of 1863, has garlic, basil, Parmigiano and Dutch cheese, and no pine nuts at all.

Genoa puts pesto on three kinds of pasta. Trofie are small twisted curls from the Golfo Paradiso east of the city, and both Sori and Recco claim them. Trenette are flat ribbons, cooked in the same pot as potatoes and green beans. Mandilli de sæa, silk handkerchiefs, are sheets of egg pasta rolled very thin. Whichever it is, the sauce is loosened with a spoonful of the pasta water and never goes into a hot pan.

Genoa's bread is focaccia, fügassa in dialect. It is a flat slab of yeasted dough about a centimetre thick. Before it goes into the oven the baker presses dimples into it with the fingertips, and olive oil and salty brine fill them. It comes out golden, crisp underneath and soft inside. It is sold by weight from seven in the morning and eaten at every hour of the day, and many Genoese have it for breakfast, dipped in a cappuccino or a caffelatte.

A little way east along the coast, in Recco, the same word means something else. Focaccia di Recco has no yeast in it. Two sheets of dough are stretched until they are as thin as paper, fresh crescenza cheese goes between them, and it is baked in a very hot oven until the top blisters. It has had protected status since 2015, and it may be made only in Recco and three neighbouring towns. Recco tells two stories about where it came from. In one, a cheese focaccia was served in 1189 to crusaders setting out for the Holy Land. In the other, people from the coast fled inland from Saracen raids, traded their oil and salt with shepherds for cheese, and baked the bread on slabs of slate. Neither can be proved. The record starts with Manuelina, a trattoria that opened in Recco in 1885 and early in the twentieth century began baking it the way it is sold today.

Older than either focaccia is farinata. It is a batter of chickpea flour, water, oil and salt, poured into a wide copper pan and baked in a wood oven. The top comes out crisp and the inside as soft as custard, and it is sold by the slice and eaten hot with black pepper. In Genoa it comes from the sciamadde, shops named after the blaze of their wood-fired ovens. They have fed dockers and travellers since at least the eighteenth century with farinata, vegetable pies and fried salt cod. The Genoese have a legend that farinata was invented in 1284, after their fleet beat Pisa at Meloria. In a storm on the way home the chickpeas on the galleys were soaked with seawater, and the sailors ate the paste after the sun had dried it on deck. The oldest record usually cited is a decree of the fifteenth century, in which the city fixed the quality of the oil, the size of the pan and the price.

The olive of Liguria is the Taggiasca, named after the town of Taggia near Imperia. Monks are said to have planted it there in the Middle Ages, though the stories disagree about which monks and in which century. It is a small olive, dark when ripe. Its oil is pale gold, sweet and low in bitterness, with a taste of almond and ripe fruit, where the oils of Tuscany and Puglia are often bitter and peppery. Ligurian cooks use it generously, the way other kitchens use butter. It is the oil for pesto, because it is mild enough for the basil to be tasted, and a peppery oil would cover it. Cured in brine, the olives go whole into the stews. Coniglio alla ligure is rabbit braised with olives, pine nuts, herbs and white wine, and stoccafisso accomodato is stockfish stewed with potatoes and olives.

Ligurian cooking draws more on the garden and the hillside than on the sea. In spring cooks gather preboggion, a mix of wild herbs and greens that changes with the place and the week. Borage, chard, dandelion and chervil are usual, and a dozen other greens can go in. It is folded into pies, frittatas and pasta fillings. Pansoti, the region's own filled pasta, are stuffed with it and a fresh curd cheese, and they are served under a sauce of pounded walnuts. Torta pasqualina, the Easter pie of chard and eggs, traditionally has thirty-three sheets of pastry, one for each year of Christ's life. Across the region the herb is marjoram rather than oregano, and pine nuts and walnuts thicken what other kitchens would thicken with cream.

In cima alla genovese a breast of veal is sewn into a pocket and filled with veal, sweetbreads, brain and udder, eggs, peas, Parmigiano, pine nuts and marjoram. It is boiled for hours wrapped in a cloth, pressed under a weight, and served cold, cut into slices with the filling showing.
