Genoa: a maritime republic's kitchen.

In Italian markets the best grade of stockfish is called Ragno, which means spider, and the name has nothing to do with spiders. The story comes from the Genoese firm that owns the mark today. The finest stockfish came from a Norwegian company, Ragnar Riksheim, and it reached Italy through one importer in Genoa, run by Enrico Gismondi, which stamped each fish with the Ragnar mark. The people selling it could not say Ragnar, so they said the nearest Italian word. The word stuck, and it became the name of the top grade across Italy.

Genoa is squeezed between the mountains and its harbour, so it grew upwards and inwards. The old town is a maze of alleys called caruggi, where the houses almost touch overhead and the streets are too narrow for a car. Few historic centres in Europe are as densely lived in. From here the Republic of Genoa ran a trading empire from the end of the eleventh century until 1797, and its ships reached the Black Sea and North Africa. In 1358 Petrarch described Genoa as a royal city, proud in its men and its walls, and the name La Superba, the proud one, has stayed with it.

The city's symbol is a lighthouse. The Lanterna stands on a rock at the western edge of the harbour, 77 metres tall, and it is the tallest lighthouse in the Mediterranean. A tower has stood there since about 1128. The present one was rebuilt in 1543, after the Genoese had badly damaged the old one while besieging the French in the fortress beside it. A document of 1449 names an Antonio Colombo, probably an uncle of Christopher Columbus, as a keeper of the tower. The boy was born in the city two years later, and he learned the sea in the port below.

In 1550 the city decided to lay out a new street above the old town. Over the following decades its great families, the Doria, the Spinola and the Grimaldi among them, lined the Strada Nuova, now Via Garibaldi, with large, richly frescoed palaces. The republic had no royal palace in which to lodge a visiting king, an ambassador or a cardinal. So from 1576 it kept lists of the finest private houses, the rolli, and their owners had to take in the state's guests in turn.

Below the palaces the caruggi begin, with Via San Luca and Piazza Banchi, where the money-changers sat. The cathedral of San Lorenzo is striped in black and white marble. In its right aisle is a replica of a British naval shell that came through the roof on 9 February 1941 and did not explode. In Piazza Soziglia two old shops still sell sweets. Pietro Romanengo was founded in 1780 by a spice merchant, and it still makes candied fruit, sugared almonds and candied violets. Klainguti was opened in 1828 by Swiss confectioners from Pontresina, whose journey had been held up in Genoa. The shop later made a brioche called the Falstaff for Giuseppe Verdi, who had written an opera of that name. His note of thanks hangs framed above the counter: superbo, molto meglio del mio, superb, much better than mine.

The stockfish Italy buys is cod dried hard in the wind of northern Norway, and Italy took to it because of the Church. After the Council of Trent tightened the rules on fasting in the sixteenth century, a Catholic household went without meat on many days of the year. A dried fish that kept for a long time and travelled well suited those days. The first commercial cargoes on record went to Venice in 1596, brought from Bergen on Dutch, Danish and German ships, and in the same centuries the fish reached the Republic of Genoa too. The city still cooks it as stoccafisso accomodato, soaked and then stewed with potatoes, olives, pine nuts and often tomato. Italy now buys more than seven tenths of the stockfish Norway sells abroad.

The fasting days also produced the grandest dish of the old Genoese table. Cappon magro has no meat in it at all, which is what magro, lean, means on the Church's calendar. It starts with ship's biscuit softened in water and vinegar. Boiled vegetables and fish go on top in layer after layer, built up into a tall pyramid. The whole is covered in a green sauce of parsley, capers and anchovies and decorated with lobster and prawns. Ratto's cookbook of 1863 called it, when well made, the best salad there is. Nobody is sure where the capon in the name comes from. The linguist Fiorenzo Toso links it to cappone, a local name for the red scorpionfish, and others trace it to pieces of biscuit rubbed with garlic.

The everyday food of the city is quicker to eat. Focaccia comes from the bakery at eight in the morning, and farinata at noon from a sciamadda, one of the wood-oven shops that bake it. There is fried fish in a paper cone from a friggitoria, and a plate of trofie al pesto. The Genoese took that food with them when they emigrated. In Buenos Aires they settled the harbour district of La Boca, whose football club is still nicknamed the Xeneizes, from the dialect word for Genoese. There farinata became fainá, which is eaten on top of a slice of pizza, and focaccia with onions became fugazza. In Gibraltar, where the census of 1725 counted 414 Genoese among 1,113 civilians, the chickpea batter became calentita, which Gibraltar now calls its national dish.

At Christmas the Genoese table has a pandolce, a dense round loaf of raisins, pine nuts, candied citron and fennel seed, scented with orange-flower water. The youngest at the table sets a sprig of bay or olive on top of it, and the eldest cuts the first slice. Two slices are put aside. One is for the poor person who comes to the door, and the other is kept for the feast of San Biagio in February, when it is eaten against a sore throat.
