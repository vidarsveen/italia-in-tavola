Genoa: a maritime republic's kitchen.

For seven hundred years Genoa was a republic that ran on banking, ships and salt cod. Its lighthouse, its street of palaces, its old town of alleys, and the food that its sailors carried around the world and brought back.

Genoa is a city that turns its back on Italy and its face to the sea. Squeezed between the mountains and the harbour, it grew upwards and inwards, into the densest medieval old town in Europe, a labyrinth of alleys called caruggi where the buildings almost touch overhead and the streets are too narrow for a car. From here, from the eleventh century to 1797, the Republic of Genoa ran a trading empire that reached the Black Sea and North Africa, invented much of modern banking, and produced Christopher Columbus and Andrea Doria. Petrarch called it La Superba, the proud one, and the Genoese have never seen a reason to disagree.

The Lanterna and the port.

The city's symbol is a lighthouse. The Lanterna stands on a rock at the western edge of the harbour, 77 metres tall, one of the oldest working lighthouses in the world; there has been a light here since the twelfth century, and the present tower was rebuilt in 1543 after the Genoese had knocked the old one about during a siege. Its keeper for a time was Antonio Colombo, the uncle of the boy Cristoforo, born in the city in 1451, who learned the sea in the port below. The port is still the largest in Italy, a wall of cranes and containers running west for miles, and the medieval waterfront in front of the old town, the Porto Antico, was rebuilt in 1992, for the five-hundredth anniversary of Columbus's voyage, by the city's own Renzo Piano: the aquarium, Italy's largest, the glass Biosfera, and the Bigo, a crane-like lift that turns slowly over the water to give the view.

Key facts.

Republic of Genoa, 11th century to 1797; the Bank of Saint George, founded 1407, was one of the first banks in the world. Christopher Columbus was born here in 1451.

The Lanterna: 77 m, rebuilt 1543, still in service. The Palazzi dei Rolli, forty-two palaces on the Strade Nuove, were listed by UNESCO in 2006; in 1576 the republic drew up rolls of the palaces obliged to house visiting heads of state.

The Porto Antico was redesigned by Renzo Piano in 1992; the aquarium is Italy's largest. The old town of caruggi is one of the largest medieval centres in Europe.

Dishes of Genoa: focaccia, farinata, pesto, cappon magro, stoccafisso, cima, pandolce, and the candied fruit and rose syrup of its old shops.

The street of palaces.

In the 1550s the republic's great families, the Doria, Spinola, Grimaldi and Pallavicini, built themselves a new street above the old town, the Strada Nuova, now Via Garibaldi, and lined it with palaces so large and so richly frescoed that the republic, which had no royal palace, made a list of them, the rolli, and obliged the owners to put up visiting kings, ambassadors and cardinals in turn. Rubens drew them and published a book of their plans in Antwerp. The Palazzi dei Rolli, forty-two of them on Via Garibaldi and Via Balbi, were listed by UNESCO in 2006, and three of them, the Palazzo Rosso, Palazzo Bianco and Palazzo Tursi, are now the city's museums, with Van Dyck and Caravaggio on the walls and a view over the roofs to the port. Below them the caruggi begin: Via San Luca, Piazza Banchi where the money-changers sat, the cathedral of San Lorenzo in black and white stripes with a British shell from 1941 that failed to explode still on display in the nave, and the ancient food shops, drogherie and friggitorie, that have sold the same things for a century and a half.

What Genoa eats.

The republic's kitchen was a ship's kitchen. Genoese galleys carried salt, dried fish and pasta, and Genoese merchants brought back stockfish from Norway and Iceland, which the city still eats stewed with potatoes and olives more than any Italian city but Venice. The grandest dish of the old table is cappon magro, "lean capon", a joke of a name for a towering Lenten pyramid of ship's biscuit soaked in vinegar, layered with boiled vegetables, fish and shellfish, and covered in a green sauce of parsley, capers and anchovies, decorated with lobster and prawns; it was the feast day dish of a city that ate fish because it had to. Around it are the everyday things: focaccia from the bakery at eight in the morning, farinata from the sciamadda at noon, fried fish in a paper cone from the friggitoria, a plate of trofie al pesto, and at Christmas pandolce, a dense round loaf of candied fruit, pine nuts and fennel seed that every Genoese family claims to make better than the shops.

The city sent its food abroad with its emigrants. In Buenos Aires, where the Genoese settled the district of La Boca, fainá is chickpea farinata served on top of pizza, and fugazza is focaccia with onions; in Gibraltar and Marseille the Genoese left their bakeries. The republic's last heir is the region's own: in 2018 the Morandi motorway bridge across the city collapsed and killed forty-three people, and Renzo Piano, who had built the port, designed the new bridge, opened two years later, as a ship's hull crossing the valley. Genoa builds, sails, trades and eats, and it does all of it with its back to the mountains.

Before you move on.

Genoa was a maritime republic for seven centuries; the Lanterna of 1543 is its symbol and the Bank of Saint George its invention.

The Palazzi dei Rolli on Via Garibaldi, listed by UNESCO in 2006, housed the republic's state guests; the caruggi below are Europe's densest old town.

The city's table is stockfish, cappon magro, focaccia, farinata and pesto, and its emigrants carried fainá and fugazza to Buenos Aires.
