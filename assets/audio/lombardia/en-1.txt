Franciacorta: Italy's answer to Champagne.

South of Lake Iseo, a sixty-year-old sparkling wine region makes Italy's most serious bottle-fermented wines. What the rules say, what Satèn means, and how it differs from Champagne.

Franciacorta is a small amphitheatre of hills between Brescia and the southern shore of Lake Iseo, and it is the only place in Italy where an entire appellation is dedicated to sparkling wine made the way Champagne is made: a second fermentation in the bottle, long months on the lees, and nothing else allowed. The region is young as Italian wine goes. The first bottles were made in 1961, the DOC came in 1967, and in 1995 it became the first Italian sparkling wine to be promoted to DOCG. Today it covers some 3,000 hectares and sells around twenty million bottles a year, most of them in Italy.

A glacier's leftovers.

The hills are a moraine, the rubble left by the glacier that carved Lake Iseo. The soil is a loose mix of gravel, sand and stones over limestone, poor in nutrients and quick to drain, which forces the vines to work and keeps yields low. The lake moderates the temperature, and the Alps to the north block the worst of the cold. The name has nothing to do with France: franchae curtes were the tax-exempt monastic courts of the Middle Ages, and the monks of Cluny were the first to drain and plant the land.

The modern story starts in 1961 in the cellar of Guido Berlucchi at Borgonato, where a young oenologist, Franco Ziliani, persuaded his employer to try making a bottle-fermented sparkling wine from Pinot grapes. Three thousand bottles of Pinot di Franciacorta followed. Ca' del Bosco, founded in 1968 and led by Maurizio Zanella, and Bellavista, founded by Vittorio Moretti in 1977, turned the experiment into an industry. In 1990 the producers set up a consortium and wrote rules stricter than most of Champagne's.

Key facts.

Grapes: Chardonnay, Pinot Nero and Pinot Bianco, plus up to 10% of the local white Erbamat, added to the rules to keep acidity in warm years.

Minimum time on the lees: 18 months for Franciacorta, 24 for Satèn and Rosé, 30 for a vintage (Millesimato), 60 for a Riserva. Release is allowed 25, 31, 37 and 67 months after harvest respectively.

Satèn is white grapes only, brut only, and bottled at a maximum of five atmospheres instead of six, which gives its softer, creamier mousse.

Rosé must contain at least 35% Pinot Nero. Grapes are hand-picked, and the second fermentation may only happen in the bottle.

Not Champagne, and not trying to be.

The method is the same, but the place is not. Franciacorta is warmer and sunnier than Champagne, the harvest starts in August, and the grapes reach full ripeness rather than the razor acidity of the north. The result is a wine with more fruit and body, ripe apple and white peach rather than green apple, a softer acidity and often a lower dosage, because the wine does not need sugar to be balanced. Many of the best cuvées are dosaggio zero or extra brut. Where Champagne is made mostly by big houses blending grapes bought from thousands of growers, Franciacorta is made mostly by estates growing their own fruit, which explains the single-vineyard and single-vintage bottlings that have become the region's signature.

The Satèn style is Franciacorta's own invention, and the name is a trademark of the consortium. A blend of Chardonnay and Pinot Bianco with no red grapes, bottled at lower pressure, it is silky rather than sharp, and it was designed to be drunk with food rather than as an aperitif. It is also the style most people in Brescia order without thinking.

In the glass: Franciacorta Brut.

 Colour: Pale straw with green glints, a fine persistent bead.

 Nose: Ripe apple, white peach, bread crust, hazelnut, a touch of citrus.

 Palate: Dry, creamy mousse, medium acidity, rounder and more fruit-driven than Champagne, long yeasty finish.

 Alcohol: 12–13%.

 Serve: 6–8 °C in a white-wine glass rather than a flute; most bottles are best within five years of disgorgement.

 At the table: Risotto, lake fish, fried food, prosciutto, and the whole of a meal if you choose a Satèn or a Riserva.

Reading the label.

The word Franciacorta on its own is the appellation, and by law it does not need the word spumante. Millesimato means at least 85% of the wine is from the stated vintage. Riserva is always a vintage wine, and its five years on the lees put it in the company of prestige Champagne. The dosage scale runs from dosaggio zero and extra brut through brut, extra dry and sec to demi-sec; almost everything worth drinking is in the first three. The back label usually gives the disgorgement date, which matters more than the vintage for a wine that starts to change the moment the yeast is removed.

Franciacorta is expensive to make and is priced accordingly, above Prosecco and Trentodoc and close to entry-level Champagne. What you pay for is a wine that has spent years in a cellar rather than weeks in a tank, and it shows on the palate as depth and length rather than as bubbles.

Before you move on.

Franciacorta is Italy's bottle-fermented sparkling DOCG, made from Chardonnay, Pinot Nero and Pinot Bianco on glacial moraine south of Lake Iseo.

Minimum lees ageing is 18 months, rising to 60 for a Riserva; Satèn is white grapes at lower pressure, softer and creamier.

Warmer than Champagne, it gives riper fruit, softer acidity and lower dosage, and is mostly made by estates from their own grapes.
