Franciacorta: Italy's answer to Champagne.

In 1955 Guido Berlucchi was unhappy with his wine. His house was Palazzo Lana, in Borgonato, south of Lake Iseo. There he made a still white from Pinot grapes, Pinot del Castello, and it came out rough and cloudy. He hired a young winemaker to steady it. Franco Ziliani was twenty-four and had just finished at the school of oenology in Alba. Ziliani proposed making the wine sparkle instead, the way the French did it, with a second fermentation inside the bottle. The first attempts failed. In 1961 three thousand bottles of Pinot di Franciacorta were sealed, and when they were opened the next year the wine was excellent.

Franciacorta is a small amphitheatre of hills between Brescia and the southern shore of the lake. The hills are a moraine. The great glacier that came down the Valcamonica pushed out and pulled back over this ground, and it left behind gravel, sand and stones carried from elsewhere. The soils it made are fairly deep and drain well. The lake softens the temperature, and the Alps to the north keep off the worst of the cold. Monks from Cluny were given the church at Provaglio in 1083, and the priory that grew there took the name Lamosa from the marshes, the lame, that they set about draining. The vine was older than the monks, and grape pips from prehistory have been found at Provaglio.

Other houses followed Berlucchi, and the DOC of 1967 covered still wines as well as sparkling ones. In 1964 Annamaria Clementi Zanella bought a house in the hills called Ca' del Bosco, and vines were planted there in 1968. Her son Maurizio, who runs it, went to Champagne and came back to make sparkling wine. Vittorio Moretti founded Bellavista in 1977. In 1990 twenty-nine producers formed a consortium, and in 1995 Franciacorta became the first Italian wine made by fermentation in the bottle to be raised to DOCG. The DOCG was kept for the sparkling wine alone, and the still wines went into a separate DOC, now called Curtefranca. Today some 3,400 hectares of vines, four fifths of them Chardonnay, give nearly nineteen million bottles a year, and almost nine in ten are drunk in Italy.

For its gentlest style Franciacorta first used the French word crémant. In 1989 a European rule kept that word for France and Luxembourg, so the producers needed a name of their own. They chose Satèn, which comes from the word for satin, and the consortium registered it as a trademark in 1995. Who thought of the name is still disputed. A Satèn is made from white grapes only, and no more than twenty grams of sugar a litre may be added before its second fermentation. The sugar limits how much gas the yeast can make, so the wine ends with at most five atmospheres of pressure. Its mousse is softer and creamier, and a Satèn is always brut.

The method is Champagne's, and the climate is not. Franciacorta is warmer and sunnier, so the grapes ripen fully instead of keeping the sharp acidity of the north. The wine has more fruit and more body, with ripe apple and white peach where Champagne has green apple, and its acidity is softer. Because the fruit is already ripe, the wine needs less sugar at the end to taste balanced, and many producers make it extra brut or with no dosage at all. The wine then rests on its yeast for a long time, and the back label usually gives the date the yeast was removed. A wine like this starts to change from that day, so the date matters as much as the vintage.

The warmth has been growing. In 2017 the rules admitted Erbamat, an old local white grape first described by Agostino Gallo in 1564. It ripens late and holds on to its acidity, and the growers planted it against the heat. The harvest used to begin in August. In 2026 the picking began on 30 July, in the vineyards of Gussago and Monte Orfano, and the consortium said it was the first time the Franciacorta harvest had ever started in July.
