Nebbiolo in the Alps: Valtellina.

On the sunny wall of an Alpine valley, Nebbiolo grows on terraces held up by 2,500 kilometres of dry-stone walls. The five sub-zones, the dried-grape Sforzato, and the food that goes with them.

The Valtellina is the valley of the river Adda, running east to west just south of the Swiss border, and it is one of the few places in the world where a great grape is grown on a mountainside. The north wall of the valley faces south, catches the sun from morning to evening, and has been cut into terraces since the Middle Ages. On those terraces grows Nebbiolo, the grape of Barolo, and here it is called Chiavennasca. The wines are lighter and more perfumed than Piedmont's, the vineyards are steeper than anything in Barolo, and every kilo of grapes is carried down by hand or by cable.

A wall of vines.

The terraces climb from about 300 to 800 metres, propped by an estimated 2,500 kilometres of dry-stone walls, which is why the landscape is a candidate for UNESCO recognition. The stone stores the day's heat and releases it at night, the slope drains instantly, and the mountains shield the vines from rain. Without the walls there would be no vineyard; with them, the growers of the valley have one of the most labour-intensive wine landscapes in Europe. Work that takes a hundred hours per hectare on the plain takes over a thousand here, which is why the valley speaks of viticoltura eroica, heroic viticulture.

The name Chiavennasca is usually traced to Chiavenna, the town at the head of the valley, though the growers prefer the dialect reading ciù venasca, "the most wine-like". Whatever the origin, the grape is Nebbiolo, and it is late-ripening, high in acid and tannin, and demanding about where it is planted. In the Valtellina it ripens later than in the Langhe and often only just, which makes altitude and exposure decisive, and the best sites are the warm central band of the valley around Sondrio.

Key facts.

Valtellina Superiore DOCG (1998): at least 90% Nebbiolo, 12% alcohol minimum, 24 months of ageing of which 12 in wood; Riserva 36 months.

Five named sub-zones, west to east: Maroggia, Sassella, Grumello, Inferno and Valgella. Inferno is the steepest and hottest, Maroggia the smallest, Valgella the largest, Sassella the most famous.

Sforzato di Valtellina DOCG (2003): Nebbiolo dried for about three months before pressing, minimum 14% alcohol, a dry wine. It was the first dried-grape dry red in Italy to reach DOCG, ahead of Amarone.

Rosso di Valtellina DOC is the lighter, earlier-drinking wine from the same grape, with six months of ageing.

Five names on the mountain.

The sub-zones are named after landmarks rather than villages. Sassella takes its name from the rock-perched sanctuary of the Madonna della Sassella just west of Sondrio, and gives the most elegant and longest-lived wines. Grumello sits below the ruins of Castel Grumello and is a little rounder. Inferno is a cluster of steep, rocky pockets that trap the heat, hence the name, and produces the ripest wines. Valgella, around Teglio and Chiuro, is the largest and the freshest. Maroggia, added in 2002, is a single steep slope near Berbenno. The names have appeared on bottles since the nineteenth century, when the valley's wine went north over the passes to Switzerland and Austria, its main customers for centuries.

The producers to know are few. Nino Negri, founded in 1897 and the valley's largest house, made Sforzato famous with its 5 Stelle; Ar.Pe.Pe., the Pelizzatti Perego family, ages its Sassella and Grumello for years before release and is the reference for the traditional style; Rainoldi, Sandro Fay and Mamete Prevostini round out the list. Total production is small, a fraction of Barolo's, and most of it is drunk in Lombardy and Switzerland.

In the glass: Valtellina Superiore.

 Colour: Pale garnet, lighter than Barolo, orange at the rim with age.

 Nose: Dried rose, red cherry, alpine herbs, tobacco, a stony, almost graphite note.

 Palate: Medium body, high acidity, firm but fine tannin, a long dry finish that stays fresh.

 Alcohol: 12.5–13.5%; Sforzato 14–15%.

 Serve: 16–18 °C; Superiore drinks well from five years and keeps fifteen, Sforzato longer.

 At the table: Pizzoccheri, bresaola, polenta with cheese, game, braised beef; Sforzato with aged Bitto.

Sforzato, the dried-grape red.

Sforzato, or Sfursat in dialect, means "forced". The best bunches are picked and laid on racks in airy lofts, the fruttai, where they lose about a third of their weight over the autumn. Pressed around December, the concentrated must ferments to a dry wine of at least 14% alcohol. It is the valley's answer to Amarone, but made from Nebbiolo rather than Corvina, and so it keeps acidity and structure under the richness: dried cherry, plum, spice and leather, with the tannin to age for twenty years. It was born as a way to ripen a grape that the mountain does not always ripen, and it has become the valley's most valuable wine.

The valley's table.

Valtellina cooking is mountain food built on buckwheat, butter and cheese. Pizzoccheri are short, thick ribbons of buckwheat pasta cooked with potato and cabbage or chard, layered with Valtellina Casera cheese and finished with butter, garlic and sage; the town of Teglio has an academy to defend the recipe. Sciatt are fritters of buckwheat batter with a heart of melting cheese, and polenta taragna mixes buckwheat and corn with cheese stirred in. Bresaola della Valtellina, salted and air-dried beef, is sliced paper-thin with lemon and oil. Bitto, made only in summer on the high pastures from cow's milk with a share of goat's, is aged for years and is one of Italy's rarest cheeses. All of it wants a glass of Nebbiolo with acid enough to cut the butter.

Before you move on.

Valtellina grows Nebbiolo, called Chiavennasca, on south-facing terraces held by 2,500 km of dry-stone walls at 300–800 metres.

Valtellina Superiore DOCG has five sub-zones, Sassella, Grumello, Inferno, Valgella and Maroggia, and needs two years of ageing.

Sforzato is a dry red from dried Nebbiolo at 14% or more, Amarone's alpine cousin, and the valley's most prized wine.
