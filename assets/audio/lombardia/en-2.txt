Nebbiolo in the Alps: Valtellina.

Two villages just over the Swiss border, Brusio and Poschiavo, still bottle wine from the Valtellina under the valley's Italian appellation, with a label word of their own, Stagafassli. The Swiss connection is much older than the appellation. From 1512 to 1797 the Valtellina belonged to the Grisons, the Three Leagues on the Swiss side of the Alps. In the late eighteenth century the valley made about 75,000 hectolitres of wine a year, and between 45,000 and 53,000 of them went north. The wine crossed the Splügen and Bernina passes to the Grisons, to the Tyrol and to southern Germany. There it was called Veltliner, a name it shares with Austria's Grüner Veltliner and nothing else.

Families from the Grisons bought many of the vineyards they were selling from, and by one historian's count the Salis owned more than a fifth of the best of them. In 1797 Napoleon joined the valley to the Cisalpine Republic, and the property of the Grisons families was sold at public auction, mostly to rich families of the valley. The trade went on for most of another century. After 1882 the railway through the Gotthard took the transit trade away from the passes. From more than 6,000 hectares in the middle of the nineteenth century, the vineyards have shrunk to about 750.

The Valtellina is the valley of the river Adda, and it runs from east to west just south of the Swiss border. Its northern side therefore faces south and takes the sun from morning to evening, and that is where the vines are. The slope has been cut into terraces since the Middle Ages. They climb from about 300 to 800 metres, held up by more than 2,500 kilometres of dry-stone walls. The stone stores the heat of the day and gives it back at night, the slope drains at once, and the mountains keep off much of the rain. Most of the work is done by hand. The grapes come down in baskets, on small tracked carriers, or by helicopter from the steepest places. The president of the consortium has said that the helicopter costs about the same as carrying by hand. One producer reckons a hectare here takes about 1,200 hours of work a year, against 300 or 400 on flatter land, and the valley counts among the most labour-intensive wine landscapes in Europe. That is why it speaks of viticoltura eroica, heroic viticulture.

The grape on the terraces is Nebbiolo, the grape of Barolo, and here it is called Chiavennasca. The name may come from the Val Chiavenna nearby, or from the dialect ciu-vinasca, the vine best suited to wine. Nebbiolo ripens late, is high in acid and tannin, and is demanding about where it is planted. In the Valtellina it ripens later than in the Langhe and often only just, so the altitude and exposure of a vineyard decide whether it ripens at all. The best sites are in the warm middle of the valley around Sondrio. The wines are lighter and more perfumed than Piedmont's.

Five sub-zones may add their names to Valtellina Superiore. Sassella gives the most elegant and longest-lived wines, and it takes its name from the sanctuary of the Madonna della Sassella on its rock west of Sondrio. Below the ruins of a castle, Grumello gives rounder ones. Inferno is a cluster of steep, rocky pockets, and its wines are the ripest; the valley says it was named for the heat the pockets trap. Arturo Pelizzatti Perego made his name with Sassella and Grumello. His family had traded wine since 1860 and sold the business in 1973. In 1984 he started again under his initials, Ar.Pe.Pe., and he released his wines years after the harvest, and only in the years he thought deserved it.

A grape that the mountain does not always ripen can be helped after it is picked. For Sforzato, or Sfursat in dialect, the best bunches are laid in small crates or on racks in airy lofts called fruttai. They dry there until December, about a hundred days, and lose a third or more of their weight. The water goes and the sugar stays. The concentrated must then ferments until it is dry, at no less than fourteen per cent of alcohol. The method is close to Amarone's, but the grape is Nebbiolo rather than Corvina, so the wine keeps its acidity and tannin under the richness. It tastes of dried cherry, plum, spice and leather, and it can age for twenty years. It is named for the practice of sforzare, forcing the grapes. Nino Negri, the house founded at Chiuro in 1897, made Sforzato famous with its 5 Stelle, first made by its winemaker Casimiro Maule from the 1983 harvest.

Valtellina cooking is mountain food made from buckwheat, butter and cheese. Pizzoccheri are short, thick ribbons of buckwheat pasta, cooked with potato and cabbage or chard. They are layered with Valtellina Casera cheese and finished with butter, garlic and sage, and the town of Teglio has an academy to defend the recipe. The same buckwheat is made into a batter and fried around melting cheese as sciatt, and mixed with maize it becomes polenta taragna. Bresaola della Valtellina, beef salted and dried in the air, is sliced very thin and eaten with lemon and oil. Food this rich wants a wine with acidity, and Nebbiolo has plenty.

The cheese that goes with a Sforzato is Bitto. It is made only in summer, on the high pastures, from cow's milk with a little goat's milk, and the old wheels keep for years. When Bitto became a protected cheese in the 1990s, the rules allowed feed for the cattle and industrial starter cultures, and they made the goat's milk optional. A group of traditional cheesemakers led by Paolo Ciapparelli refused them. After years of dispute they gave up the name in 2016, and their cheese is now sold as Storico Ribelle, the historic rebel.
