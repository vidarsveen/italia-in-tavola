Saffron risotto and the Milanese table.

Every spring, until machines took over the work in the 1960s, special trains brought women to the station at Mortara, in the rice country south-west of Milan. They came from Emilia, from the Veneto and from the villages of Lombardy, and at the station they were sent on to the farms. For about forty days they stood barefoot in the flooded paddies, bent double and knee-deep in water, and pulled the weeds out of the rice by hand. They were called mondine, after the monda, the weeding. They slept on straw in shared rooms at the farmhouses, and part of their pay was a few sacks of rice.

In 1906 the mondine of Vercelli won a working day of eight hours. Their song for it told the employers that if eight hours seemed few, they should try working them. The fields are still there, flooded every spring through canals that were begun in the Middle Ages. Rice was already established on this plain in 1475, when Galeazzo Maria Sforza, Duke of Milan, sent twelve sacks of seed rice as a gift to the Duke of Ferrara. Today more than nine tenths of Italy's rice comes from Lombardy and Piedmont, and the province of Pavia, with the Lomellina inside it, is the largest rice district in Europe.

The rice a Milanese cook wants for risotto is Carnaroli. Ettore de Vecchi bred it in his paddies at Paullo, south-east of Milan, and finished in 1945. Its grain is the firmest of the risotto rices and gives up its starch slowly, so the risotto turns creamy while each grain stays whole. Arborio, named after a village near Vercelli, is the everyday grain.

South of Milan, around the abbey of Chiaravalle, Cistercian monks perfected a way of keeping grass growing through the winter. They let a thin sheet of running water from the canals flow over the meadows all winter, and under it the grass kept growing. These meadows are called marcite. The best of them were mown eight or nine times a year, four or five times in winter, and the first fresh grass was cut at the end of February. The grass fed the cattle, and the milk yields of the plain were among the highest in Europe. Lombardy still produces nearly half of Italy's milk. Its kitchen cooks with butter where the south of Italy uses olive oil, and its dairies make fourteen cheeses with protected origin, more than any other region. The consortium of Grana Padano dates its cheese to the monks of Chiaravalle and the year 1135. The historian Massimo Montanari, whom the consortium's own foundation quotes, warns that origins of that kind are often a myth.

Milan's yellow rice first appears in print in a cookbook of 1809, Il cuoco moderno. There it is called riso giallo in padella: rice cooked in butter with onion, beef marrow and cervellata, a Milanese pork sausage, then finished with broth coloured with saffron. The name risotto alla milanese came twenty years later, in Giovanni Felice Luraschi's Nuovo cuoco milanese economico of 1829. Today's recipe leaves out the sausage and keeps the rest. The rice is toasted in butter with onion and marrow, the stock goes in a ladle at a time, and the saffron goes in near the end. Then the pan comes off the heat, and cold butter and grated cheese are beaten in. That is the mantecatura, and it leaves the risotto all'onda, loose enough to move in a wave when the plate is tilted.

Milan prefers an older story. In 1574, it goes, a Flemish glass painter called Valerio was working on the windows of the Duomo, and his apprentice was nicknamed Zafferano because he mixed saffron into his colours. At the wedding of his master's daughter the apprentice put saffron in the rice as a joke. The retellings say the story comes from a manuscript in the city's Trivulziana library, and none of them says which.

In Milan the risotto is often served under an ossobuco, a slice of veal shin three or four centimetres thick. It is cut across the bone, so each slice has a ring of marrow in the middle. The slices are braised slowly in butter until the meat comes away from the bone, and they are finished with gremolata, parsley, garlic and lemon zest chopped together. The rice and the meat make a single dish.

The cotoletta alla milanese is a veal chop on the bone, dipped in egg and breadcrumbs and fried in clarified butter until the crust is gold and the meat by the bone is still pink. In dialect it is the costoletta, the rib chop, and the bone belongs to the dish. A boneless cutlet beaten thin and fried until it hangs over the plate, the orecchia d'elefante or elephant's ear, is a recent restaurant version that purists disapprove of. Milan and Vienna both claim the idea, and neither has the document it needs. Milan points to a banquet of the canons of Sant'Ambrogio in 1134, which Pietro Verri described in his history of Milan in 1783. One course was lombolos cum panitio, which is usually read as loin in breadcrumbs. The food historian Pierangelo Frigerio reads panitio as a side dish instead, perhaps a polenta of millet.

Vienna's version was put in the mouth of Field Marshal Radetzky. He is supposed to have written from Milan to a count at the imperial court, praising a breaded veal cutlet, and the Wiener Schnitzel is supposed to have followed. The Austrian linguist Heinz-Dieter Pohl traced that letter to Italian food writing of the 1960s, and no one has found it in an archive. In Vienna breaded veal was being fried in lard in the eighteenth century, and the name Wiener Schnitzel first appears in a cookbook of 1831.

Milan's Christmas bread is older than its name. On 23 December 1599 the Collegio Borromeo in Pavia entered in its accounts what it had bought for the pani grossi, the large loaves its students were given on Christmas Day. Pietro Verri, two centuries later, wrote about the old Milanese custom of a large bread at Christmas. The first printed recipe for panettone is Luraschi's again, in the edition of 1853. The shape came in the twentieth century. In 1919 the Milanese baker Angelo Motta let the dough rise three times over about twenty hours, and his panettone came out as a tall dome. Gioacchino Alemagna founded a rival brand in the 1920s, and between them the two firms turned a city's Christmas bread into a product of factories.

In Milan the leftovers of the boiled meat become mondeghili, meatballs bound with bread soaked in milk and fried in butter. Their name comes from the Spanish albondiguilla, a little meatball, and probably dates from the years when Spain ruled Milan. Cremona eats the same boiled meat with mostarda, fruit candied in a syrup hot with mustard, and Mantua folds that mostarda with pumpkin and amaretti into its tortelli. Through the winter there is polenta on every table of the plain.

In the middle of January comes cassoeula. It is eaten around the feast of Sant'Antonio on the 17th, which used to mark the end of the season for killing pigs. It is made from the parts of the pig that were left once the good cuts had gone: the rind, the trotters, the ears and the ribs, with small sausages called verzini. They cook for hours with a savoy cabbage that has been left standing in the field until the first frost.
