Saffron risotto and the Milanese table.

Rice from the Po plain, butter instead of oil, and saffron from a stained-glass legend. Risotto, ossobuco, cotoletta, panettone and the cheeses of a region that has more of them than any other.

Lombardy cooks with butter. That single fact separates it from the olive-oil south and explains most of what follows: rice cooked slowly in fat and stock, veal fried in butter, cheeses that need cold cellars and long winters, and a Christmas bread so rich in butter and egg that it takes a day to rise. The region is Italy's richest and its most industrial, and its food was shaped by the wet plain of the Po, by the Spanish and Austrian courts that ruled Milan for four centuries, and by a middle class that could afford veal and saffron.

Rice, and why it is here.

Italy grows more rice than any other country in Europe, and nearly all of it is on the flat, canal-fed plain shared by Lombardy and Piedmont. The province of Pavia, and its southern corner the Lomellina, is the biggest rice district in the country. The Sforza dukes were promoting rice by the 1470s, and the network of canals begun in the Middle Ages, some of them with locks that Leonardo da Vinci worked on, made it possible to flood the fields every spring. The varieties matter. Carnaroli, bred in 1945, has the firmest grain and releases starch slowly, which makes it the cook's choice for risotto; Vialone Nano from the Veneto side is smaller and creamier; Arborio is the everyday grain.

Risotto alla milanese.

The city's dish is risotto giallo, yellow with saffron. The legend attaches it to the Duomo: in 1574 a Flemish glass painter working on the cathedral windows, Valerio di Fiandra, had an apprentice nicknamed Zafferano for his habit of adding saffron to his pigments, and at the master's daughter's wedding the apprentice, as a joke, put it in the rice. The first printed recipe is from 1809, and it already has the elements that define the dish: rice toasted in butter with onion, stock added a ladle at a time, bone marrow for richness, saffron near the end and grated cheese to finish. The texture should be all'onda, a wave that moves when the plate is tilted. In Milan it is the traditional partner of ossobuco, veal shin braised on the bone until the marrow can be spooned out, finished with gremolata of parsley, garlic and lemon zest, and the two are served together as a single course.

Key facts.

Lombardy has more DOP cheeses than any other Italian region, among them Grana Padano, Gorgonzola, Taleggio, Bitto, Quartirolo and Provolone Valpadana.

Grana Padano was first made by Cistercian monks at the abbey of Chiaravalle, south of Milan, in the twelfth century, as a way of storing surplus milk.

Gorgonzola takes its name from a town east of Milan and comes in two styles, dolce (soft, mild) and piccante (firmer, sharper). Most of it is now made across the border in Piedmont.

Panettone is documented in Pavia in 1599; Angelo Motta gave it its tall dome in 1919, and the rival Alemagna firm followed six years later.

Cotoletta, cassoeula and the winter table.

Cotoletta alla milanese is a veal rib chop on the bone, dipped in egg and breadcrumbs and fried in clarified butter until the crust is gold and the meat still pink. A document of 1134 from the canons of Sant'Ambrogio lists lombolos cum panitio, loin with breadcrumbs, and Milan takes that as its birth certificate. Vienna claims the schnitzel was born there and that Field Marshal Radetzky merely brought the Milanese version home; Milan says the opposite. The modern orecchia d'elefante, a boneless cutlet beaten thin and fried until it hangs over the plate, is a restaurant invention of the last decades and purists disapprove.

Winter brings cassoeula, a stew of pork ribs, rind, trotters and small sausages with savoy cabbage picked after the first frost, eaten around Sant'Antonio on 17 January; mondeghili, meatballs made from the leftovers of boiled meat; and polenta everywhere. Each city of the plain has its own dish: Bergamo its casoncelli, stuffed pasta with butter and sage; Mantova its tortelli filled with pumpkin, amaretti and mostarda; Cremona its mostarda of candied fruit in mustard syrup, served with boiled meats, and the nougat called torrone, said to have been invented for a Visconti–Sforza wedding in 1441.

Cheese and panettone.

The dairies of the plain and the Alpine valleys produce a third of Italy's milk, and the cheeses follow. Grana Padano is the cousin of Parmigiano, made over a wider area with fewer restrictions and aged from nine months; Taleggio from the Bergamo valleys is a washed-rind square, soft and pungent; Gorgonzola is Italy's blue, eaten with walnuts, honey or a spoon; Bitto is the rare Alpine cheese of the Valtellina. At Christmas the region eats panettone, a naturally leavened dome of butter, egg yolk, candied orange and raisins that the pastry chefs of Milan have turned into an art form and the industrial bakers into an export. Verona's pandoro is its only rival, and the argument is settled by eating both.

The aperitivo and the other wines.

Milan invented the modern aperitivo. Gaspare Campari opened his bar in the city in 1867, and the red bitter he made there became the base of the Americano, the Negroni and the Negroni sbagliato, mixed by mistake with sparkling wine at Bar Basso in 1972. From six in the evening the bars fill and a drink comes with food, from olives to a full buffet. The sparkling wine in the glass is often Franciacorta, but Lombardy has other wines worth knowing: Lugana, a full white from the Turbiana grape on the southern shore of Lake Garda; the Oltrepò Pavese, south of the Po, with the largest Pinot Nero plantings in Italy and a frothy red Bonarda; and Valtellina's Nebbiolo from the reading before this one.

Before you move on.

Lombardy cooks with butter and rice: risotto alla milanese is Carnaroli, marrow and saffron, served with ossobuco and gremolata.

Cotoletta is a veal chop on the bone fried in butter; cassoeula, casoncelli, mostarda and torrone belong to the winter table.

The region leads Italy in DOP cheeses (Grana Padano, Gorgonzola, Taleggio, Bitto) and gave the world panettone and the aperitivo.
