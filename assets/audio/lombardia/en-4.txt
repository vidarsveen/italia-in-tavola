The Duomo and the city of design.

On 24 October 1387 Gian Galeazzo Visconti, lord of Milan, gave the builders of the new cathedral the marble quarry at Candoglia, in the mountains above Lake Maggiore. He also gave them the right to bring its pink-white stone to the city by water without paying tolls. The blocks went down the river Toce, across the lake and down the Ticino, and then along the Naviglio Grande, the canal that enters Milan from the south-west. Each barge was marked AUF, ad usum fabricae, for the use of the works, and the toll-keepers let it through. Italians call anything got for nothing a ufo, and in Milan they say the phrase comes from those barges. The Treccani dictionary calls the origin of a ufo unknown, and Florence tells the same story about the marble for its own cathedral.

Marble was only one cargo. Barges loaded at Sesto Calende, where the Ticino leaves the lake, and brought stone, coal and firewood down to Milan. The city's cheese, wine, fish and chestnuts came with them, and on the way back horses towed the barges up the canal with salt and grain. Most of the canals inside the city were later covered over for roads, but the Naviglio Grande and the Naviglio Pavese still run into the old docks of the Darsena. The last working barge arrived in 1979.

In 1774 a gilded copper statue of the Virgin a little over four metres tall, the Madonnina, was raised on the highest spire of the cathedral, 108 and a half metres above the square. The Veneranda Fabbrica del Duomo, set up in 1387 to build the church, still exists and still repairs it. A custom grew up that nothing in Milan should stand higher than the Madonnina.

Between the cathedral square and La Scala the city built a covered street to celebrate the new kingdom of Italy, and named it after the first king. The first stone of the Galleria Vittorio Emanuele II was laid in 1865, and the king opened it in September 1867. Two arcades cross there under a glass dome about 37 metres across, and the Milanese call it their salotto, their drawing room. Its cafés and food shops opened with it. Gaspare Campari opened his café that same year, at the corner facing the Duomo. He had settled the recipe for his red bitter in Novara in 1860, and his son Davide was born at the café in 1867. In 1915 Davide opened a bar of his own there, the Camparino, where soda water came to the counter in a steady supply from the cellars. In 1932 the firm began to sell Campari already mixed with soda, in a small bottle for one person, the first aperitif sold that way. The painter Fortunato Depero designed the bottle.

The Milanese bar gained its other fixture in the same decades. In 1901 Luigi Bezzera of Milan patented a machine that used steam pressure to make coffee to order, one cup at a time. Desiderio Pavoni bought the patent and began building the machines in a Milan workshop, one a day. In 1938 Achille Gaggia patented a machine that made coffee without steam. A lever drove a piston that pushed hot water through the ground coffee at high pressure. The coffee came out with a layer of fine foam on top, the crema, and Gaggia's company began selling the machines after the war.

For La Pavoni, in 1948, the architect Gio Ponti designed a bar machine with Antonio Fornaroli and Alberto Rosselli. It was the first with a horizontal boiler, and the heads that held the coffee stuck out from it like horns, so it was called La Cornuta, the horned one. In 1954 the department store La Rinascente founded a prize for industrial design, the Compasso d'Oro, on an idea of Ponti and Rosselli. Two of the winners that first year were a fitted kitchen built from units and a dinner service whose plates stacked. Ponti went on to build the Pirelli tower, which opened in 1960 at 127 metres. It stood higher than the Madonnina, so a copy of the statue was put on its roof.

In 1972 Mirko Stocchetto, the owner of Bar Basso, made a Negroni by mistake with sparkling wine where the gin should have gone. It has been called a Negroni sbagliato, a mistaken Negroni, ever since. The buffet came later. From about the 1990s Milanese bars began to set out food with the evening drink, from a bowl of olives to enough to replace dinner. Elsewhere in Italy that became known as the aperitivo alla milanese. On a summer evening the towpaths of the Naviglio Grande are lined with bars and workshops. The glass is often a Franciacorta, the plate holds something fried, and both are set down beside the water that brought the Duomo's marble into the city.
