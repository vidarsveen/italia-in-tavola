The Duomo and the city of design.

Six centuries of building, marble floated down a canal, a golden Madonna nothing may overtop, and a city that turned furniture and fashion into an industry. Milan's cathedral, its Galleria, and the design week that fills it every April.

Milan is the Italian city that does not look Italian. It has no hill town, no harbour and few ruins; it was heavily bombed in 1943 and rebuilt for business. What it has instead is a cathedral that took six centuries, a nineteenth-century shopping arcade that every mall in the world copies, and a reputation as the capital of design and fashion that it earned in the fifty years after the war. The Duomo is where the city starts, in every sense: the streets radiate from it, and the rule that nothing may stand taller than its golden Madonna shaped the skyline until the towers of the last decade.

Six hundred years of marble.

Work began in 1386 under Archbishop Antonio da Saluzzo and the city's new lord, Gian Galeazzo Visconti, who wanted a cathedral to match his ambitions. He gave the building works the quarries of Candoglia on Lake Maggiore, and the pink-white marble came down by water, across the lake, along the Ticino and up the Naviglio Grande canal to a dock a few hundred metres from the site. The barges were marked AUF, ad usum fabricae, for the use of the works, and paid no toll; Milanese still say a uf for anything that comes free. The Veneranda Fabbrica del Duomo, the body set up in 1387 to run the project, still exists and still repairs the building.

The style is late Gothic, the only large Gothic cathedral in Italy, designed with the help of French and German masters who quarrelled with the Lombard builders about almost everything. The Madonnina, a gilded copper statue of the Virgin four metres tall, was raised on the main spire in 1774, at 108 metres. Napoleon, crowned King of Italy in the nave in 1805, ordered the façade finished at the state's expense, and it was, in 1813, with a statue of himself on one spire as thanks. The last bronze door was inaugurated in 1965. The result is the largest church in Italy, since Saint Peter's stands in the Vatican, 158 metres long and able to hold forty thousand people.

Key facts.

Begun 1386; façade finished 1813; final portal 1965. 135 spires, around 3,400 statues, more than any other building in the world.

The Madonnina (1774) stands at 108.5 metres. By custom no building in Milan may rise above her, so the Pirelli tower (1960) and the newer skyscrapers each carry a copy of the statue on their roof.

The Galleria Vittorio Emanuele II (1865–1877) by Giuseppe Mengoni links the Duomo to La Scala under a glass dome 37 metres across; Mengoni fell to his death from its scaffolding shortly before it was completed.

Leonardo's Last Supper (1495–98) is in the refectory of Santa Maria delle Grazie; La Scala opened in 1778.

The Galleria and the drawing room of the city.

Next to the cathedral, the Galleria Vittorio Emanuele II is two glass-roofed streets crossing under an octagonal dome, built between 1865 and 1877 to celebrate the new Italian kingdom and named after its first king. It was among the first buildings in Milan lit by gas and then by electricity, it is the model for every covered arcade and shopping mall since, and Milanese call it their salotto, their drawing room. The floor mosaic of the bull of Turin has a hole worn in it by visitors who spin on their heel for luck. Around the octagon are the city's oldest cafés and food shops: Camparino, opened by the Campari family in 1915, where the aperitivo was refined, and the pastry houses that made panettone a luxury.

The city of design.

Milan's second act began after 1945. The furniture workshops of Brianza, the hills north of the city, had made chairs and cabinets for centuries; in the boom years they hired architects, and the architects became designers. The Triennale, the design museum in Sempione park, dates from 1933; the Compasso d'Oro, Italy's design prize, from 1954; and the Salone del Mobile, the furniture fair, from 1961. Today the Salone fills the fairground at Rho every April with hundreds of thousands of visitors, and the Fuorisalone spills into showrooms, courtyards and palazzi across the city for Design Week. Gio Ponti's Pirelli tower of 1960, a slim slab of concrete and glass, is the emblem of that era; the Torre Velasca, with its overhanging medieval-looking top, is its awkward sibling.

Fashion followed. Milan Fashion Week began in 1958, and by the 1980s Armani, Versace and Prada had made the Quadrilatero della Moda around Via Montenapoleone the most expensive four streets in Italy. The last twenty years have added a skyline: the Porta Nuova towers, Stefano Boeri's Bosco Verticale with 800 trees on its balconies, the three CityLife towers on the old fairground, and the Fondazione Prada, a distillery remade by Rem Koolhaas with a bar designed by Wes Anderson. Each new tower carries its own small copy of the Madonnina, so that the rule is kept in spirit while being broken in fact.

Where the canals were.

The marble came by canal, and until the 1930s the city centre was ringed by water, a circle of navigable canals that gave Milan a port bigger than most on the coast. Most were filled in for roads; the Naviglio Grande and the Naviglio Pavese survive in the south-west, lined with bars and workshops, and are where the city goes for its aperitivo on a summer evening. Standing on the towpath with a glass of Franciacorta and a plate of something fried, you are on the road the Duomo came down.

Before you move on.

The Duomo was begun in 1386 and finished in stages until 1965; its Candoglia marble came by canal, and its golden Madonnina sets the height of the city.

The Galleria Vittorio Emanuele II (1877) is the prototype of the shopping arcade and Milan's drawing room, with its cafés and food shops.

Milan became the capital of design through the Triennale, the Compasso d'Oro and the Salone del Mobile, and of fashion through the Quadrilatero.
