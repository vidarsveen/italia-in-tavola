Verdicchio: a white that ages like a red.

A grape that spent thirty years in a novelty bottle and is now taken seriously again. Where Verdicchio grows, why the two zones taste different, and what happens to it after ten years.

Verdicchio is the best white grape in Italy that most people have never deliberately bought. It grows almost nowhere except the Marche, it makes a wine that is lean and lemony when young and waxy, nutty and almost Burgundian at ten years old, and for a generation it was sold in a green bottle shaped like a fish-tail amphora that made it impossible to take seriously. The bottle sold enormous quantities and destroyed the wine's reputation at the same time. Both the bottle and the reputation are now largely gone, and what is left is a grape that ages better than almost any other white in the country.

Two zones, one grape.

The vineyards sit in the hills between the Apennines and the Adriatic, in two separate zones about forty kilometres apart. Castelli di Jesi is the larger by far, a spread of low hills behind the town of Jesi, west of Ancona, named after the castle-villages that crown them: Cupramontana, Montecarotto, Staffolo, Monte Roberto. It runs down almost to the sea, and the wines have a saltiness and a broad, ripe fruit that goes with it. Matelica is small, inland and much higher, in a narrow upland valley of the river Esino that runs north to south rather than east to west, so it gets no sea breeze at all. The days are hot, the nights are cold, and the wines are tighter, more acidic and more mineral, with a lemon-pith bite that takes longer to soften.

Key facts.

Castelli di Jesi Verdicchio Riserva DOCG (2010): at least 85% Verdicchio, minimum 12.5% alcohol, 18 months of ageing of which at least 6 in bottle. The everyday wine is Verdicchio dei Castelli di Jesi DOC, minimum 11.5%.

Verdicchio di Matelica DOC dates from 1967 and covers communes in the provinces of Macerata and Ancona; its Riserva is also a DOCG.

Verdicchio is closely related to the Trebbiano grown in Soave and Lugana; the clones are near-identical, which is why Soave and Verdicchio can taste like cousins.

The Classico zone is the historic core of the Castelli di Jesi hills. Passito and traditional-method sparkling Verdicchio are both made in small quantities.

The bottle.

In the middle of the last century the house of Fazi Battaglia put its Verdicchio into a tall green bottle shaped like a stylised amphora, with a little scroll of paper round the neck. It was a brilliant piece of packaging. It went everywhere, it was instantly recognisable, and it turned Verdicchio into a wine that people bought for the container and drank without attention. The wine inside was made to match: light, neutral, cheap. By the 1980s the amphora was shorthand for a certain kind of trattoria carafe wine, and the growers who wanted to make something better had to fight the shape they had been so successful with. Most serious producers now use an ordinary Burgundy bottle, and the amphora survives mainly as nostalgia.

What it tastes like, and what it becomes.

Young Verdicchio is pale, sometimes with a green cast, and smells of lemon, green apple and white flowers. There is usually a saline note, and always the bitter almond finish that is the grape's signature and the easiest way to recognise it blind. The acidity is high but not sharp. It is a wine that suits the food it grew up with, which is fish, and it does the job that a good Chablis does at half the price.

The interesting part is what happens next. Verdicchio has enough acidity and extract to survive a decade in bottle, and it does not merely survive but changes character: the citrus turns to dried apricot and honey, a waxy, lanolin texture appears, and the almond deepens into something closer to hazelnut. Producers such as Bucci, whose Villa Bucci Riserva is the most famous example, release wines at four or five years and expect them to be drunk at fifteen. Others to know are Garofoli, Umani Ronchi and Sartarelli in the Castelli di Jesi, and Bisci and La Monacesca in Matelica. Almost none of them is expensive, which remains the point.

In the glass: Verdicchio dei Castelli di Jesi.

 Colour: Pale straw with a green tinge; gold with age.

 Nose: Lemon, green apple, white flowers, fennel, a saline note; honey and apricot when older.

 Palate: Dry, high acidity, medium body, a waxy texture, and a clear bitter-almond finish.

 Alcohol: 12.5–14%.

 Serve: 10–12 °C; the everyday wine young, a Riserva from four years and for fifteen.

 At the table: Brodetto, stoccafisso all'anconetana, fried olives, grilled fish, white meat.

The rest of the white.

Verdicchio is not alone. The southern Marche grows Pecorino, a low-yielding white with real body and an almost herbal cut, rescued from near-extinction in the 1980s and now planted well beyond the region, and Passerina, lighter and made in every style from still to sparkling to passito. Both appear under the Offida DOCG and the Falerio DOC. But Verdicchio is what the region is for, and the argument between Jesi and Matelica about which does it better has been running for a century without either side conceding.

Before you move on.

Verdicchio grows in two zones: coastal, broader Castelli di Jesi and inland, higher, tighter Matelica.

The Castelli di Jesi Verdicchio Riserva became DOCG in 2010, with 18 months of ageing; Matelica's DOC dates from 1967.

The grape's marks are high acidity, a saline edge and a bitter-almond finish, and it ages for a decade or more.
