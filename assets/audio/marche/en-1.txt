Verdicchio: a white that ages like a red.

On 12 January 1579 a notary at Matelica, Niccolò Attucci, drew up a contract in which a tenant undertook to plant "all good vines, and moreover Verdicchio and Brungentile". It is the oldest known document that names Verdicchio in a place where it grew, and the rules of the Matelica appellation quote it today. The name comes from verde, green. Even when they are ripe, the berries keep a green tinge.

Vines were at Matelica long before the notary. About two hundred grape pips were found in the tomb of a Picene prince buried there in the seventh century BC. The town lies inland, in the upper valley of the Esino, where the river runs north, parallel to the Apennines and to the coast, before it turns east towards the sea. The vineyards stand between 250 and 700 metres, most of them between 280 and 480, and no influence from the sea reaches them. Summers are hot, winters are hard, and the nights are cold. The wine comes out tight, acidic and mineral, with a bite of lemon pith that takes years to soften.

The other Verdicchio grows further down the same river. There the Esino runs east past Jesi, west of Ancona, and the hills on either side hold the much larger zone of the Castelli di Jesi. The castelli are the walled villages that looked to Jesi for their politics and their trade, Cupramontana, Montecarotto and Staffolo among them. The zone ends about twenty kilometres short of the Adriatic. Its wines are broader and riper than Matelica's, with a salty edge.

Young Verdicchio from either zone is pale, sometimes with a green cast, and smells of lemon, green apple and white flowers. The finish is slightly bitter. Tasters call it bitter almond, and it is the quickest way to recognise the grape blind.

Here the vines were worked for generations under the mezzadria, the sharecropping contract. As it spread, wine stopped being a drink for the well-off and became part of what farming families lived on. But the contract divided the harvest. The landowner and the sharecropping family split the grapes, and each side made its wine separately at home. The rules of the Castelli di Jesi say plainly that so many methods and such different skills could not give a wine of quality. The vines grew in rows through fields of other crops. A law of 1964 banned new sharecropping contracts, and in the same decade money from the European Community paid for those mixed fields to be replaced with vineyards.

Two families, the Fazi and the Battaglia, had gone into business together at Cupramontana, and in 1953 their firm, Fazi Battaglia, held a competition to design a bottle for its Verdicchio. The architect Antonio Maiocchi won it with a tall green bottle shaped like a fish-tail amphora, with a paper scroll attached to its neck, and the design was patented that year. The firm says the shape came from Etruscan jars. The rules of the appellation say it was a Greek amphora, for the Greeks who founded Ancona. In America the bottle was nicknamed the Sophia Loren. It is still made and sold around the world. In 2008 the firm filled more than three million of them. The next year the Ara Pacis museum in Rome showed the bottle in an exhibition of Italian design patents.

In the 1970s plenty of people wrote Verdicchio off, but in the 1980s the growers who had kept at it were proved right. The wine has the acidity and the extract to last. In bottle the lemon turns to dried apricot and honey, the texture becomes waxy and the almond deepens towards hazelnut. The critic Ian D'Agata counts twenty-year-old Verdicchio among the longest-lived white wines in Italy.

In the same decade a white grape that had almost disappeared was found again in the south of the region. A sommelier, Teodoro Bugari, told the grower Guido Cocci Grifoni about an old vineyard at Pescara del Tronto, a hamlet of Arquata up the valley from Ascoli. One September morning Cocci Grifoni went up to see it. A man called Cafini still grew Pecorino there. Cocci Grifoni grafted cuttings from those vines, and his estate first sold a Pecorino in 1990. It has real body and a herbal edge, and it is now planted in Abruzzo, Lazio and Umbria as well.

Among the Verdicchio growers of that decade was Ampelio Bucci. He was born at Montecarotto in 1936, studied economics in Milan and made a career in fashion and design, and in 1982 he made his first Verdicchio. With the oenologist Giorgio Grai he chose large, old casks of Slavonian oak. He sold the estate in July 2024 and died in August 2025, at eighty-nine. He said that a vineyard needs at least seven years before it begins to know how, and nearly fifteen before it does well.
