Montepulciano on the Conero.

The divers of Portonovo went down every year from the middle of May, five to ten metres under the white cliffs of Monte Conero, and tore wild mussels off the rocks. The best-known beds lie on a reef called the Trave, off the beach at Mezzavalle. Nobody farms these mussels, which Ancona calls moscioli. They grow on the rock at the foot of a mountain that comes straight down to the sea.

Monte Conero rises to 572 metres. It is a block of limestone at the eastern end of the folds of the central Apennines, and it forms the elbow of Italy's Adriatic coast. For about twenty kilometres, from the port of Ancona to Numana, the shore is high, rocky and broken, where most of that coast is low. Around 387 BC Greeks from Syracuse settled beside the bend and called their town Ankon, which is Greek for elbow. Pliny the Elder, more than four centuries later, listed the wines "born at Ancona".

The Conero's red is Montepulciano, the grape Abruzzo grows in bulk further south, but here there is little of it. Most of the vineyards are not on the mountain. Seven tenths of them lie between 20 and 140 metres on the low hills behind it, mostly facing south-west, on limestone near the coast and on marl and sandstone further inland. The wine has been Rosso Conero DOC since 1967, with at least 85 per cent Montepulciano and Sangiovese allowed for the rest. It comes out deep ruby, with black cherry, plum and dried herbs, ripe but firm tannin and a long savoury finish, and it takes oak without losing its fruit.

In 2004 the Rosso Conero Riserva became a DOCG of its own and took the shorter name, Conero, so the plain name on the label belongs to the grander wine. It must age for at least two years, counted from the first of November of the harvest year, and it may be sold only in dark bottles of the Bordeaux shape. A Rosso Conero drinks well from two years old; a Conero is better from five, and good ones keep for fifteen.

Across most of the rest of the region the red is Rosso Piceno. It is the region's everyday red. Its zone runs down the coast from the Pesaro border to Porto d'Ascoli and inland across four provinces, and only the Conero is left out. The rules let Montepulciano fall to 35 per cent of the blend and Sangiovese rise to half of it. The Sangiovese makes the wine softer and rounder, a red to drink within a few years. The Superiore comes from the far south, from the hills of Offida and Ripatransone behind the coast at San Benedetto del Tronto.

North of Ancona, around the village of Morro d'Alba, grows a red grape that smells of rose petals and violets. The scent of Lacrima is so strong that people tasting it for the first time assume something has been added. In the mouth it is soft and dry, with red fruit and little tannin. Its name means tear. The likeliest reason is that the ripe berries split and weep juice, though nobody has proved it, and the grape is not the one behind the Lacryma Christi of Vesuvius. In September 1872 Ancona held an exhibition of the vines and wines of the Marche and Abruzzo, and the book that came out of it judged Lacrima an excellent aromatic wine, for its pleasant perfume. In 1879 it was one of only three Marche grapes in the first volume of the national survey of Italian vines.

After the Second World War, when the vineyards were replanted, Lacrima lost ground, and by the time it became a DOC in 1985 fewer than ten hectares were left. That year Stefano Mancinelli came home from studying agriculture in Florence and persuaded his father to bottle the family's Lacrima. For about a decade the growers around Morro d'Alba still ranked it below Verdicchio, Montepulciano and Sangiovese. By 2025 it covered 256 hectares.

At Portonovo the wild mussels had been a Slow Food Presidium since 2004. On 26 June 2025 Slow Food suspended it and asked for the harvest to stop completely for the season. It gave three reasons: the water had grown warmer, mucilage had spread in the sea, and too many mussels were being picked, some of them illegally. "The aim, of course, is to reopen the Presidium in the future," said Raffaella Ponzio of Slow Food.
