Montepulciano on the Conero.

A mountain of limestone drops straight into the Adriatic, and Montepulciano grows on its flanks. The Conero DOCG, the softer Rosso Piceno, and the rose-scented red that almost died out.

Nearly all the Adriatic coast of Italy is flat. For fifteen kilometres south of Ancona it is not: Monte Conero, a limestone hump 572 metres high, ends in white cliffs that fall into unusually clear water. The mountain is a nature reserve, its coves are the region's best beaches, and its landward flanks are planted with Montepulciano, the same grape that fills the tankers in Abruzzo further south. Here it is grown in small quantities on limestone rather than in bulk on the plain, and it makes a serious, structured red that the rest of Italy has mostly failed to notice.

Conero and Rosso Piceno.

The Marche has two red zones worth knowing, and they work the grape differently. Conero is the small one, on the mountain itself and the hills immediately behind it: at least 85% Montepulciano, up to 15% Sangiovese, minimum 12.5% alcohol. The everyday version is Rosso Conero DOC, an appellation since 1967; in 2004 the Riserva was separated out and promoted to Conero DOCG, which is why the top wines carry the shorter name. The limestone and the sea air give a wine with more grip and more perfume than Montepulciano usually shows, and it takes oak without disappearing.

Rosso Piceno is the other, a much larger zone running down the southern half of the region towards Ascoli. Here Montepulciano is blended with a substantial share of Sangiovese, which lightens it: the wine is softer, rounder and made to be drunk within a few years. The best part of it, around the villages behind San Benedetto del Tronto, may call itself Rosso Piceno Superiore. It is the everyday red of the region and it is very hard to spend much money on.

Key facts.

Conero DOCG (2004): at least 85% Montepulciano, maximum 15% Sangiovese, minimum 12.5% alcohol, made in Ancona, Offagna, Camerano, Sirolo, Numana and parts of Castelfidardo and Osimo. Rosso Conero DOC dates from 1967.

Rosso Piceno blends Montepulciano with Sangiovese over a much wider area; the Superiore comes from the hills inland of San Benedetto del Tronto.

Lacrima di Morro d'Alba DOC (1985): at least 85% Lacrima, from six communes around Morro d'Alba in the province of Ancona.

Vernaccia di Serrapetrona DOCG is a red sparkling wine made partly from dried grapes, and is unlike anything else in Italy.

Lacrima, the one that nearly went.

North of Ancona, around the village of Morro d'Alba, grows a grape called Lacrima that has no relation to the Lacryma Christi of Vesuvius and none to Alba in Piedmont either. It is worth finding because it does not smell like a red wine at all. The nose is rose petal and violet, so overtly floral that people assume it has been adulterated, followed by red fruit; the palate is soft, dry and low in tannin. It survived the twentieth century by luck. After the Second World War it was down to fewer than ten hectares, effectively one village's private habit, and the DOC granted in 1985 came just in time to make replanting worthwhile. It is now a small but real appellation, and the wine is best drunk young and slightly cool.

In the glass: Conero / Rosso Conero.

 Colour: Deep ruby, dense, purple at the rim when young.

 Nose: Black cherry, plum, dried herbs, liquorice, a note of pencil lead from the limestone.

 Palate: Full body, ripe but firm tannin, moderate acidity, a long savoury finish.

 Alcohol: 13–14.5%.

 Serve: 16–18 °C; Rosso Conero from two years, a Conero Riserva from five and for fifteen.

 At the table: Vincisgrassi, grilled lamb, game, aged pecorino, stews with rosemary.

Why nobody has heard of it.

The Marche has everything a fashionable wine region needs except attention. It is squeezed between Tuscany and Abruzzo on the map and in the mind; its capital, Ancona, is a working port that most travellers pass through on the way to a ferry; and its best wines are made by a few dozen small producers who have never had the marketing budget of the Langhe. Umani Ronchi, Garofoli and Moroder are the names most likely to reach a shop abroad. The upside for a drinker is straightforward: the quality-to-price ratio here is among the best in Italy, and it has stayed that way precisely because the region has not become fashionable.

Before you move on.

Monte Conero's limestone gives Montepulciano more structure and perfume than it shows elsewhere; the Riserva became Conero DOCG in 2004.

Rosso Piceno blends Montepulciano with Sangiovese over a wider area and is the region's everyday red.

Lacrima di Morro d'Alba smells of roses and violets, and survived on fewer than ten hectares before its 1985 DOC.
