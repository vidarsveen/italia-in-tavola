Ascoli olives and the Adriatic brodetto.

A green olive with a meatball inside, a fish stew argued over from port to port, and a baked pasta named after an Austrian general who probably never ate it. The cooking of a region with a coast and a mountain range.

The Marche is narrow and stands on edge: from the Adriatic to the peaks of the Sibillini is barely eighty kilometres, and the cooking changes completely along the way. The coast eats fish, and cooks it in a stew that every port claims to have perfected. The hills eat pork, in a salami soft enough to spread. The inland towns bake a rich layered pasta for feast days. And in the south, at Ascoli Piceno, they take the largest green olives in Italy, remove the stones, put meat inside and deep-fry them, which is the single most addictive thing the region has produced.

Olive all'ascolana.

The olive is a specific one: Ascolana tenera, unusually large, thin-skinned and mild, grown around Ascoli Piceno and Fermo and protected since 2005 as Oliva Ascolana del Piceno DOP. To make the stuffed version the olive is pitted with a small knife in a single spiral cut, so that it opens into a ribbon and can be wound back around a filling of minced beef, pork and sometimes chicken, cooked with wine and bound with egg and Parmigiano. Then it is floured, egged, breadcrumbed and fried. Done properly they are the size of a walnut, crisp outside, and the olive's saltiness cuts the richness of the meat. They are eaten as a snack, as an antipasto, and as part of the fritto misto all'ascolana, a plate of fried things that also includes lamb chops, courgettes and squares of custard, which sounds wrong and is not.

Key facts.

Oliva Ascolana del Piceno DOP (2005) covers both olives in brine and the stuffed kind, from the Ascolana tenera cultivar grown in Ascoli Piceno, Fermo and part of Teramo.

Ciauscolo IGP (2009) is a pork salami soft enough to spread on bread, made in the provinces of Ancona, Macerata, Fermo and Ascoli Piceno.

Vincisgrassi first appears as lasagna in princisgras in Antonio Nebbia's Il cuoco maceratese of 1779 — with prosciutto and truffles, and without either béchamel or giblets.

Casciotta d'Urbino DOP is a soft sheep-and-cow cheese from the hills around Urbino, said to have been Michelangelo's favourite.

Brodetto, thirteen times over.

Brodetto is the Adriatic fish stew, and like every dish of its kind it began as what the fishermen could not sell. Each port has its own rules and defends them. At Porto Recanati the broth is yellow with saffron and there is no tomato. At San Benedetto del Tronto there is no ripe tomato either, sometimes a green one, and the wine is replaced by vinegar with sweet peppers added. At Fano, on the northern coast, it is made with tomato paste and vinegar. And at Ancona, the version the region is best known for, tradition insists on thirteen kinds of fish, though nobody can produce a document that says why thirteen, and cooks will tell you the number matters less than the mix of firm and soft, oily and lean. Whatever the port, the fish go into the pot in order of how long they take, the broth is thickened by the shellfish, and the whole thing is eaten with grilled bread and a bottle of Verdicchio.

Vincisgrassi and the general.

Vincisgrassi is the region's feast-day pasta, and it is not lasagne, whatever it looks like. The sheets are layered with a ragù of coarsely chopped meat rather than mince, traditionally including chicken livers and other giblets, and sometimes sweetbreads; the béchamel is stiffer; and the seasoning runs to cloves and nutmeg. The story attached to it is that a cook in Ancona invented it in honour of the Austrian general Alfred zu Windisch-Graetz during the siege of 1799, and that the name is his, Italianised. It is a good story and almost certainly not true, since Nebbia printed a recipe for princisgras twenty years before the general arrived. What Nebbia's version shows is that the dish began as an aristocratic one, full of truffles and prosciutto, and became a family dish later.

The hills and the pig.

Ciauscolo is the salami of the inland Marche and the thing to look for in any shop there. It is made from shoulder, belly and other fatty cuts, ground very fine, seasoned with garlic, pepper and white wine, and cured only briefly, so it stays soft: soft enough to spread with a knife on bread, which is exactly how it is eaten. The version from the upper Macerata hills has the highest fat content and is the most spreadable. Alongside it the region bakes crescia, a flatbread cooked on a hot stone, and in Urbino a layered version rich with egg and lard that is split and filled with ham and cheese. The cheeses are sheep's-milk pecorino and the milder casciotta d'Urbino, and at the top of a meal there is often vino cotto, must boiled down and aged, poured over ice or into a glass at the end.

Before you move on.

Olive all'ascolana are Ascolana tenera olives pitted in a spiral, stuffed with meat, breaded and fried; the DOP dates from 2005.

Brodetto changes from port to port — saffron at Porto Recanati, vinegar and peppers at San Benedetto — and Ancona's is traditionally said to use thirteen fish.

Vincisgrassi predates the general it is named after; ciauscolo is a salami soft enough to spread.
