Ascoli olives and the Adriatic brodetto.

Take a large green olive from Ascoli Piceno, set the point of a small knife at the stalk end and cut round the stone, turning the olive against the blade. If the cut is right, the flesh comes away in one long ribbon and the stone drops out. The ribbon is wound back around a nut of cooked, minced meat, and the olive closes into its own shape again, a little fatter than before. Then it is rolled in flour, dipped in egg, coated in breadcrumbs and fried. This is olive all'ascolana. The spiral cut is the traditional way to stone it, although the rules of the DOP say only that the olives must be pitted.

The olive is the Ascolana tenera, large, thin-skinned and mild, grown around Ascoli Piceno and Fermo and over the border in Abruzzo. The Romans already rated the olives of this part of Italy. Pliny the Elder wrote that within Italy the olives of Picenum and of the Sidicini were preferred to all others for eating. The meat came much later. A local historian dates the stuffed olive to about 1800, when the cooks of noble families wanted a use for leftover meat. The rules of the DOP put it around the unification of Italy, in 1859–61. Neither date rests on a document. The filling today is beef and pork with a little poultry, cooked with dry white wine, then minced and bound with egg, aged cheese and nutmeg.

In Ascoli they are eaten hot, as a snack or an antipasto. They are also one part of the fritto misto all'ascolana, where they share the plate with fried lamb chops, courgettes, artichokes and cremini, which are squares of custard, breaded and fried.

Along the coast the fish stew changes from one port to the next. Brodetto began as the fishermen's own meal, cooked on board from the part of the catch they could not sell. At San Benedetto del Tronto, in the south, there is no ripe tomato in it. There are green tomatoes, sweet peppers and wine vinegar, and the usual explanation is practical. The vinegar and the green tomato softened fish that was strong or no longer fresh, and they kept it through days at sea. Fano, in the north, uses vinegar too, and adds tomato concentrate.

There is no tomato at all in the brodetto of Porto Recanati. The broth is yellow, and the colour comes from zafferanella, a wild plant that is not true saffron. The recipe is credited to Giovanni Velluti, who kept one of the town's oldest beach chalets early in the twentieth century. In 1923 a Touring Club guide called it brodetto bianco, white brodetto, though the broth is yellow.

In Ancona, whose version the region is best known for, the brodetto is said to need thirteen kinds of fish. Some say thirteen for the table of the Last Supper, some say fishermen count it lucky, and some point to the thirteen spouts of the city's old fountain. Where the rule really comes from, nobody knows. The pot itself follows an order. Squid and cuttlefish go in first, firm fish later, and oily fish, langoustines and mussels at the end, each for as long as it needs, and the shellfish thicken the broth.

For feast days the inland Marche bakes vincisgrassi, sheets of egg pasta layered with ragù, béchamel and cheese. The name comes with a story. An Austrian prince of the Windisch-Graetz family was in Ancona during a siege, and the dish was named after him. The story was first written down in 1927, in a Marche cookbook by Cesare Tirabasso, and the dates do not support it. When the Austrians besieged Ancona in 1799, their general was Michael von Frölich, and Alfred zu Windisch-Graetz, the prince usually named, was twelve years old. The recipe is older than the story. In 1779 the Macerata cook Antonio Nebbia printed princisgras in his book Il cuoco maceratese. Between the sheets of pasta went prosciutto and sliced truffle, with a sauce of milk thickened with flour and enriched with cream, eggs, butter and Parmigiano. The dish was baked until a crust formed.

Nebbia's dish was made for a rich table and became a family dish later, and it had no giblets. The one Macerata makes today has them. Since 2022 vincisgrassi alla maceratese has been a Traditional Speciality Guaranteed in the European Union. Its rules ask for chicken, duck, goose or rabbit with their stomachs and livers, an onion studded with cloves, a béchamel with nutmeg, and seven or eight layers of pasta. The dough may be made with a little vino cotto or Marsala.

In the hills the winter pig-killing brought people together. Under the mezzadria it was also when the sharecropper sent gifts to the landowner. The pig gave ciauscolo, a salami of belly and shoulder with trimmings of ham and loin, minced very fine and seasoned with salt, black pepper, garlic and wine. It dries for a few days and cures for as little as a fortnight, so it stays soft enough to spread on bread with a knife. The rules of its IGP say the name may come from ciabusculum, a small meal: the bite eaten between breakfast and lunch, and again between lunch and supper.

Lard goes into the flatbread of Urbino, the crescia sfogliata, which is made with eggs, rolled into layers and cooked on a hot slab, then split and filled with ham and cheese. At the end of a meal there may be vino cotto. The grape must is boiled for ten or twelve hours in a copper cauldron over a wood fire, until a third or half of it has gone. Then it ferments and waits for years in wooden casks.

Nearly two thousand years ago the poet Martial wrote a two-line label for a gift of olives from the presses of Picenum. The olive, he wrote, both begins the meal and ends it.
