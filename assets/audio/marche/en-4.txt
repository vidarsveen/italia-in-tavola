Urbino and the ideal city.

A mercenary soldier built the most civilised building of the Renaissance on a hill in the middle of nowhere. The palace, the tiny study made of inlaid wood, the painting of a city that never existed, and the caves under the mountains.

In the fifteenth century the small hill town of Urbino was, for about forty years, one of the most cultivated places in Europe. It had no natural advantages: no river, no port, no trade route, no plain. What it had was a duke who was very good at war, spent the proceeds on architects and books, and understood that a court could be a work of art in itself. When he died the moment ended almost at once, and the town has been quietly preserved ever since, which is why walking into it now feels like walking into a painting.

Federico da Montefeltro.

Federico da Montefeltro ruled Urbino from 1444 to 1482 and paid for it by hiring himself out as a condottiero, a mercenary captain, to whichever Italian power was fighting that season. He was reliable, expensive and rarely defeated, and the contracts made him rich. He lost his right eye and the bridge of his nose in a tournament, which is why every portrait shows him in profile from the left. He built one of the largest libraries in Europe, employed scholars to copy manuscripts because he disliked printed books, and turned his court into the model that Baldassare Castiglione would later describe in The Book of the Courtier, set in these rooms.

The palace.

Work on the Palazzo Ducale began in 1454. The Florentine Maso di Bartolomeo started it; Luciano Laurana, a Dalmatian better known for fortifications, built the substructures and the celebrated courtyard before leaving; Francesco di Giorgio Martini finished much of the rest, and the young Bramante, born nearby, grew up watching it go up. Its famous face is the western one, where two slender round turrets frame a stack of loggias, a piece of architecture that manages to be a fortress and a balcony at once. Inside, the arcaded courtyard is the calmest space of the early Renaissance, and the scale throughout is domestic rather than imperial: the building is enormous but never shouts.

Its smallest room is its most famous. The studiolo is a private study of about three and a half metres square, lined to shoulder height with wood inlay so precise that it reads as trompe-l'œil: half-open latticework cupboards, shelves of books and instruments, a lute, an hourglass, all made of coloured wood laid flat. Above the panelling hangs a row of twenty-eight portraits of great writers and thinkers, painted by Joos van Wassenhove and Pedro Berruguete. Below the study are two matching chapels, one Christian and one dedicated to the Muses, which tells you most of what you need to know about the mind of the man who commissioned them.

Key facts.

The historic centre of Urbino has been a UNESCO World Heritage Site since 1998. The university was founded in 1506 and still fills the town with students.

The Galleria Nazionale delle Marche in the palace holds Piero della Francesca's Flagellation and Madonna di Senigallia, Raphael's La Muta, and the panel known as the Ideal City, whose author is still disputed.

Raphael was born in Urbino in 1483, the son of a court painter; his family house is a museum.

The Frasassi caves were found on 25 September 1971 by Ancona speleologists led by Giancarlo Cappanera and opened to visitors in 1974.

The painting of a city that does not exist.

Hanging in the palace is a long, low panel showing a paved square, a round temple at the centre, and a row of perfectly proportioned buildings receding to a vanishing point. There are almost no people in it. The Ideal City is one of three similar panels that survive, and nobody agrees who painted it: Piero della Francesca, Laurana, Francesco di Giorgio and Alberti have all been proposed, and the argument has run for a century. What matters is what it represents. Somebody in this court sat down and worked out what a city would look like if it were designed according to reason, proportion and perspective, rather than growing by accident over centuries. It is the clearest surviving statement of what the Renaissance thought it was doing.

Below and behind.

The rest of the region is quieter but not empty. At Loreto, on a hill above the coast, stands the basilica built around the Holy House, brought here according to tradition in the thirteenth century; it has been one of the most visited pilgrimage sites in Italy ever since, and the piazza in front of it is one of the finest in the Marche. Inland, under the Apennines near Genga, the Frasassi caves open into some of the largest underground chambers in Europe: the Abisso Ancona alone is roughly 180 by 120 metres and nearly 200 metres high, big enough, as the guides like to say, to hold Milan cathedral. Further south the Monti Sibillini rise to over 2,400 metres, a national park of high pasture and beech forest with a lake said to be bottomless and a cave said to have held a sibyl. And at Recanati, between the mountains and the sea, Giacomo Leopardi grew up in his father's library and wrote poems about the hill at the end of his garden, which is still there, and still called L'Infinito.

Before you move on.

Federico da Montefeltro paid for Urbino's golden age with mercenary contracts and ruled from 1444 to 1482.

The Palazzo Ducale's twin-turret façade, arcaded courtyard and inlaid studiolo are the high point of early Renaissance civility; Urbino has been World Heritage since 1998.

The Ideal City panel, of disputed authorship, is the Renaissance's clearest picture of a city designed by reason.
