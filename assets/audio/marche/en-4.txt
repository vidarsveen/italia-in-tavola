Urbino and the ideal city.

When Federico da Montefeltro sat down to eat in his palace at Urbino, every door stood open. Anyone could come in to where the duke was, and he never ate unless the hall was full. While he ate, someone read aloud to him in Latin: spiritual books in Lent, and at other times Livy's history of Rome. The food was plain. He ate no sugared sweets, and out of self-restraint he drank no wine, only drinks made from pomegranates or from fruit such as cherries and apples. People who wanted a word with him could have it while he ate or afterwards. The Florentine bookseller Vespasiano da Bisticci, who supplied his library, wrote all of this down.

Federico became lord of Urbino in 1444, at twenty-two, after his half-brother was murdered, and the pope made him a duke in 1474. Urbino was a small hill town. He paid for his court by fighting other states' wars as a condottiero, a captain who hired out himself and his troops. Milan, Naples, Florence and the pope all hired him, for as much as 120,000 ducats a year, and Vespasiano says that Venice once offered him 80,000 a year just to stay at home. He was rarely beaten.

The money kept a large household. Vespasiano counted five hundred mouths or more living at Federico's expense, and he says the house was run like a religious community, with no gambling and no swearing. Every day it gave away bread and wine as alms. Federico kept the fasts of the Church, on the eve of feasts and through Lent. In his last year a papal dispensation from the Lenten fast was obtained for him, and it was handed to him at table. He laughed, gave thanks for it, asked what example he would be setting his household, and went on fasting.

That household lived in the Palazzo Ducale, which Federico began to build around 1454 by joining older houses on the hill. Below its state rooms lay the kitchens, an ice house and the cisterns, and a spiral ramp led down to the stables.

Food from the hills of Urbino went as far as Rome. Francesco Amadori, Michelangelo's servant and assistant for twenty-five years, came from Casteldurante, now Urbania, and he was called simply l'Urbino. Michelangelo owned three farms near Casteldurante, let to tenants through Amadori, and a deed of 1554 bound the tenants to send twenty-five pounds of cheese to Rome every year, with Amadori paying the carriage. It was cascio di guaime, cheese of the grass that grows back after mowing. Amadori died in 1555, and his widow, Cornelia Colonnelli, kept on sending. "When you sent me the cheeses," Michelangelo wrote to her, "you wrote that you wanted to send me other things as well." Two hams and two pairs of cheeses went to Rome in 1558, and six small casciotti in 1561. The cheese of these hills today is Casciotta d'Urbino, made mostly from sheep's milk with some cow's.

In the palace, which now holds the Galleria Nazionale delle Marche, hangs a long, low panel painted in central Italy between 1480 and 1490. It shows a paved square with a round temple at its centre, and buildings in perfect proportion run back to a vanishing point. It is a city laid out by reason and perspective instead of grown by accident. Nobody knows who painted it. The Ideal City has not a single person in it, and it hangs in the palace of a duke who would not eat unless his hall was full.
