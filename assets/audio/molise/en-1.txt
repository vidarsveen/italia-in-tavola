A region rediscovered.

Italy's youngest region is also its least visited, and the joke is that it does not exist. What Molise actually is, why almost nobody drinks its wine, and the three appellations that carry its name.

Molise has a problem that no other Italian region has: people are not sure it is there. "Il Molise non esiste" is a running joke on the Italian internet, complete with maps that leave a hole between Abruzzo and Puglia. The region is real, but it is small, young and empty. It became a region in its own right only in 1963, when the old Abruzzi e Molise was split in two, which makes it the newest region in Italy. It is the second smallest after Valle d'Aosta, about 4,460 square kilometres, and fewer than 290,000 people live in it, under one per cent of the country.

Mountain, hill, and thirty-five kilometres of coast.

The shape of Molise explains most of its wine. Roughly 55% of the region is mountain and almost all the rest is hill; there is very little flat land. The Apennines run down the western side, rising to the limestone wall of the Matese, and the land then falls eastwards in ridge after ridge of clay hills towards a short Adriatic coastline of about thirty-five kilometres. Three rivers cut the region on their way to the sea, and the largest of them, the Biferno, rises at Bojano under the Matese and runs eighty-five kilometres to the coast between Termoli and Campomarino. Halfway down it is dammed into the long reservoir of Guardialfiera, and the valley below the dam is the warmest, broadest and most planted land in Molise.

Two provinces and a long emigration.

Molise has only two provinces, Campobasso and Isernia, and its capital, Campobasso, is a town of around 45,000 people. The mountains made it poor, and between 1951 and 1971 the region emptied: people left for northern Italy, for the rest of Europe, for the Americas. The population is still falling, by about seven per cent in the decade to 2021, and there are villages in the interior where the winter population is counted in dozens. This matters for wine, because it means small growers, small cellars, and very little of the money that turned Piedmont and Tuscany into export machines. It also means that the hills were never industrialised, and that old grape varieties survived in corners where nobody thought to pull them out.

Key facts.

Molise separated from Abruzzo in 1963: the youngest Italian region, the second smallest, and the second least populated.

Biferno DOC (1983), named after the river: Rosso and Rosato are 70–80% Montepulciano with 10–20% Aglianico; Bianco is mostly Trebbiano Toscano. Riserva needs three years.

Molise DOC (1988) covers both provinces and a long list of varietal wines, among them Falanghina, Aglianico, Trebbiano and Moscato.

Tintilia del Molise DOC (2011) is the region's own grape, and its vineyards must sit at 200 metres or higher.

Three names on the label.

Molise's appellations are few and recent. Biferno, from 1983, is the oldest and the best known, a Montepulciano and Aglianico blend from the river valley and the hills around Larino and Campomarino; it is the wine most likely to be sold outside the region. Molise DOC, from 1988, is a catch-all covering both provinces and almost any grape you can name, which makes it useful for growers and confusing for drinkers. Tintilia del Molise, from 2011, is the interesting one, and the next reading is about it. There is a curiosity in the Molise DOC rules: Montepulciano was permitted under the appellation until 2000, when it was removed after a legal challenge from Tuscan producers over the use of the name Montepulciano, which is also a Tuscan town.

Why you have probably never drunk it.

Molise makes a small amount of wine and exports very little of it. To put it plainly for a Norwegian reader: at the time of writing, Vinmonopolet lists a single wine from Molise in its entire range, a Biferno Rosso Riserva. That is not a judgement on the quality; it is a statement about scale and distribution. The region has perhaps a few dozen bottling estates, and most of the crop still goes to cooperatives or is sold as bulk wine to blenders elsewhere. If you want to taste Molise you will most likely have to go there, which is exactly what its growers keep saying.

Before you move on.

Molise split from Abruzzo in 1963 and is Italy's youngest region, second smallest, mountainous and steadily emptying.

Its three appellations are Biferno (1983), the catch-all Molise DOC (1988) and Tintilia del Molise (2011).

Production and export are tiny: Vinmonopolet currently lists one Molise wine, so the region is a discovery rather than a shelf staple.
