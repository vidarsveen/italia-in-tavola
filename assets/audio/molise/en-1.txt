A region rediscovered.

Tuscany went to the administrative court in Rome in 2000 to take a word off a neighbour's wine labels. The word was Montepulciano, and the neighbour was Molise, which had been granted a denomination two years earlier with a type inside it called Molise Montepulciano. The parties were the Regione Toscana, the Provincia di Siena and the Consorzio del Vino Nobile di Montepulciano. The challenge was aimed at the ministry that had granted the name, and a decree of 10 October 2000 struck the type out.

Molise's growers kept the grape and lost the label. The wine is sold today as Molise Rosso, at least 85 per cent Montepulciano, with the word off the front of the bottle. The Tuscan wine the case was brought to protect is made from Sangiovese and named after a hill town, and it has no connection with the Montepulciano grape at all. Abruzzo, next door, had held the name since 1968 and kept it. Molise asked in 1998 and did not.

The denomination the word was taken out of had been a catch-all from the day it was granted. Molise DOC, from May 1998, covers over eighty comuni in Campobasso and over forty in Isernia, which is effectively both provinces entire. It runs a dozen varietal wines at 85 per cent each, red and white, from Montepulciano and Aglianico to Falanghina and Trebbiano. What the decree of 2000 removed was one type out of that list.

Molise is said to be the only Italian region ever made by splitting another in half. A constitutional law of 27 December 1963 rewrote the article that lists Italy's regions and divided Abruzzi e Molise in two, and the new region came into being on 19 January 1964. For its first six years it had a single province, because Isernia was created only in 1970; the capital is Campobasso. It is the second smallest region after Valle d'Aosta, 4,460 square kilometres, and about 285,000 people live in it, under half a per cent of the country.

The land is 55.3 per cent mountain and 44.7 per cent hill, and there is no plain at all. The vineyard has exactly the same shape, mountain and hill and nothing else. The Apennines run down the western side and rise to the limestone wall of the Matese. From there the land falls eastwards in ridge after ridge of clay hills, towards thirty-six kilometres of Adriatic coast. Three rivers cross it on the way to the sea, and the largest of them is the Biferno.

Since the early 2000s the region has been the subject of a joke on the Italian internet, that it does not exist, complete with maps leaving a hole between Abruzzo and Puglia. It went properly viral around 2013 and 2014 as il Molise non esiste, and the region's own tourist marketing later took it up. Underneath the joke is a real emigration. The population peaked at about 331,000 in 1951 and has been falling since.

The oldest is Biferno, from 1983. It is a Montepulciano and Aglianico blend, named for the river. The Biferno rises in the comune of Bojano, at a locality called Pietrecadute five hundred metres up under the Matese. It gathers streams off the limestone and runs some eighty-five kilometres to an estuary between Termoli and Campomarino. Halfway down it fills the long reservoir of Guardialfiera, built in the 1960s and 1970s, which is younger than the region itself.

The rules put Biferno's vineyards below five hundred metres for black grapes and below six hundred for white. Tintilia's rules put its vineyards above two hundred. So the same hillside is divided between two appellations, one working up from the valley and the other down from the ridge. The line between them is written into both rulebooks. Pentro di Isernia, from the same year as Biferno, is the fourth name, and it is the one that gets left out.

The scale explains it. Biferno DOC is forty-eight hectares. Put it together with Molise DOC and under three per cent of the wine the region makes carries either of its two main names.

The rest goes through the co-operatives, which are old and much larger than the estates. Cantina Valtappino was founded in 1969 by 274 growers, Cantina Cliternia three years later by about a hundred and fifty. The Cantina Sociale San Zenone at Montenero di Bisaccia was started in the mid-1970s by about thirty growers and now has around a hundred. It describes its own trade without embarrassment: in bottle, in five-litre bag-in-box, and loose.
