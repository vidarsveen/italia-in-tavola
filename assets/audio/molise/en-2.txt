Tintilia: a grape saved from extinction.

Raffaele Pepe, an agronomist at Civitacampomarano, wrote down in 1810 the oldest surviving description of Molise's own grape. He called it Le Tenturier d'Espagne = Tintiglia. He was asking for marze forastiere — cuttings from outside — for the interior of the region, to give body and colour to wines that had neither. He also told the growers around him to make better wine without resorting to sophisticated oenological practices. The appellation still quotes him in its own historical section. And the name he used is French.

The obvious guess is the Italian tingere, to dye, because the wine is dark. Almost every wine page prefers the Spanish tinto, a deeply coloured red, and says that Spanish rule over the Kingdom of Naples brought the vine with it. Read those pages closely and they hedge: presumibilmente, potrebbe. It is a plausible etymology that has been repeated until it reads like a history. Against it stands the one primary document, which gives the vine a French name, says it came from France, and asks to import it rather than describing something already at home. And against both stands the genetics, which match nothing in Spain. There is a further puzzle nobody resolves: a teinturier is properly a grape with red flesh, and no source describes Tintilia as one.

In 2006 three researchers at the Università del Molise — Reale, Pilla and Angiolillo — ran thirteen SSR markers across thirty-seven samples. They covered Tintilia and everything it had been confused with, from southern Italy and from Spain. The Molise samples came back identical to one another. They were different from every other vine called Tintilia in Italy, and different from Spain. They also came back different from Bovale grande, the Sardinian grape the Vitis International Variety Catalogue had recorded as the same variety. Fabio Pilla's own conclusion stayed careful: an Iberian origin remains plausible, and either the original vine has been lost or Tintilia has changed. Twenty years on, reference sites still list Bovale names among Tintilia's synonyms.

The grape had been going out for a century. Phylloxera took the old plantings and the post-war years finished them, and what went back into the ground was Montepulciano and Trebbiano, which yielded more and sold more easily. In the early 1990s an agronomist named Michele Tanno, who worked on old fruit varieties, ancient grains and rare grapes, went round the region collecting genetic material from the Tintilia vines that were left. He is credited with being the first to believe the variety could be brought back. Claudio Cipressi worked with him on small trial vinifications and replanted at four hundred and fifty metres at San Felice del Molise. The grape entered the national register in 2002 or early 2003, and the denomination followed in 2011.

Molise has a second candidate for the same credit. Giuseppe Mogavero sat on the regional council through five terms, from its first legislature in 1970 until 1995. In 1975 he founded an estate at Petrella Tifernina and put Tintilia back in the ground there. His obituary calls him the variety's discoverer. Both accounts are in circulation, and neither cancels the other.

It had also never quite gone. When Pentro di Isernia was written in 1983, its red and its rosato were required to contain between twenty and twenty-five per cent Tintilia, twenty-eight years before the grape had a denomination of its own.

The line that defines the denomination is the altitude clause. Only vineyards standing on hillside land, and no lower than two hundred metres above sea level, may be entered in the register. Both conditions have to hold. Growers defend that floor as the thing that keeps the wine fresh. What height does to any vineyard is slow the nights down. Cool air after a hot day holds acidity in the grape and keeps the aromatics from cooking off, and a late variety can hang longer to get there. The rules themselves record none of that. They give a line and no reason for it, and the growers' argument is the growers'.

Tintilia ripens late, generally between the end of September and the middle of October, on small compact bunches of small berries with thick, heavily bloomed blue-black skins. The rules call the Rosso deep ruby with violet lights, and the Riserva speziato, spiced. That word is in the law, and it is the one drinkers reach for anyway: black pepper over sour cherry and blackberry, with dried herbs, tobacco and a wild, scrubby edge. Drinkers reach for the Syrah of the northern Rhône as a comparison; the grapes are not related. Tannins are firm and a little rustic in the plainer bottlings.

The rules ask of the Rosato only a pink "more or less intense". That allows a pale wine as readily as a deep one, so the dark rosato Molise is known for is a house style.

One estate is much the largest, and it is the one most likely to be met abroad. Di Majo Norante at Campomarino farms a hundred and twenty-three hectares on the old fief of the Marchesi Norante di Santa Cristina, between a hundred and two hundred metres. It has been in certified organic control since 19 October 1996, an early date for Italy, with Riccardo Cotarella advising from the mid-1990s, and it grows Tintilia alongside Aglianico, Montepulciano, Falanghina and Greco. Alessio Di Majo Norante, who carried the name of Molise out of Molise and went to Vinitaly in Verona every year to the end, died on 19 April 2026, in his sixties.

The rest are small. Claudio Cipressi at San Felice del Molise farms sixteen hectares, eleven of them Tintilia, at four hundred and fifty metres, and makes around fifty thousand bottles. Tenimenti Grieco at Portocannone has four and a half hectares at Larino and Ururi, at three hundred metres.

On 9 August 2026, at San Martino in Pensilis, the growers argued in public about whether the two hundred metres should stand at all. The proposal was three tiers: a DOCG above four hundred metres, the DOC on the present rules, and an IGT running down to the coast. Cipressi, as president of the consortium, was against it. The Tintilia DOC is not to be touched, he said, and the present parameters, cultivation from two hundred metres up, must stay unchanged. Adamo Spagnoletti of Coldiretti Molise wanted the rules revised. Adamantonio Flocco, who runs the San Zenone co-operative, spoke about what the rule costs the growers who have to obey it. Nothing was settled. Down at Portocannone, Tenimenti Grieco has printed the line on the label: the wine is called 200 Metri.
