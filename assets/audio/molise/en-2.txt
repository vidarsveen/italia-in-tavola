Tintilia: a grape saved from extinction.

A dark, peppery grape that almost disappeared after the war, brought back by a handful of growers and given its own appellation in 2011. What it is, what it tastes like, and why nobody agrees where the name comes from.

Every Italian region has a native grape it is proud of. Molise's is Tintilia, and until about thirty years ago it was close to gone. It survived in old mixed plantings in the hills, was written off as a peasant vine, and was replaced almost everywhere by Montepulciano and Sangiovese, which yielded more and sold more easily. A small group of growers and the regional agricultural service went looking for what was left, propagated it, and in 2011 it got an appellation of its own: Tintilia del Molise DOC. It is now the thing Molise is known for, to the extent that Molise is known for anything.

Where the name comes from, probably.

The obvious explanation is the Italian tingere, to dye, because the wine is dark. The explanation most sources prefer is the Spanish tinto, meaning a deeply coloured red, which would date the name to the long Spanish rule over the Kingdom of Naples. Neither is proven, and you will find both stated confidently. What is documented is the grape itself: an agronomist's manuscript of 1810 describes it in Molise, which makes it a genuinely old regional variety rather than a recent rebranding. DNA work has separated it from the Bovale grapes of Sardinia, with which it was long confused.

Key facts.

Tintilia del Molise DOC, 2011: at least 95% Tintilia, in Rosso, Rosso Riserva and Rosato.

Minimum alcohol 11.5% for Rosso and Rosato, 13% for Riserva, which must also age two years from 1 November of the harvest year.

Vineyards must be at 200 metres or higher, which is unusual in an appellation's rules and is there to keep the wine fresh.

Maximum yield is 80 quintals per hectare, about 8 tonnes, and lower again for Riserva.

Altitude written into the law.

The 200-metre rule is the most interesting line in the appellation. Molise is a southern region, on the same latitude as Naples, and on the low ground its grapes ripen fast and flabby. The inland hills where Tintilia grows sit between roughly 200 and 600 metres, on clay with limestone under it, and they are genuinely cold at night: the Matese sends air down the valleys and the difference between afternoon and dawn in September can be fifteen degrees or more. That swing is what gives the wine its acidity and its perfume, and the appellation's authors clearly decided it was the one thing worth making compulsory.

What it tastes like.

Tintilia is a small-berried, thick-skinned grape that ripens late and gives a deeply coloured wine with more structure than its alcohol suggests. The character people reach for is black pepper: sour cherry and blackberry underneath, dried herbs, tobacco and a peppery, slightly wild edge that has led to comparisons with northern Rhône Syrah, which is flattering and not entirely wrong. Tannins are firm and a little rustic in the plainer bottlings. The Rosato is worth knowing in its own right, a deep pink wine with real grip, in the southern Italian style rather than the pale Provençal one.

In the glass: Tintilia del Molise.

 Colour: Deep ruby with a purple rim, dense for a wine of moderate alcohol.

 Nose: Black pepper, sour cherry, blackberry, dried herbs, tobacco, a wild scrubby note.

 Palate: Medium to full body, fresh acidity, firm and slightly rustic tannin, a peppery finish.

 Alcohol: 12.5–14%; Riserva from 13%.

 Serve: 16–18 °C; drink the Rosso from three years, the Riserva from five.

 At the table: Cavatelli with lamb ragù, pampanella, grilled mutton, aged caciocavallo.

The growers.

The names to look for are few, and that is part of the point. Di Majo Norante at Campomarino is by far the largest and the one you are most likely to meet abroad, farming organically on the coastal hills and making Biferno, Tintilia and a good Falanghina. Cantine Cipressi, Claudio Cipressi, Tenimenti Grieco and Angelo D'Uva are among the small estates that pushed Tintilia into bottles with their names on them. Between them they make a few hundred thousand bottles a year, which is less than a single large Tuscan estate. Molise's wine problem is not quality; it is that there is barely any of it, and that almost none of it leaves.

Before you move on.

Tintilia is Molise's native red, nearly lost after the war and given its own DOC in 2011, needing at least 95% of the grape.

The appellation forces vineyards up to 200 metres or higher, and that altitude gives the wine its freshness and perfume.

Expect black pepper, sour cherry and firm tannin; the deep-pink Rosato is a serious wine, not a summer afterthought.
