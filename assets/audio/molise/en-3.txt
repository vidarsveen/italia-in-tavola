Caciocavallo and the transhumance routes.

The best cuts of the pig go into it, not the scraps: lean from the thigh, the loin and the shoulder. It is minced with the point of a knife in the coldest days of winter, with whole black peppercorns, coriander, ground chilli and wild fennel. It is packed into the caecum, which the village calls la zia, the aunt, and that gives it the shape of a beehive. It hangs at least six months, and the women of the house keep the recipe. The household that made the Signora di Conca Casale rarely ate it: it went to the doctor, the notary and the mayor.

All five of the royal tratturi cross this one small region. They are the grass drove roads that carried the flocks twice a year between the Apennine summer pastures and the winter plains of Puglia. About four hundred and fifty kilometres of them lie inside Molise, covering some four thousand hectares of a region of four and a half thousand square kilometres. A royal road was sixty paces across, a hundred and eleven metres; below it ran the tratturelli, of twenty or ten. Stones cut with the letters R.T., for Regio Tratturo, were set along the edges between 1574 and 1884. On the ground they survive as broad green strips, sometimes ploughed right to the edges, and Sabatino Moscati put it plainly: here the tratturi dictated the law of settlement and of movement.

The registers of the sheep customs at Foggia name the men who used them. Mattia Pizzella of Capracotta had 9,005 head entered against his name in 1740, and ten thousand by 1750. By 1780 the family was down to 2,610. The Cappella della Madonna di Loreto — a chapel — held 9,500 head in 1600 and 21,210 in 1700. A small owner ran up to two hundred sheep and a middling one up to two thousand, which is the scale those figures have to be read against. The registers survive because the animals were taxed.

The cheese of the tratturi is caciocavallo, and Molise's, from Agnone in the northern mountains, is the one with a reputation. It is a pasta filata cheese. The curd is heated and stretched, as mozzarella is, then shaped into a fat pear with a head, tied with a cord and hung over a beam in pairs. That is the usual explanation of the name, a cavallo, astride, though the dictionaries offer three others: the shape, a mark once stamped on the rind, and the Turkish kaşkaval. Agnone's is cow's milk. Young, at three months, it is springy, milky and mild; at a year it has gone firm and straw-yellow and savoury, with a piquancy that sharpens as it dries. It is a registered traditional product and sits in Slow Food's Ark of Taste, but it is not a DOP — though Caciocavallo Silano DOP does cover both Molise provinces.

Around it the region makes scamorza, the same stretched curd eaten within days or smoked over beech or oak. Its name comes either from scamozzare, to lop, or from capa mozza, cut-off head, which is exactly what it looks like. It is the one that goes on the grill, where it slumps and browns. And then there is burrino, which the region also calls manteca: a ball of butter wrapped inside a skin of stretched curd, so that butter would keep in a house with no cold room. It is a shepherd's refrigerator, and it is the cleverest thing in the Molise dairy.

The pasta is cavatelli, made from durum semolina and water with no egg, rolled into a rope, cut into pieces and dragged with two fingers or a knife into a small hollow shell. They belong to the same dragged family as Puglia's orecchiette and are built the same way, to hold a sauce rather than to be admired. In Molise that sauce is most often a slow lamb or mutton ragù, or beans, or simply garlic and chilli. On feast days the ragù has cooked all morning with a piece of pork in it.

The dish outsiders remember is pampanella, from San Martino in Pensilis near the coast. Belly, capocollo, leg and ribs are rubbed with sweet and hot dried red pepper, garlic, vinegar and salt. They are wrapped in vine leaves — the pampane the dish is named after — and baked until the meat is soft and the outside has gone brick red and slightly sticky. It is sold by the portion, wrapped in paper, and eaten with bread. The comune has given it a De.Co. with a written specification of its own, and it has been on the regional list of traditional products since 1999; the sagra is in August.

The other pork speciality is ventricina, and Molise's is not the one people expect. The version from Montenero di Bisaccia is made from the lean cuts — thigh, loin and shoulder — with lard, salt, chilli, paprika and fennel seed. It is packed into the bladder, dried by a fire and then aged. It is the leanest and longest-kept of the three ventricine of this coast. The soft one that is spread on bread is Abruzzese.

Molise digs about forty per cent of Italy's truffle harvest, and about eighty per cent of that leaves the region. The reason is documentary rather than commercial. The Molise truffle carries no DOP and no IGP, so nothing on it says where it came from, and it is sold as Alba's or Acqualagna's. The centre of the trade is San Pietro Avellana, a village of just over four hundred people high in the province of Isernia. It is the region's second product to lose its name on the way out, and this time no court was involved.

Mutton here is eaten as pezzata, and the name is the method: the animal is depezzata, cut into pieces, and boiled a long time with tomato, potato, chilli and wild herbs. The people who cook it say where it came from: the animals that did not survive the move between lowland and mountain pasture, cooked on the road by the men walking them. The mountains on both sides of this border solved that problem the same way. Capracotta's sagra is on the first Sunday of August, up on the Prato Gentile plateau at about 1,420 metres. Winter food below it is what a cold hill region would be expected to eat: beans and greens, polenta, mutton stewed or grilled, pork in every preserved form, and bread baked in quantity.

The Signora at the other end of the region is the same economy read from the other side. A household spent its best pork, its knife work and six months of hanging on something it then gave away.
