Caciocavallo and the transhumance routes.

Grass roads a hundred metres wide, walked by millions of sheep for two thousand years, and the cheese, pasta and cured pork of the people who walked them. Molise's kitchen is shepherds' food, and it says so.

To understand what Molise eats you have to understand the tratturi. These are the drove roads of the central Apennines, grass highways along which flocks moved twice a year between the summer pastures of Abruzzo and Molise and the winter plains of Puglia. They are not paths. A royal tratturo was fixed at 111 metres wide, sixty Neapolitan paces, and the longest ran well over two hundred kilometres. Molise sits in the middle of the network, and four of the five great tratturi cross it. The cheese, the pasta, the cured pork and the mutton of this region are all, in one way or another, food that could be made, carried or eaten on the move.

The drove roads.

Transhumance here is Roman and older, but its bureaucratic peak came under Aragonese rule: in 1447 Alfonso of Aragon reorganised the sheep customs, the Dogana della Mena delle Pecore, and moved it to Foggia, taxing every animal that came down from the mountains. For four centuries the tratturi were the region's main economic fact. The great routes are named for their endpoints, and they read like a map of the south: L'Aquila–Foggia, Celano–Foggia, Castel di Sangro–Lucera, Pescasseroli–Candela. Since 2006 Italy has been pressing the case for the tratturi as a UNESCO World Heritage site, so far without success. On the ground they survive as broad green strips, sometimes ploughed to the edges, sometimes still walked.

Caciocavallo.

The cheese of the tratturi is caciocavallo, and Molise's version, from Agnone in the northern mountains, is the one with a reputation. It is a pasta filata cheese: the curd is heated and stretched, as mozzarella is, then shaped into a fat teardrop, tied with a cord and hung over a beam in pairs, which is where the name comes from, a cavallo, astride. Young, at three months, it is springy, milky and mild. At a year it turns firm, straw-yellow and savoury, with a piquancy that grows sharper as it dries. It was the perfect shepherds' cheese: made from the milk to hand, needing no cave or special cellar, and keeping for months on a hook.

Alongside it Molise makes scamorza, the same stretched curd eaten fresh within days or lightly smoked, and a range of small sheep's cheeses. Scamorza is the one that goes on the grill, where it slumps and browns and is eaten with bread.

Cavatelli, and pork with paprika.

The pasta is cavatelli, made from durum semolina and water with no egg, rolled into a rope, cut into pieces and dragged with two fingers or a knife into a small hollow shell. They are cousins of Puglia's orecchiette, and like them they are built to hold a sauce; in Molise that sauce is most often a slow lamb or mutton ragù, or beans, or simply garlic and chilli. On feast days they come with a ragù that has cooked all morning with a piece of pork in it.

The dish outsiders remember is pampanella, from San Martino in Pensilis near the coast. Pieces of pork, usually the cheaper cuts and often the ribs, are rubbed with sweet and hot dried red pepper, garlic and vinegar and baked in a wood oven until the meat is soft and the outside is brick red and slightly sticky. It is sold by the portion, wrapped in paper, and eaten with bread. Ventricina, a coarse cured sausage flavoured with the same red pepper and fennel, is the other pork speciality; the version around Montenero is softer and more spreadable than the Abruzzese one.

Truffles, oil and the winter table.

Molise is one of Italy's quiet truffle regions. The woods around San Pietro Avellana, high in the province of Isernia, produce a serious quantity of white truffle, and the town holds a fair for it in early November and another for the summer truffle in August. Because Molise has no marketing machine, a good deal of what is dug here has historically been sold on and sold under other regions' names, which is a complaint you will hear from anyone in the trade there. The region also presses good olive oil from the hills behind the coast, and grows the peppers that end up dried and ground into everything.

Winter food is what you would expect of a cold hill region: beans and greens, polenta, lamb and mutton grilled or stewed, pork in every preserved form, and bread baked in quantity. It is not a subtle kitchen and it does not pretend to be. It is the food of people who walked a long way behind animals, and it is best eaten with a glass of something peppery from the hills above.
