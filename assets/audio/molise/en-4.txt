Campobasso and the hill villages.

Four shoeprints, two pairs, are pressed into a clay roof tile at the sanctuary of Pietrabbondante. Two women put them there about a hundred years before Christ, while the clay was still wet, and wrote their names beside them. Detfri wrote in Oscan, the Samnite language. Amica wrote in Latin, and declined her owner's name with an Oscan genitive, so she had both. Both were almost certainly slaves: Amica is a common slave name, and Detfri is found nowhere else. The tile is two thirds of a metre by nearly a metre, and it was probably made when a portico beside a temple was re-roofed. Nothing else about either of them is known.

Before Rome this was Samnium. The Samnites fought Rome in three wars across the fourth and third centuries BC, and at the Caudine Forks in 321 they trapped a Roman army. Their great sanctuary stands high on a shoulder of mountain with a view over half the region. The temple and the theatre below it went up in the last decades of the second century BC, and one of the Samnite magistrates who paid for the temple is named in the stone: L. Statiis Klar.

The seats are the thing. Each one is cut from a single block, free-standing, with a backrest tilted slightly back to follow the line of a spine. The dividers between the wedges are carved as eagles, with wings and talons. Franco Valente calls them unprecedented in Mediterranean theatre architecture, and gives the reason: this was an assembly building in the shape of a theatre, and the people in it sat for hours.

Sulla ended it. The Samnites backed the losing side in the civil war, the sanctuary was confiscated after his victory around 82 to 80 BC, and it was never restored. The Oscan inscriptions were still there to be found, some of them built into the walls of the village church.

Twenty kilometres south, at Altilia near Sepino, stands a small Roman town that has kept nearly all of its plan. Saepinum was a municipium of no great importance. Tiberius and Drusus paid for its walls out of their own pockets between 2 BC and AD 4, and said so in an inscription over the gate, flanked by two carved German prisoners in chains.

The drove road runs under the Porta Bojano, the north-west gate, and four metres up the pilaster beside it someone carved a letter. It is not an emperor's ruling, as it is often called. It is a dossier of three letters, posted in reverse order. The one at the top is from the two praetorian prefects, Bassaeus Rufus and Macrinius Vindex, writing between AD 169 and 172 to the magistrates of Saepinum. They tell them to stop injuring the contractors of the sheep flocks. Below it is the request that prompted theirs, asking that they write to Saepinum and Bovianum alike. The magistrates and the road police had been stopping the flocks as they passed, claiming that the pack animals were stolen and the shepherds were runaway slaves.

The reason the prefects give is money. Their letter turns on the phrase magna fisci iniuria, grave damage to the treasury, and the request below it asks only that the treasury be kept whole. The flocks were imperial property and so were the men driving them — these were the animals whose milk became the region's cheese. The shepherds on that road were protected because somebody owned them, and the notice was cut where anyone stopping a flock would have to look up and read it.

In the ninth century the people of Saepinum moved uphill to Castellum Sepini, the modern Sepino, for safety. What saved the ruins was what happened next. From the eighteenth century farmers came back down and built houses out of the robbed stone, inside the theatre, following the curve of the seating so exactly that the buildings repeat its plan. There are hearths and bread ovens in them, and the site's Antiquarium is housed in them today. Systematic excavation began at the Porta Bojano in 1950, under the superintendent Valerio Cianfarani.

The regional capital is a town of about forty-seven thousand people that climbs a hill, with a nineteenth-century grid at the bottom and a tight medieval quarter above it. It is the second smallest regional capital in Italy; only Aosta is smaller. On the summit stands Castello Monforte, rebuilt in 1458 after the Sannio earthquake of 1456 had left the citadel in rubble. From its walls you can see the Matese on one side and, on a clear day, the Adriatic on the other.

The town's own trade was steel. Blacksmiths were working here from the fifteenth century, and Nicola di Monforte is said to have brought some of them from France. Scissors and blades are still made in Campobasso. The documented knife town, though, is Frosolone, in the province of Isernia. It sits on a tratturo, and it is one of the communities named in Italy's UNESCO transhumance nomination: a town that made knives and walked sheep.

The ironworkers left one other thing. At Corpus Christi the town carries the Misteri, thirteen wrought-iron structures on wooden bases. Each branches upward, with a harness at the end of every arm for a child dressed as an angel or a saint. The sculptor Paolo Saverio Di Zinno designed them around 1740 and the town's smiths built them, and the rhythm of the carriers makes the iron sway, so that the children appear to fly.

In the northern mountains, Agnone has been casting bells for as long as anyone can document. A bell of about two quintals was made for a church near Frosinone in 1339 by Nicodemo Marinelli, called Campanarus, and the record of it is kept in the foundry's own museum. The Pontificia Fonderia Marinelli is run today by the twenty-seventh generation of the same family, who say themselves that the centuries before 1339 are tradition and not record. The bells are still cast in clay moulds, by hand, and the mould has to be broken to release the bell, so no two are quite alike.

Molise's thirty-six kilometres of Adriatic end at Termoli, a fishing port whose old town sits behind a wall on a promontory. The Castello Svevo out on the headland is an eleventh-century structure, fortified a century later under Frederick II. The cathedral behind it is Apulian Romanesque of the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, its upper facade rebuilt after the same earthquake of 1456 that levelled the citadel at Campobasso.

Termoli's evening dish is u' bredette, the soup of whatever did not sell: weever, gurnard, small hake, ray, mantis shrimp and cuttlefish, with tomato, garlic and sweet green pepper. That pepper is the thread running up this coast, because the brodetto at Vasto, up the coast in Abruzzo, is built on the same three things. Termoli's difference is that the larger fish go into the pot cut into pieces rather than whole.

Along this coast and southwards into Puglia stand the trabucchi, timber platforms on stilts reaching out over the water with a net slung between two long arms. A trabucco is a machine for fishing without a boat: you lower the net, wait, and winch it up. They are not ancient. The first Molisan one is credited to Felice Marinucci, a Termoli fisherman who saw one near Ancona around 1850 and built his own. A notarial deed of 1793 for coastal land is argued to push their presence back a few decades further. Two are left at Termoli, one at the port and one at Marina di Sant'Antonio, and almost nobody fishes from them now. A regional law of 2020 protects them as heritage rather than as gear.

From Termoli the boats go out to the Tremiti islands. They belong to the province of Foggia in Puglia, but they are reached from Molise all year, in an hour and a quarter, while the Puglian ports sail only in season.

Nothing much came here after the Samnites, which is why the theatre is still legible and why there are farmhouses standing inside it. The hill villages emptied slowly instead of being rebuilt, and that is the same fact from the other side. Two women pressed their shoes into a wet tile at Pietrabbondante and signed their names beside the prints. The feet are still in the clay.
