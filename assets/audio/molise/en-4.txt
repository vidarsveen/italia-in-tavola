Campobasso and the hill villages.

A castle above the capital, a Roman town nobody destroyed, a Samnite theatre with seats shaped for the human back, a bell foundry that has been in one family for a thousand years, and a fishing machine on the Adriatic.

Molise's monuments are not famous, and that is the best thing about them. There are no queues at Saepinum, no coaches at Pietrabbondante, and the man showing you the bell foundry at Agnone may well be called Marinelli. What the region has is a set of places that survived precisely because nothing much happened here after the Romans left: a walled Roman town used as a farmyard for centuries, a Samnite sanctuary abandoned after a war, hill villages that emptied slowly rather than being rebuilt.

Campobasso and its castle.

Campobasso, the regional capital, is a town of about 45,000 people that climbs a hill, with a nineteenth-century grid at the bottom and a tight medieval quarter above it. On the summit stands Castello Monforte, rebuilt in 1458 by Nicola II Monforte after an earthquake, on foundations that are older, and from its walls you can see the Matese on one side and, on a clear day, the Adriatic on the other. The town is known in Italy for two things: a school of cutlers who made scissors and blades from the fifteenth century, and the Misteri, a Corpus Christi procession in which children are strapped into iron frames and carried through the streets as angels and saints, apparently floating. The frames were designed by a local sculptor, Paolo Saverio Di Zinno, in the eighteenth century, and thirteen of them still go out each June.

Saepinum: the Roman town that nobody wanted.

Twenty kilometres south, at Altilia near Sepino, is one of the most complete small Roman towns in Italy. Saepinum was a municipium of no great importance, walled in the years around the birth of Christ with a circuit of thirty-five round towers and four gates. It has a forum, a basilica, a theatre, baths, a market and a paved main street with the wheel ruts still in it. It survives because it was never worth destroying: the town faded in the early Middle Ages, and from the eighteenth century farmers moved back in and built their houses against the Roman walls, using the ruins as barns rather than as a quarry. Excavation from the 1950s onwards uncovered the rest.

The detail that connects Saepinum to the rest of this module is on the Porta Bojano, the gate at the north-west, which opens directly onto the line of the tratturo. Carved beside it is an imperial ruling that settled a dispute: the town's officials had been stopping the migrating shepherds and accusing them of stealing, and the shepherds complained to Rome. The reply, inscribed on the gate so that everybody could read it, told the magistrates to leave the flocks alone. It is a two-thousand-year-old note about sheep on a road that was still being walked in living memory.

Pietrabbondante and the Samnites.

Before Rome, this was Samnium, and the Samnites fought Rome in three long wars in the fourth and third centuries BC and came closer to breaking it than anyone before Hannibal. Their great sanctuary sits at Pietrabbondante, high on a shoulder of mountain with a view over half the region. There is a temple and, below it, a small theatre of the second century BC whose stone seats are cut with a curved back and a shaped seat, an ergonomic detail you will not find in a Roman theatre. Inscriptions here are in Oscan, the Samnite language, written in its own alphabet; fragments of them were later built into the walls of the village church. The sanctuary was abandoned after the Social War, around 95 BC, and never reused, which is why it is still legible.

Key facts.

Castello Monforte, Campobasso, rebuilt 1458; the Misteri procession uses eighteenth-century iron frames designed by Paolo Saverio Di Zinno.

Saepinum (Altilia, Sepino): a walled Roman town of about 12 hectares with 35 towers and four gates, preserved because farmers reoccupied it rather than quarried it.

Pietrabbondante: the main Samnite sanctuary, with a 2nd-century BC theatre and Oscan inscriptions, abandoned around 95 BC.

The Marinelli bell foundry at Agnone has cast bells since at least 1339, claims origins around the year 1000, and was granted the use of the papal arms by Pius XI in 1924.

The bells of Agnone.

In the northern mountains, Agnone has been making bells for as long as anyone can document. The Pontificia Fonderia Marinelli is run by the same family that ran it in the Middle Ages; documented casting goes back to 1339, and the family traces its origins to around the year 1000, which would make it one of the oldest family firms in the world. The claim is theirs and is repeated everywhere, but the early centuries rest on tradition rather than documents. What is certain is the work: bells from this small Molise town hang at Montecassino, at the sanctuary of Pompeii, in the cathedral of Buenos Aires and at the leaning tower of Pisa, and in 1924 Pius XI granted the family the right to put the papal coat of arms on them. They are still cast in clay moulds, by hand, and the foundry keeps a museum.

The coast, and a machine for catching fish.

Molise's thirty-five kilometres of Adriatic end at Termoli, a fishing port whose old town sits behind a wall on a promontory, with a Swabian castle on the point and a Romanesque cathedral inside. Along this coast and southwards into Puglia stand the trabucchi, timber platforms on stilts reaching out over the water with a net slung between two long arms. A trabucco is a machine for fishing without a boat: you lower the net, wait, and winch it up. They are centuries old, they look like something washed up from a shipwreck, and several still work. From Termoli the boats go out to the Tremiti islands, which belong to Puglia but are easiest to reach from Molise, which is a fair summary of the region's position generally.

Before you move on.

Campobasso is Italy's smallest regional capital, crowned by Castello Monforte of 1458 and known for its Misteri procession.

Saepinum survived because farmers lived in it, and its Porta Bojano carries an imperial ruling protecting the migrating shepherds.

Pietrabbondante is the Samnite sanctuary with a theatre of shaped stone seats; Agnone casts bells; Termoli fishes from trabucchi.
