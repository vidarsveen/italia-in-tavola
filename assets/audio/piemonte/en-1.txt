Nebbiolo and the Langhe hills.

One grape, one fog, one range of hills. Why Nebbiolo grows almost nowhere else, and how the Langhe became Italy's answer to Burgundy.

Drive south from Turin in late October and the plain gives way to a sea of hills that look drawn with a soft pencil. Every ridge is combed with vines, every summit has a village and a castle, and in the mornings the valleys fill with a white fog, the nebbia, that lifts by noon. This is the Langhe, and the fog gave its name to the grape that made it famous: Nebbiolo.

A difficult grape in the right place.

Nebbiolo is one of the world's great wine grapes and one of the most stubborn. It buds early, so spring frost is a danger; it ripens very late, in the last weeks of October when the fog has already arrived; and it wants a specific soil, the calcareous marl of these hills, laid down when the Po plain was a sea. Move it fifty kilometres and it sulks. Outside Piemonte there are only a few serious plantings in the world, in Lombardy's Valtellina and a handful of experiments abroad. The Langhe, the Roero across the Tanaro river and the Alto Piemonte around Gattinara are its home, and that is nearly the whole list.

What it gives, when it works, is a wine that looks light and drinks big. The colour is pale garnet, almost translucent, and misleads people expecting a dark, heavy red. The nose is where Nebbiolo announces itself: roses, tar, dried cherry, liquorice, a whiff of truffle and forest floor. On the palate come the tannins, firm and drying when young, and the acidity that keeps the wine alive for decades. Locals describe it as tar and roses, and once you have smelled it you will recognise it anywhere.

Hills with names.

The Langhe are not one vineyard but hundreds of small, named hillsides, and their names matter as much as the producer's. The Burgundian idea of the cru, a single site with its own character, took root here in the 1960s and was made official in 2010, when Barolo's vineyards were mapped into 181 Menzioni Geografiche Aggiuntive, additional geographic mentions that can appear on the label. Cannubi, Brunate, Rocche dell'Annunziata, Bussia, Vigna Rionda, Ginestra: to a Piedmontese these are as evocative as Chambertin or Montrachet to a Burgundian.

Key facts.

The Langhe, Roero and Monferrato vineyard landscapes have been a UNESCO World Heritage site since 2014.

Nebbiolo covers only about 6% of Piemonte's vineyard area; Barbera is the most planted grape.

Barolo and Barbaresco were among Italy's first DOCG wines, promoted in 1980.

Two soils shape the wines: older, compact Helvetian marl in the east (Serralunga, Monforte) gives power; younger Tortonian marl in the west (La Morra, Barolo) gives perfume.

The other grapes of the hills.

Nebbiolo takes the best south-facing slopes; everything else takes the rest. Barbera, juicy, dark and high in acidity, is the wine Piedmontese families actually drink on a weeknight, and around Asti and Nizza Monferrato it is made with real ambition. Dolcetto, "the little sweet one", is anything but sweet: a soft, purple, almond-bitter red for drinking young with a plate of salumi. And from Gavi, near the Ligurian border, comes Cortese, the crisp white that the fishermen of Genoa used to drink with their catch.

In the glass: Nebbiolo (Langhe or Nebbiolo d'Alba).

 Colour: Pale to medium garnet, orange rim with age.

 Nose: Rose, tar, sour cherry, liquorice, dried herbs.

 Palate: Light in colour but full in structure: high acidity, firm tannin, long finish.

 Alcohol: 13.5–14.5%.

 Serve: 16–18 °C; decant young wines for an hour.

 At the table: Tajarin with butter and truffle, braised beef, aged Castelmagno cheese.

How to read the hills.

Stand on the terrace at La Morra, the highest village in the zone, and the whole Barolo amphitheatre is below you: Barolo village in the bowl, Castiglione Falletto on its spur, Serralunga's castle on the far ridge, Monforte to the south. Each village has a character, and the next reading goes through them one by one. For now, remember the principle: in the Langhe, where the grape grows matters as much as who grows it, and the fog is part of the recipe.

Before you move on.

Nebbiolo is a late-ripening, site-obsessed grape that thrives almost only in Piemonte's marl hills.

Pale colour, big structure: rose and tar on the nose, firm tannin and high acidity on the palate.

The Langhe are mapped into named crus; the village and vineyard on the label tell you what to expect.
