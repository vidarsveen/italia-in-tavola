Nebbiolo and the Langhe hills.

Early in spring a clear night can still bring frost to the Langhe, and by then Nebbiolo is already in bud. Its grapes are picked late, in the last weeks of October, when the valleys south of Alba fill with fog every morning and clear by noon. The fog is nebbia, and most people take the grape's name from it. Others say the name comes from the grey bloom that covers the ripe berries like a mist, and a few say it comes from nobile, noble. Nobody can settle it. The hills themselves are the Langhe. Every ridge is lined with vines, and nearly every summit carries a village and a castle.

Nebbiolo is counted among the world's great wine grapes, and it is also among the most stubborn. A grape that ripens so late needs all the autumn sun it can get. So in the Langhe Nebbiolo is given the best slopes, the ones that face south, and the other vines get what is left. The grape that made Piedmont's name covers less than a tenth of its vineyards, while Barbera covers nearly a third. Nebbiolo is also particular about soil. It wants the calcareous marl of these hills, a mix of clay and limestone laid down when the Po plain was a sea. Planted fifty kilometres away, it rarely gives the same wine. Outside Piemonte it has done well in only a few places: the Valtellina in Lombardy, Donnas at the foot of the Aosta valley, and a handful of experiments abroad.

The wine looks light and is not. In the glass it is pale garnet, almost translucent, which misleads anyone expecting a dark, heavy red. The smell gives it away, with roses, tar and dried cherry, and sometimes a hint of truffle and forest floor. English wine writers have called it tar and roses since a book on Barolo took that title in 1990. Then come the tannins, firm and drying while the wine is young, and an acidity high enough to keep it alive for decades.

Every one of these hillsides has a name, and in the Langhe the name of the hill can matter as much as the name of the grower. Until the 1960s a Barolo was usually blended from grapes grown on several slopes. In 1961 two houses broke with that. Prunotto bottled the wine of the Bussia hill on its own, and Vietti did the same with Rocche di Castiglione. It was the Burgundian idea of the cru, a single site with a character of its own. Other growers followed, and in 1976 Renato Ratti published a map of the vineyards. In 2010 the zone was mapped officially into 181 named sites, the Menzioni Geografiche Aggiuntive, whose names may be printed on the label. To a Piedmontese, Cannubi, Brunate and Vigna Rionda sound the way Chambertin or Montrachet sound to a Burgundian.

The slopes Nebbiolo does not get go to the grapes the Piedmontese drink every day. Barbera is dark and juicy, with high acidity, and it is the bottle a family opens on a weeknight. Around Asti and Nizza Monferrato it is now made with real ambition. The name Dolcetto means the little sweet one, and the wine is not sweet at all. Soft and purple, with a bitter-almond finish, Dolcetto is drunk young with a plate of salumi. Among the whites there is Cortese, grown around Gavi near the Ligurian border, and the fishermen of Genoa used to drink its crisp wine with their catch.

La Morra is the highest village in the Barolo zone, and from its terrace the whole amphitheatre lies below you. Barolo village sits in its bowl, and Serralunga's castle stands on the far ridge. Each village makes a different wine from the same grape. On an October morning the bowl is full of fog, and only the towers and the church roofs stand out of it. By noon the fog has lifted, and the pickers are in the rows.
