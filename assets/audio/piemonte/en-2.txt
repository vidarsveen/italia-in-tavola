Barolo and Barbaresco, village by village.

Two wines from the same grape, made a few hills apart: the king and the queen, the traditionalists and the modernists, and what the village name on the label tells you.

Barolo is called il re dei vini, il vino dei re, the king of wines and the wine of kings, a phrase coined when the House of Savoy took to it in the nineteenth century. Barbaresco, made from the same Nebbiolo grape on hills across the river Tanaro, is by tradition the queen: a little softer, a little earlier to drink, and for a long time unfairly treated as the lesser of the two. Learning to tell them apart, and then to tell the villages within them apart, is the heart of any Piemonte wine education.

The king: Barolo.

The Barolo zone covers eleven villages south-west of Alba, five of them essential. La Morra and Barolo itself, on the younger, sandier marl to the west, give the most perfumed and approachable wines. Serralunga d'Alba and Monforte d'Alba, on older, denser soils to the east, give the darkest, most tannic and longest-lived. Castiglione Falletto, between them, has a bit of both. By law Barolo must age at least 38 months before release, 18 of them in wood; a Riserva waits 62 months. In practice the best need ten years to open and can live for forty.

The queen: Barbaresco.

Barbaresco is smaller, just four villages north-east of Alba, and the hills are lower and closer to the Tanaro, which moderates the climate. The wines ripen a little earlier and are released a year sooner (26 months of ageing, 9 in wood). They are not lighter so much as more elegant: the same rose and tar, the same structure, but rounder and more fragrant. Barbaresco, Neive and Treiso are the villages to know, and the crus of Asili, Rabajà, Martinenga and Santo Stefano are as celebrated as anything in Barolo.

Reading the label.

Barolo DOCG or Barbaresco DOCG: the zone. Both must be 100% Nebbiolo.

A village name (Serralunga d'Alba, Neive): the wine comes from that commune.

A cru name (Cannubi, Rabajà): a single mapped vineyard, listed among the official additional geographic mentions.

Riserva: longer ageing before release.

Vintage matters more here than almost anywhere in Italy: cool years give lean, long-lived wines, warm years give richer ones.

The Barolo wars.

In the 1980s and 1990s the Langhe split into two camps. The traditionalists fermented for weeks on the skins and aged the wine for years in large old Slavonian oak casks, the botti, making austere wines that needed a decade. The modernists, influenced by Burgundy and by the export market, shortened fermentations, used small new French barriques, and made darker, softer, oakier wines that could be drunk young. Families argued, neighbours stopped speaking, and journalists chose sides. Thirty years on the argument has largely dissolved: most producers use some of each, new oak is out of fashion, and the wines have settled into a modern-classic style that is cleaner than the old Barolo but recognisably Nebbiolo.

Bottles for the rest of the week.

Nobody in the Langhe drinks Barolo on a Tuesday. Langhe Nebbiolo is the young, unoaked version from the same hills, released after a year and drunk for its perfume. Nebbiolo d'Alba and Roero come from the sandier soils across the river and are lighter still. And Barbera d'Alba or d'Asti is the house red: dark fruit, bright acid, low tannin, made for tajarin with ragù.

In the glass: Barolo.

 Colour: Medium garnet with a brick-orange rim.

 Nose: Rose, tar, dried cherry, leather, truffle, liquorice.

 Palate: Full body, firm drying tannin, high acidity, very long finish.

 Alcohol: 14–15%.

 Serve: 17–18 °C in a large glass; decant for 1–2 hours if under ten years old.

 At the table: Brasato al Barolo, roast game, agnolotti del plin, aged cheeses.

A pairing that closes the circle.

The classic dish is brasato al Barolo: a piece of beef marinated overnight in a bottle of Barolo with onion, carrot, celery, cloves and bay, then braised for three hours until the sauce is dark and glossy. It is cooked with the same wine it is served with, which is the Piedmontese way of saying that food and wine here were never meant to be separated.

Before you move on.

Barolo: eleven villages, 38 months' ageing, power from the east (Serralunga, Monforte), perfume from the west (La Morra, Barolo).

Barbaresco: four villages, 26 months' ageing, rounder and earlier, no less serious.

The label's village and cru names tell you the style; Langhe Nebbiolo and Barbera are the everyday bottles.
