Barolo and Barbaresco, village by village.

Until the 1830s Barolo was a sweet wine, and often a slightly fizzy one. Nebbiolo was picked so late that by the time the grapes were in the vat, the cellars of the Langhe had turned cold. The yeast slowed down and stopped with sugar still in the wine, and in the warmth of spring the fermentation started again. In 1836 Camillo Benso di Cavour, who would later be the first prime minister of Italy, took on a general named Paolo Francesco Staglieno to run the cellars of his family's estate at Grinzane. Staglieno fermented the wine until it was dry. The story often told gives the credit to a French oenologist, Louis Oudart. The records point to Staglieno instead, and Oudart turns out to have been a wine merchant with no documented link to the change. Dry, the wine needed years before it could be drunk, and in the nineteenth century the Savoy court took to it. The phrase coined for it then is still used: il re dei vini, il vino dei re, the king of wines and the wine of kings.

The village has a legend of its own. Giulia Falletti, the Marchesa of Barolo, owned the castle that stands in it. She is said to have sent King Charles Albert 325 casks of her wine, one for every day of the year that was not Lent. No document for the gift has been found.

The Barolo zone covers eleven villages south-west of Alba, and five of them are its core. They stand on two kinds of marl. La Morra and Barolo itself are on the younger, sandier marl to the west, and they give the most perfumed and approachable wines. Serralunga d'Alba and Monforte d'Alba are on older, denser soil to the east. Their wines are the darkest and the most tannic, and they live the longest. Castiglione Falletto, between the two sides, has something of both. The law makes every Barolo wait more than three years before it is sold. In practice the best need ten years to open, and they can live for forty.

Across the Tanaro, north-east of Alba, Barbaresco has long been called the queen to Barolo's king, and for a long time it was treated as the lesser wine. For most of its history the village grew Nebbiolo without making a wine of its own name, because it sold its grapes to the makers of Barolo. Domizio Cavazza changed that. He came from Modena, and in 1881 he became director of the royal school of oenology in Alba. In 1894 he founded a cooperative cellar in Barbaresco with nine growers, and it made the first wines sold under the village's name. The cooperative closed in the 1920s. In 1958 the parish priest, Don Fiorino Marengo, gathered nineteen growers into a new one, the Produttori del Barbaresco.

The zone is small, and Barbaresco, Neive and Treiso are its main villages. Its hills are lower than Barolo's and closer to the river, which softens the climate, so the grapes ripen a little earlier. The wine can be released a year sooner. It has the same roses and tar and the same structure, but it is rounder and more fragrant. Its best vineyards, Asili, Rabajà and Martinenga among them, are as sought after as any in Barolo.

In the 1980s and 1990s the Langhe split into two camps. The traditionalists fermented the grapes for weeks on their skins, and then aged the wine for years in large casks of old Slavonian oak, the botti. What came out was austere wine that needed a decade in the cellar. The modernists, influenced by Burgundy and by the export market, shortened the fermentation and aged the wine in small new French barrels, barriques. Their Barolo was darker, softer and oakier, and it could be drunk young. Elio Altare, one of the first of them, came home from Burgundy and took a chainsaw to his father's old casks. By his own account that was in 1976, and his father disinherited him. Journalists chose sides as well. Bartolo Mascarello never gave up the big casks, and in the late 1990s he drew his own labels by hand. One of them read No Barrique, No Berlusconi.

Today the argument has mostly faded. Most producers now use some of each method, and new oak has gone out of fashion. Barolo has settled into a style that is cleaner than the old wine and still plainly Nebbiolo.

Nobody in the Langhe drinks Barolo on a Tuesday. A weeknight bottle is a Langhe Nebbiolo, the young version from the same hills, kept out of oak, released after a year and drunk for its perfume. The sandier soils across the river, in the Roero and the Nebbiolo d'Alba zone, give a lighter wine still. The house red is a Barbera, from Alba or from Asti. It has dark fruit, bright acidity and very little tannin, and it is poured with tajarin under a meat ragù.

The dish that goes with it is brasato al Barolo. A piece of beef is left overnight in a bottle of Barolo with onion, carrot, celery, cloves and bay. The next day it braises in that wine for three hours, until the sauce is dark and glossy. A second bottle of the same Barolo goes onto the table.
