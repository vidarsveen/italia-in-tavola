Truffles, tajarin and bagna cauda.

The kitchen of the Langhe is built on butter, egg yolks, veal and one very expensive fungus. The dishes to know and the season to eat them.

Piemonte does not cook like the rest of Italy. There is butter where the south has olive oil, egg yolks by the dozen, veal in almost everything, and a taste for the long, slow and rich that comes from Alpine winters and a century of French influence at the Savoy court. Then, for two months each autumn, there is the truffle, and the whole region reorganises itself around it.

The white truffle of Alba.

Tuber magnatum Pico, the white truffle, grows underground on the roots of oaks, poplars and willows in the damp woods of the Langhe, Roero and Monferrato. It cannot be cultivated; it is found, between late September and December, by a trifolau and his dog, usually at night and always in secret. The best specimens sell at the Alba truffle fair for several thousand euros a kilo. Its smell is the point: garlic, honey, hay, wet earth and something that is only itself. It is never cooked. It is shaved, with a special slicer, over hot food at the table, and the heat releases the aroma.

The classic vehicle is tajarin, the Piedmontese tagliolini, a pasta so rich in egg yolk that it is deep gold before it meets the butter. Dressed with melted butter and a few grams of shaved truffle, it is the dish that people cross Europe to eat in October. The second is a simple fried egg with truffle shaved on top, which is what the locals order. The black truffle, Tuber melanosporum, grows here too but belongs to winter and to cooking; the white one is eaten raw or not at all.

Agnolotti and the Sunday table.

Agnolotti del plin are the region's ravioli: tiny parcels of roast meat, usually a mix of veal, pork and rabbit with cabbage, pinched closed with a plin, a pinch of the fingers. They are served in the roasting juices, or in butter and sage, or, the old way, in a napkin with nothing at all, so that the filling can be tasted. A Sunday lunch in the Langhe still runs to several courses: a plate of vitello tonnato to start, cold sliced veal under a sauce of tuna, capers and egg yolk that sounds odd and tastes wonderful; then agnolotti; then brasato or a bollito misto, the boiled meats served with salsa verde; then cheese; then bonet, the chocolate and amaretto pudding.

Key facts.

The Alba white truffle fair runs every weekend from early October to early December.

Tajarin are cut by hand from a sheet of egg pasta into strands about 2 mm wide.

Piemonte has more DOP cheeses than any other region: Castelmagno, Raschera, Bra, Toma, Robiola di Roccaverano and others.

Hazelnuts from the Langhe (Nocciola Piemonte IGP, the Tonda Gentile variety) are the base of gianduja.

Bagna cauda: the winter ritual.

When the truffle season ends, the region turns to its most sociable dish. Bagna cauda is a hot sauce of garlic, salted anchovies and olive oil, cooked slowly until the garlic melts, sometimes softened with milk or butter, and kept warm in a terracotta pot over a candle. Around it go raw vegetables, cardoons above all, but also peppers, celery, cabbage leaves and Jerusalem artichokes, and everyone dips. It comes from the poorest kitchens: anchovies were the cheap preserved fish that came up over the mountains from Liguria, and the sauce was a way to make a table of winter vegetables into a feast. In the villages there are still bagna cauda nights that go on until the pot is empty.

What to drink with it.

Truffle dishes want a wine that will not fight the aroma: a mature Nebbiolo, whose own truffle and forest notes echo the fungus, or a Langhe Nebbiolo if the bottle is young. Agnolotti and roasts are Barolo and Barbaresco territory. Vitello tonnato and the antipasti take a white, Arneis from the Roero or a Gavi. And bagna cauda, with all that garlic and anchovy, needs a young Barbera or Dolcetto, chilled a little, and plenty of it.

Before you move on.

The white truffle is found, not farmed, in October and November, and is shaved raw over hot food.

Tajarin, agnolotti del plin, vitello tonnato and brasato are the core of the Langhe table.

Bagna cauda is the region's communal winter dish: anchovy, garlic, oil and raw vegetables.
