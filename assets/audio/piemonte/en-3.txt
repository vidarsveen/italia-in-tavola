Truffles, tajarin and bagna cauda.

The trifolau goes out with his dog, traditionally at night, so that nobody can follow him to his places in the damp woods of the Langhe, the Roero and the Monferrato. Since 1985 Italian law has said it must be a dog. Pigs can find truffles too, but they tear up the ground to get at them. When the dog stops and scratches at the foot of a tree, the man digs out what it has found. It is the white truffle of Alba, Tuber magnatum Pico, and nobody has ever managed to farm it.

It grows underground on the roots of oaks, poplars, willows and hazels. The season opens on the first of October, and the truffle fair in Alba fills the autumn weekends. The best pieces sell there for several thousand euros a kilo. What the buyers pay for is the smell, of garlic, honey, hay and wet earth, and of something that is only the truffle's own. It is never cooked. It is shaved thin with a special slicer over hot food at the table, and the heat of the food releases the aroma. The wine for it is a mature Nebbiolo, whose own notes of truffle and forest floor echo the fungus, or a Langhe Nebbiolo if the bottle is young.

The truffle is usually shaved over tajarin, the Piedmontese tagliolini. The dough takes up to forty egg yolks for a kilo of flour, so the pasta is deep gold before it meets the butter. Dressed with melted butter and a few grams of truffle, it is the dish people cross Europe to eat in October. The locals are just as likely to order a fried egg with truffle shaved over it. The black truffle, Tuber melanosporum, grows here too, but it belongs to winter and is cooked. The white one is eaten raw or not at all.

Piemonte does not cook like the rest of Italy. It cooks with butter where the south uses olive oil, and it uses egg yolks by the dozen and veal in almost everything. It likes food that is slow and rich, and that taste comes from long Alpine winters and from a century of French influence at the Savoy court. A Sunday lunch in the Langhe still runs to several courses, and it shows the whole of that kitchen in order.

It opens with vitello tonnato, cold sliced veal under a sauce of tuna, capers and egg yolk, which sounds odd and tastes wonderful. With it and the other antipasti goes a white, an Arneis from the Roero or a Gavi. Next come agnolotti del plin, the region's ravioli. They are tiny parcels of roast meat, usually veal, pork and rabbit with cabbage, and each is closed with a plin, a pinch of the fingers. They are served in the juices of the roast or in butter and sage. The old way was to serve them in a napkin with nothing on them at all, so that the filling could be tasted. The meat is a brasato, beef braised in red wine, or a bollito misto, boiled meats with a green sauce, and Barolo or Barbaresco has been poured since the pasta. Cheese follows, and bonet, a pudding of chocolate and amaretti, ends the meal.

In winter the region turns to its most sociable dish. Bagna cauda means hot bath. Garlic is cooked slowly in olive oil until it melts, and salted anchovies are stirred in until they dissolve. Some cooks soften it with milk or butter. The sauce stands in the middle of the table in a terracotta pot, kept warm over a candle. Raw vegetables go round it, cardoons above all, and also peppers, celery, cabbage leaves and Jerusalem artichokes, and everybody dips.

A region with no coast eats this much anchovy because of salt. Salt came into Piemonte over the mountains, from Liguria and from Nice, which belonged to the House of Savoy until 1860, and salted anchovies came along the same roads. The men who sold them from village to village were called acciugai, and many of them came from a single valley, the Valle Maira. A story told there says the fish were a disguise. Salt was taxed, so smugglers are supposed to have packed it in barrels under a layer of anchovies. But anchovies cost more than salt, so the smugglers would have been hiding the cheaper cargo under the dearer one. What is certain is that salted anchovy was the fish a poor kitchen could reach, and that the sauce turned a table of winter vegetables into a feast.

It wants a young Barbera or Dolcetto, slightly chilled, and plenty of it. In the villages there are still bagna cauda nights, and they go on until the pot is empty.
