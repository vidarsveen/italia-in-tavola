Turin: vermouth, chocolate and the Mole.

In 1786 Antonio Benedetto Carpano was working in a liquor shop under the arcades of Piazza Castello, a few steps from the royal palace in Turin. He took white wine and steeped wormwood, herbs and spices in it. He called the result vermouth, after the German Wermut, which means wormwood. The story goes that he sent a case of it to King Victor Amadeus III, and that within a year the court was drinking it. Nobody has found a record of the case, but the drink caught on. Cinzano, Martini, Cora and Gancia, all Piedmontese houses, built their names on it, and Turin became the capital of vermouth.

People who come to Turin expecting the industrial city of Fiat are surprised by it. They find a planned baroque capital with eighteen kilometres of arcaded streets and the Alps at the end of the avenues. This was the seat of the House of Savoy, and from 1861 to 1865 it was the first capital of united Italy.

Turin also worked out how vermouth should be drunk. The aperitivo is a glass before dinner with something to eat beside it, and in the city's historic cafés it still comes with a small buffet. The classic vermouth is the sweet red, poured over ice with a twist of lemon or mixed into a Negroni. Since 2017 Vermouth di Torino has been a protected geographical indication.

Turin has drunk chocolate since the seventeenth century, when the Savoy dukes received cocoa through their Spanish connections. In 1678 the regent granted a licence to one Giovanni Antonio Ari to sell chocolate as a hot drink in the city. The bicerin grew out of an older drink of the city, the bavareisa, at the café Al Bicerin, which opened in 1763. It comes as a small glass of coffee, hot chocolate and cream in layers that nobody stirs. Alexandre Dumas praised it in a letter. Cavour is said to have drunk it there. The café still keeps its recipe secret.

In 1806 Napoleon's blockade of British trade made cocoa scarce. The city's confectioners, so the story goes, stretched what they had with hazelnuts from the Langhe, and the paste they made was gianduja. It may be tidier than what happened, since sugar was as scarce as cocoa that year. The later part is on firmer ground. At the carnival of 1865 the firm of Caffarel handed out small foil-wrapped ingots of the paste. They were presented by the city's carnival mask, a character called Gianduja, and the ingot took his name, gianduiotto.

The building on every postcard of Turin began as a synagogue. The city's Jewish community commissioned it in 1863 from the architect Alessandro Antonelli. He kept making the design taller, the cost kept rising, and in the 1870s the community could no longer pay, so the city took the building over. The spire was finished in 1889. At 167 metres the Mole Antonelliana was then the tallest masonry building in the world. In 1953 a storm brought down the top of the spire, and it was rebuilt. Since 2000 the Mole has housed the National Cinema Museum, and a glass lift rises through the empty dome to a terrace with a view of the Alps. Mole simply means a massive building, and the Turinese use this one to find their bearings.

At table Turin eats the food of the Langhe on white tablecloths. There is vitello tonnato and agnolotti, and there is fritto misto alla piemontese, a platter of fried meats, vegetables, semolina and amaretti. Beside the bread lie grissini. By Turin's own account, the baker Antonio Brunero made the first breadsticks in 1679 for the sickly young Victor Amadeus II, on a doctor's advice, because the boy could not digest ordinary bread. But a very thin bread was already described at Lanzo, north-west of the city, in 1643, so the baker may have improved on something rather than invented it.

The other side of the city's table is Porta Palazzo, a market of some eight hundred stalls, which Turin calls the largest open-air market in Europe. The region's cheeses, the truffles of Alba and the Ligurian anchovies for bagna cauda are all sold there.

The Langhe are an hour south of Turin and the rice paddies of Vercelli an hour north. France is two hours west over the mountains, and a good part of this kitchen came from there in the first place. In the early evening the aperitivo begins under the arcades of Piazza Castello, a few steps from where Carpano's shop once stood.
