Turin: vermouth, chocolate and the Mole.

Italy's first capital is a city of arcades, cafés and inventions: vermouth, gianduja, the aperitivo and the Mole that defines its skyline.

Turin surprises people. They expect an industrial city, the home of Fiat, and find a planned baroque capital with eighteen kilometres of arcaded streets, the Alps at the end of every avenue, and a café culture that predates Vienna's. This was the seat of the House of Savoy and, from 1861 to 1865, the first capital of united Italy. Its food and drink still carry the manners of a court.

The Mole.

The building on every postcard began as a synagogue. Turin's Jewish community commissioned it in 1863, but the architect Alessandro Antonelli kept enlarging his design until the community could no longer pay, and the city took it over. Finished in 1889 at 167 metres, the Mole Antonelliana was the tallest masonry building in the world. Its spire has been rebuilt after storm and lightning damage, and since 2000 the interior has housed the National Cinema Museum, with a glass lift that rises through the empty dome to a terrace with the Alps in view. Mole simply means a massive building; Turinese use it as a compass point.

Vermouth and the aperitivo.

In 1786 a distiller's apprentice named Antonio Benedetto Carpano began selling a white wine infused with wormwood, herbs and spices from a shop opposite the royal palace. He called it vermouth, from the German Wermut, wormwood, and it became the drink of the court within a year. Martini, Cinzano, Cora and Gancia followed, all Piedmontese houses, and Turin became the world's vermouth capital. It also invented the way to drink it: the aperitivo, a glass before dinner with something to eat, which in Turin's historic cafés still arrives with a small buffet. Vermouth di Torino has been a protected geographical indication since 2017; the sweet red is the classic, drunk on ice with a twist of lemon, or in a Negroni.

Key facts.

Turin was the first capital of the Kingdom of Italy, 1861–1865, before Florence and then Rome.

Carpano's shop opened in 1786; Martini & Rossi in 1863; the Negroni cocktail uses Turin vermouth by tradition.

The city's historic cafés, Al Bicerin (1763), Fiorio, Baratti & Milano, Platti and Mulassano, are protected as historic shops.

Gianduja, the chocolate and hazelnut paste, was created in Turin in 1806 when a Napoleonic blockade made cocoa scarce.

Chocolate and the bicerin.

Turin has been a chocolate city since the seventeenth century, when the Savoy dukes received cocoa through their Spanish connections. When Napoleon's blockade cut the supply in 1806, the city's confectioners stretched their cocoa with the local hazelnuts of the Langhe and invented gianduja. The gianduiotto, the little foil-wrapped ingot of it, followed in 1865, named after the carnival mask of the city. The other chocolate ritual is the bicerin, a layered glass of hot chocolate, coffee and cream that has been served at the café of the same name since the 1700s. Cavour, Dumas and Nietzsche are said to have drunk it there; the recipe is still a secret.

The city at table.

Turin eats the Langhe's food in a more formal setting: vitello tonnato and agnolotti in restaurants with white tablecloths, fritto misto alla piemontese, an extravagant platter of fried meats, vegetables, semolina and amaretti, and the grissino, the breadstick, invented here in 1679 for a sickly Savoy prince who could not digest bread. The markets are the other side: Porta Palazzo, the largest open-air market in Europe, where the region's cheeses, the Alba truffles and the Ligurian anchovies for bagna cauda all meet.

Leaving the city.

Turin is the door to the whole region. An hour south are the Langhe of the first three readings; an hour north the rice paddies of Vercelli that feed every risotto in Italy; two hours west, over the mountains, France, which is where a good part of this kitchen came from in the first place. Have a vermouth under the arcades of Piazza San Carlo before you go.

Before you move on.

The Mole Antonelliana, begun as a synagogue in 1863, is Turin's landmark and now the cinema museum.

Turin invented vermouth (1786), the aperitivo, gianduja (1806) and the grissino (1679).

The bicerin and the historic cafés are the city's living rituals; Porta Palazzo is its pantry.
