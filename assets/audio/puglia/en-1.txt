Olives, sun and the Salento.

The heel of Italy is a flat, sun-struck plain with sixty million olive trees, a coast on two seas and a landscape that a bacterium has been quietly destroying since 2013. What makes Puglia's oil, and what is at stake.

Puglia is the long, flat heel of the boot, four hundred kilometres of coast on two seas and almost no mountains. What it has instead is sun, three hundred days of it, limestone that drains and holds heat, and more olive trees than any other region in Europe: around sixty million, producing something like 40% of Italy's olive oil in a normal year. Drive south from Bari and the trees go on for hours, in grids of young plantations near the coast and, inland and in the Salento, in groves of enormous, twisted trees that were old when the trulli were built. This reading is about that landscape, the oil it makes, and the disease that is killing it.

Three landscapes.

The region divides into three. The north is the Tavoliere, the great wheat plain around Foggia, second only to the Po valley in size, with the forested Gargano promontory sticking into the Adriatic beside it. The middle is the Murge, a limestone plateau that rises to 600 metres behind Bari and Andria, where the trulli stand, the Primitivo of Gioia del Colle grows and the bread of Altamura is baked. The south is the Salento, the peninsula between the Adriatic and the Ionian sea that ends at the lighthouse of Santa Maria di Leuca, flat, red-earthed, dry, and planted almost entirely with olives and vines. Its capital is Lecce, its language was Greek until a few centuries ago and still is in a handful of villages, and its coast has the clearest water in Italy.

Key facts.

About 60 million olive trees and, in a good year, roughly 40% of Italy's olive oil; Puglia is also the largest producer of table olives.

Main cultivars: Coratina around Bari and Andria, intensely bitter and peppery, with the highest polyphenol content of any Italian olive; Ogliarola, gentler; Cellina di Nardò and Ogliarola Salentina in the Salento.

Protected oils: Terra di Bari, Terra d'Otranto, Collina di Brindisi and Dauno DOP. A regional law of 2007 protects the olivi monumentali, trees judged to be centuries old.

Xylella fastidiosa, a bacterium spread by spittlebugs, was found near Gallipoli in 2013 and has since killed or infected an estimated 21 million trees in the Salento.

The oil.

Puglia's problem for most of the twentieth century was that it made oil for other people. Tankers of it went north to be blended and bottled in Tuscany and Liguria, whose names sold better, and the region's own oil was cheap and often coarse, made from olives that had fallen and fermented before pressing. That has changed. The Coratina olive, from the plain of Bari, makes one of the most powerful oils in the world when picked early and milled within hours: grassy, bitter as an artichoke, with a pepper burn at the back of the throat that is the sign of the polyphenols the doctors talk about. A good Coratina oil poured on hot bread or a bowl of fave is the region's cooking in a spoon. The Salento's oils, from Cellina and Ogliarola, are softer and rounder, and the best of them now carry the grove's name on the label.

The dying groves.

In 2013 olive trees around Gallipoli began to dry from the tips of the branches inward, as if scorched. The cause was Xylella fastidiosa, a bacterium from the Americas that blocks the vessels that carry water up the trunk, carried from tree to tree by the meadow spittlebug. There is no cure. The only response is to fell infected trees and the healthy trees around them, which farmers refused to do to olives their families had tended for three hundred years, and by the time the courts and the European Union had argued it out, the infection had moved a hundred kilometres north. Today the lower Salento is a landscape of grey, dead giants, and the front is somewhere around Brindisi and Taranto. Growers are replanting with two cultivars that resist the disease, Leccino and the new Favolosa, in dense modern rows that will never look like the old groves. The monumental trees near Ostuni and in the Valle d'Itria, for now, are north of the line.

The Salento's two seas.

The peninsula has an Adriatic side and an Ionian side, and they are different. The east coast, from Brindisi past Otranto to Leuca, is rocky, with cliffs and small coves and a wind that comes straight from Albania across seventy kilometres of water; Otranto's cathedral floor is a twelfth-century mosaic of the tree of life, and its chapel holds the bones of the eight hundred townspeople the Ottomans killed in 1480. The west coast, the Ionian, is sandy and shallow, Gallipoli and the beaches of Porto Cesareo, and warmer by a few degrees. In between is a plain of red earth, dry-stone walls and the pajare, the Salento's own cone-roofed field huts, and everywhere the vines that the next reading is about.
