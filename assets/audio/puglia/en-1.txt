Olives, sun and the Salento.

Three hundred and thirty-two thousand four hundred and ninety-eight olive trees in Puglia are on a list. A tree earns its place by documented history, by the girth and shape of its trunk, or by standing beside something already protected. Once it is listed, felling it, uprooting it, damaging it or trading it is against the law. A bacterium is loose in those same groves, and the only measure that stops it is cutting trees down.

Puglia began counting its oldest olives in 2007. The regional law of that June set the terms. While the list was being compiled, the ban covered every centuries-old olive in the region, so that nothing could be felled while the surveyors were still at work. The register now names 332,498 trees, and a provisional list adds another 1,751 across fourteen comuni from Barletta to Sternatia. The register does not say how many of them stand inside the infected zone.

An average extra virgin oil carries three or four hundred parts per million of polyphenols, and the better Italian varieties reach five or six hundred. Coratina, the olive of the plain between Bari and Andria, is quoted at around a thousand. The compounds behind that number are oleuropein, oleocanthal and ligstroside, and what they do is burn at the back of the throat. People new to the oil often think it has gone off. Picked early and milled within hours, it goes onto hot bread and into a bowl of puréed broad beans, and that is the everyday way to eat it. The Salento grows two others, Cellina di Nardò and Ogliarola Salentina, and their oils are softer and rounder. Over long stretches of the peninsula it grew almost nothing else, one variety without a break in it.

Puglia is the heel of the boot, flat and limestone, with the Adriatic on one side and the Ionian on the other, and Brindisi's thirty-year averages give it sixty-nine days of rain in a year. Between a third and a half of Italy's olive oil comes out of it, depending on the year. Mediobanca's industry report of February 2026 put the region at 45.1% of national production, ahead of Sicily and Calabria at about ten per cent each. Between fifty and sixty million olive trees grow there, on the regional census. Close to half the national crop comes off them while the big bottling names sit in other regions, and Puglia is now first in Italy for protected-oil turnover, at twenty-six million euro. For olives eaten rather than pressed Sicily leads and Puglia is second, though Bella di Cerignola is Puglian.

Italy notified the European Commission in October 2013 that Xylella fastidiosa had been found in the province of Lecce. Maria Saponari's team at the CNR made the identification. The bacterium came out of the Americas, and the strain here is subspecies pauca, genotype ST53. By the time it was named it was already across eight to ten thousand hectares. It was found late. The bacterium multiplies in the xylem, the vessels that carry water up the trunk, and blocks them, so the tree dries from the tips of its branches inward. It travels in the meadow spittlebug, an insect that feeds on that same sap. There is no cure, and the control is the saw: infected trees come down, and the healthy trees around them come down too.

The number usually attached to the losses is twenty-one million trees. It comes from Coldiretti, the farmers' union, and it counts the trees standing inside the infected territory, not the trees that died. What has been counted is about four million infected and 2.6 million felled. The second figure is the one to carry, because somebody cut down every one of them.

Growers did not want to cut. In December 2015 the prosecutors at Lecce seized the trees that were due to come down and halted the containment plan. Researchers and regional officials were placed under investigation, on a theory that the drying and the bacterium were not connected. The case was archived in May 2019, and that September the Court of Justice of the European Union found that Italy had failed to remove infected plants as the rules required.

The edge of the infection is no longer in the Salento. It has moved more than a hundred kilometres north, and the ground it came through is a grey landscape of dead trees. Monitoring on the south-eastern edge of Bari, towards Torre a Mare, turned up six olive trees and three almonds carrying ST53. That was the first time the epidemic had been found north of the forty-first parallel, eleven years after Gallipoli. At Triggiano a different subspecies got into the vines, and more than thirty hectares of vineyard were uprooted to hold it; monitoring closed in 2026 without finding that it had spread. The comuni of the Valle d'Itria, Alberobello and Polignano among them, sit inside a targeted search programme of their own.

The advance has also slowed, and by a great deal. Donato Boscia, of the CNR institute at Bari, puts the first five years in a different class from everything since. Around a new focus in the province of Bari, the bacterium reaches ten or fifteen further plants in the year after the first infected tree is found. In the Salento between 2013 and 2018 it moved by kilometres a year. Colder winters inland, better organisation, grafting, changed farming and fewer spittlebugs are the reasons offered. Since about 2021 some surviving Cellina di Nardò have shown partial recovery from their symptoms, which is still under study, and nobody yet knows whether it lasts.

Several hundred trees in mixed groves were tested for how well they survive infection, under the heaviest natural pressure available. Ogliarola Salentina came back infected in every plant, Leccino in half, and FS-17, sold abroad as Favolosa, in twelve per cent — and those twelve per cent showed no symptoms. FS-17 is a seedling of Frantoio selected by Giuseppe Fontanazza and patented by the CNR, so the tree now going in against the disease is itself a research selection. Francesco Loreto, who directs the CNR's bio-agrifood department, refuses the word everyone else uses: this is not a resistance, but a tolerance. The bacterium still infects the tree. The tree lives with it.

Replanting has barely begun. Of nine thousand applications, 440 have been completed, and of thirty million euro available for grafting, five million has been spent, mostly for want of certified plants.

The peninsula runs south to Santa Maria di Leuca, and its eastern extremity is Punta Palascìa, a few kilometres from Otranto. From the lighthouse there the Albanian coast is seventy-one kilometres off, and the strait at its narrowest is seventy-two. Much of what the Salento is came across that gap. Greek-speaking villages stood between Otranto and Gallipoli, about forty of them. Whether the language arrived with the colonists of Magna Graecia or with Byzantine settlers many centuries after them is still argued. Thirteen villages still had it in the early nineteenth century, and seven have speakers now, almost all of them elderly. UNESCO classed Griko as severely endangered in 2011. The Adriatic side is rocky and the Ionian side sandy, and between them lies a plain of red earth, dry-stone walls and pajare, the cone-roofed field huts.

A listed tree may not be cut down. An infected tree may not be left standing. In the lower Salento both rules now apply to the same ground, and the register goes on naming trees one at a time.
