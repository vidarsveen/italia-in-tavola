Primitivo and Negroamaro.

Empty beer bottles were being collected across Brindisi in 1943. The glassworks of the south were not producing, and an order had come in that had to be filled. General Charles Poletti, who ran Allied procurement, wanted a large supply of rosé with an American name on it. The house of Leone de Castris, at Salice Salentino, had the wine; what it did not have was anything to put it in. The name came from a contrada called Cinque Rose, and from a family in which every generation had five children; on the label it went into English, as Five Roses. The wine itself was a great deal older than the bottling.

The house has always called it the first rosé bottled and sold in Italy, and every source repeating the claim traces back to the house. The older half is not in doubt: the Salento had been drinking this wine from the barrel for centuries before anyone put it into glass. What went into those beer bottles was 90% Negroamaro with a tenth of Malvasia Nera, and it is still made.

A rosato this dark means getting the juice off the skins inside a day, and the Salento has three ways of doing it. In the lacrima the must is never pressed at all: the grapes are destemmed, they sit about a day, and only the free-run juice is drawn off, roughly a third of the mass. Salasso bleeds part of the liquid off the skins as the cap rises. The vino di una notte gives the skins a single night and separates them in the morning. Squinzano writes its timings into its own rulebook, so the method behind that rosato is fixed by law. What comes out is deep, dry and heavy enough for food, and the Salento was making it that way long before the pale Provençal style became the world's default.

Negroamaro grows almost nowhere else. Its name is argued over: niger and mavros, Latin and Greek for black, doubled up as southern words often are, or simply black and bitter, which is what the Italian says. No ampelographer has settled it. Pliny is firmer ground, and he calls Manduria viticulosa, full of vines. Salice Salentino has been a denomination since 1976, and the wine most people know is the red: savoury, medium-tannined, with black cherry and dried herbs behind it and a faint bitterness at the finish. Malvasia Nera, which the textbooks give as its partner, is no longer named in the rules for the red, and survives in them only in the Aleatico type.

At Gioia del Colle, at the end of the eighteenth century, a priest with some botany picked out the vine that ripened before all the others. Don Francesco Filippo Indellicati wrote the name he gave it into the town archive: Primativo, the first to ripen. The planting year usually given, 1799, comes from an agronomist writing a century later. By about 1860 the name had settled into Primitivo, and in the 1870s it turns up in government publications.

The rest took two hundred years and four techniques. In 1967 Austin Goheen, visiting from the University of California, thought a Primitivo tasted like Zinfandel. Ampelographers agreed in 1972, enzyme fingerprints agreed in 1975, and DNA settled it in 1993 in Carole Meredith's laboratory at Davis. If the grape was not Californian it had come from somewhere, so Meredith's team sampled more than 150 old vines along the Dalmatian coast with Ivan Pejić and Edi Maletić. In December 2001 a match turned up at Kaštel Novi near Split: a vine called Crljenak kaštelanski. In 2008 a technique for reading DNA from century-old dried leaves gave the family its oldest name, Tribidrag, written down in Croatia in 1444. The American grape turned out to be Italian, and then both turned out to be Croatian.

How the vine crossed the Adriatic is unknown. The first firm record on the Italian side is the priest's block of vines at Gioia del Colle.

From Gioia del Colle the grape spread south into the Salento, above all around Manduria on the Ionian side, where the sandy soil and the sea air suited it; its DOC dates from 1974. Gioia del Colle itself is up on the Murge, between two hundred and four hundred and fifty metres and cooler for it, with real acidity in the wine. Its varietal Primitivo is the whole grape and nothing else. Fatalone bottled that wine in the DOC's own first year, from the 1987 vintage; Polvanera, founded in 2003, dug its cellar into the rock. Gianfranco Fino's Es, from bush vines about sixty years old near Manduria, showed in the 2000s what the grape could do. Primitivo is picked from late August, among the first fruit in Italy, and it ripens so unevenly that part of every bunch has raisined while the rest is ready. That is where the fourteen to sixteen per cent comes from, and the sweet wine with it. Dolce Naturale, Puglia's first DOCG, is Primitivo and nothing else, from grapes dried on the vine or on racks. It keeps at least fifty grams of sugar a litre, and nothing is added: the sugar is the grape's own.

From the later nineteenth century Puglia's dark, strong reds went north in bulk, to give colour and alcohol to thinner wines in Piedmont and in France. From the 1950s they travelled by rail, in cisterns. Puglia makes about nine million hectolitres of wine a year, second only to the Veneto. Seven per cent of it is DOC. Seventy per cent leaves as table wine. The denominations are smaller than the map suggests: Brindisi is 394 hectares and Squinzano 52. Salice Salentino Rosato amounts to something like 140,000 bottles a year, while IGT Salento runs to 6.8 million. The famous names sit on top of a bulk trade that never went away. The vineyard has shrunk from 105,601 hectares in 2008 to 86,240 in 2021. Much of that went under the European grubbing-up scheme of 2008, which allotted Puglia a ceiling of 10,560 hectares and paid growers to pull the vines.

And one of the two grapes is taking the other's ground. Between the censuses of 2000 and 2010 Negroamaro fell from 16,670 hectares to about 11,400, while Primitivo rose from 7,440 to 11,766, and nationally the grape is now on about 12,200. In the Salento itself, the grape that carries the region's name is losing ground to the one whose name American drinkers already knew.
