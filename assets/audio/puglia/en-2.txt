Primitivo and Negroamaro.

One grape turned out to be California's Zinfandel; the other has been the Salento's for a thousand years and makes Italy's oldest bottled rosé. How Puglia went from Europe's blending tank to a source of serious, sun-ripened reds.

For most of the twentieth century Puglia was the wine cellar of northern Europe, and not in a flattering sense. Its vast, high-yielding vineyards on the flat Salento made dark, strong, cheap wine that was shipped north in tankers to add colour and alcohol to the thin reds of France and Piedmont; the region produced more wine than any other in Italy and bottled almost none of it. Two grapes have changed that reputation. Primitivo, which a DNA test revealed to be the same vine as California's Zinfandel, and Negroamaro, the "black bitter" grape of the Salento, now make reds that are sold by name and rosés that were doing what Provence does before Provence thought of it.

Primitivo, or Zinfandel, or Tribidrag.

The grape was recorded in Gioia del Colle in the late eighteenth century, when a priest named Francesco Filippo Indellicati selected it and gave it its name: primativus, the early one, because it ripens weeks before anything else. From there it spread to the Salento, above all around Manduria on the Ionian side, where the sandy soil and the sea air suited it. In the early 1990s Carole Meredith at the University of California showed by DNA that Primitivo and Zinfandel were the same grape, and in 2001 both were traced to an obscure Croatian vine, Crljenak Kaštelanski, itself the ancient Tribidrag of Dalmatia. How it crossed the Adriatic is not known; a Benedictine cutting is the usual story.

Primitivo makes a big wine: deep purple, 14 to 16% alcohol, with blackberry jam, dried fig, black pepper and a sweet, almost port-like edge, because the grape ripens unevenly and part of every bunch is raisined by the time the rest is ripe. There are two schools. Primitivo di Manduria, from the hot Ionian plain, is the rich one, and its sweet Dolce Naturale version, from partly dried grapes, is the region's only DOCG. Gioia del Colle, from the Murge plateau at 350 to 500 metres, is cooler, fresher and more elegant, with real acidity, and it is where the grape started and where the most interesting wines now come from.

Key facts.

Primitivo di Manduria DOC (1974): at least 85% Primitivo, 13.5% alcohol minimum, 14% for Riserva; Primitivo di Manduria Dolce Naturale DOCG (2011) is the sweet version. Gioia del Colle DOC (1987) covers the Murge.

Salice Salentino DOC (1976): mostly Negroamaro with a share of Malvasia Nera, aged from a year; Riserva two years. Squinzano, Copertino and Brindisi are the neighbouring Negroamaro DOCs.

Puglia's other reds: Nero di Troia in the north around Castel del Monte, tannic and dark; Bombino Nero for rosé. Whites: Verdeca from Locorotondo, Fiano Minutolo, Bombino Bianco.

Leone de Castris bottled the first Italian rosé, Five Roses, in 1943, from Negroamaro, for the American forces at Brindisi.

Negroamaro and the rosé.

Negroamaro is the Salento's own grape, grown almost nowhere else. The name is usually explained as "black and bitter", though it may simply be black twice over, Latin niger and Greek mavro, from the two languages the peninsula has spoken. It is dark, but not as heavy as Primitivo: black cherry and plum, a note of dried herbs and tobacco, medium tannin and a faint, pleasant bitterness on the finish that is the grape's signature. On its own it can be rustic; blended with the perfumed Malvasia Nera, as in Salice Salentino, it becomes rounder and more scented. The classic style is a warm, savoury red for grilled meat and aged cheese, best with three or four years in bottle.

The Salento's real speciality is the rosato. The grape's skins give up colour quickly, and the tradition here is to run off part of the juice after a night on the skins, the lacrima or "tear", and ferment it as a deep coral-pink wine with body and bite, much more than a summer drink. It was drunk from the barrel for centuries before Leone de Castris bottled it in 1943 as Five Roses, the first Italian rosé to be sold in a bottle. Today the Salento makes more serious rosé than any part of Italy, and it is the wine to drink with the region's fish and its vegetable cooking.

In the glass: Primitivo di Manduria.

 Colour: Opaque purple-black, garnet at the rim with age.

 Nose: Blackberry jam, dried fig, prune, black pepper, cocoa, a warm alcoholic lift.

 Palate: Full body, soft ripe tannin, low to medium acidity, sweet fruit, long warm finish.

 Alcohol: 14–16%.

 Serve: 16–17 °C, no warmer, and not with delicate food; drink from two years, most within eight.

 At the table: Grilled lamb, bombette, horse-meat stews, aged caciocavallo, roasted peppers and aubergine.

The producers and the new Puglia.

The change of reputation came from a few estates. Leone de Castris and Cantele in the Salento made Negroamaro respectable; Gianfranco Fino's Es, a Primitivo di Manduria from ninety-year-old bush vines, showed in the 2000s that the grape could make one of Italy's great reds; Polvanera and Fatalone in Gioia del Colle proved the cooler, fresher style. The problem the region still has is the opposite of quality: the vineyards of the Salento have been pulled up faster than anywhere in Italy, with EU grants for grubbing up vines, and much of what remains is trained on the high tendone pergola for volume. The old alberello vineyards, low bush vines on red soil that give the concentrated fruit the best wines need, are the ones worth saving, and the growers know it.

Before you move on.

Primitivo, the early-ripening grape of Gioia del Colle and Manduria, is genetically the same as Zinfandel; it makes a rich, jammy red of 14–16%.

Negroamaro is the Salento's grape, savoury and faintly bitter, blended with Malvasia Nera in Salice Salentino, and the base of Italy's oldest bottled rosé.

Puglia was the blending tank of Europe; the old alberello vineyards on the Salento's red soil are the source of its serious wines.
