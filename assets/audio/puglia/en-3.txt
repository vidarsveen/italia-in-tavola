Orecchiette, burrata and the bread of Altamura.

Puglia's kitchen is poor and vegetable: durum wheat, olive oil, greens and beans, with cheese and seafood at the edges. The pasta shaped on a thumb, the cheese invented to use up scraps, and the bread that Horace praised.

The cooking of Puglia is cucina povera in the strict sense: what a landless peasant could grow, gather or afford. That means durum wheat in every form, olive oil rather than butter or lard, wild greens, dried beans, and vegetables cooked with more care than meat gets elsewhere. Cheese comes from the sheep and cows of the Murge; fish comes from two seas and is often eaten raw. There is no cream, very little beef, and the region's most famous dish is a pasta shaped on a thumb and dressed with turnip tops.

Orecchiette.

Orecchiette, "little ears", are made from durum-wheat semolina and water, no egg. A rope of dough is cut into pieces the size of a fingernail, each is dragged across the board with a knife so that it curls, and then turned inside out over the thumb to make a rough-surfaced cup that holds sauce. In the old town of Bari, in Via dell'Arco Basso, women still make them on tables outside their front doors and sell them to passers-by, and the same shape is called strascinati, dragged ones, in the hills. The classic dressing is cime di rapa, the bitter flowering tops of the turnip, boiled in the pasta water and finished with olive oil, garlic, chilli and anchovy. The second classic is ragù of horse meat or of braciole, rolled slices of meat, cooked on Sunday.

Key facts.

Pane di Altamura DOP (2003), the first bread in Europe with a protected designation: durum-wheat semolina from the Murge, sourdough, a wood-fired oven, and a loaf that keeps for two weeks. The town claims Horace, passing through in 37 BC, called it the best bread in the world.

Burrata di Andria IGP (2016): a pouch of mozzarella filled with shredded mozzarella and cream, invented on a farm near Andria, traditionally around 1956, to use up the scraps of the day's cheese-making.

Other protected cheeses: Caciocavallo Silano, the pear-shaped stretched-curd cheese hung in pairs; Canestrato Pugliese, a hard sheep's cheese ripened in reed baskets.

Bari eats raw seafood, crudo di mare: sea urchins, octopus, cuttlefish and prawns, with lemon and nothing else.

Burrata and the stretched curds.

Puglia's cheeses are pasta filata, stretched curd: mozzarella, the ball of it in brine; scamorza, dried and sometimes smoked; caciocavallo, aged until it is sharp. Burrata is the youngest and the most famous. On a farm at Andria, on the Murge, cheesemakers found a use for the ragged strands left over when mozzarella is pulled: they mixed them with cream, and sealed the mixture inside a bag of fresh mozzarella, tied at the top like a purse. The result must be eaten within a day or two, cut open at the table so that the cream runs out, with good oil, salt and perhaps a tomato. It was a local secret until the 1990s; now it is made in Wisconsin. The one from Andria, wrapped in asphodel leaves and protected since 2016, is still the reference.

The bread of Altamura.

The Murge grows Italy's hardest wheat, and the town of Altamura turns it into a bread that has been famous for two thousand years. The loaf is big, a kilo or two and sometimes more, folded over itself before baking so that it has a crest, with a thick dark crust and a yellow, dense, sweet crumb that stays fresh for a fortnight, which is why shepherds and farm workers who spent a week away from home carried it. It is made only from durum semolina, a sourdough starter kept alive from the previous batch, water and salt, and baked in a wood oven; in the old days each family brought its loaves to the communal oven with the family's initial stamped on the crust to tell them apart. It was the first bread in Europe to receive a protected designation, in 2003. Toasted with oil and tomato, or torn into a bowl of fave e cicoria, it is the region's staple.

Beans, greens and the street.

Fave e cicoria is the dish that explains Puglia: dried broad beans cooked to a purée, next to boiled wild chicory, with oil poured over both. It costs almost nothing and is on every trattoria menu. Taralli, small crisp rings of dough with oil and fennel seed, are eaten with wine at any hour. Focaccia barese is thick, with potato in the dough, topped with cherry tomatoes and olives and drenched in oil, sold by the slab from bakeries in Bari. Inland, at Martina Franca and Cisternino, the butchers' shops become restaurants in the evening: you choose your meat from the counter and it is grilled behind the shop, the fornello pronto, and the speciality is bombette, small rolls of pork neck around cheese, and the cured capocollo di Martina Franca. On the coast at Bari the fishermen sell sea urchins and raw octopus from the quay, and in Lecce the day starts with a pasticciotto, a custard-filled pastry, and a caffè in ghiaccio, espresso poured over ice with almond milk.

Before you move on.

Puglia's kitchen is durum wheat, olive oil, greens and beans: orecchiette con cime di rapa and fave e cicoria are its signature dishes.

Burrata was invented at Andria to use up mozzarella scraps and cream; Pane di Altamura was the first protected bread in Europe.

Raw seafood in Bari, grilled bombette in the Valle d'Itria and the pasticciotto of Lecce complete the table.
