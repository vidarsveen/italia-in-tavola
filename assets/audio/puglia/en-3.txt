Orecchiette, burrata and the bread of Altamura.

A McDonald's opened in Altamura in 2001. A few months later the baker Luca Di Gesù and his brother Giuseppe opened a focaccia shop a short walk away. Within about a year the McDonald's had closed for want of custom, and by 2002 the story had been picked up in Panorama, in Libération and in the New York Times. The focacceria was one reason among several, and the town has been dining out on it ever since. Bread is what Altamura sells, and it has a European registration to prove it.

Pane di Altamura is made from remilled durum semolina of four named varieties — appulo, arcangelo, duilio and simeto — which must be at least eighty per cent of the flour. Each loaf is turned over and pushed into the oven by hand at two hundred and fifty degrees. The door stays open for the first fifteen minutes, shut for another forty-five, then open again at the end so the steam can leave and the crust dry. That crust must be at least three millimetres thick, over a dense straw-yellow crumb. The oven should preferably be wood- or gas-fired, and where it is heated directly the wood must be oak.

The grain and the baking both belong to five comuni — Altamura, Gravina, Poggiorsini, Spinazzola and Minervino Murge — so the name is a town's and the appellation a plateau's.

The specification opens its history with Horace, who came this way in 37 BC and praised the bread of a little town he says he cannot fit into a hexameter. That is a joke about metre. The standard commentary reads the town as Venusia, which was Horace's own birthplace and is in Basilicata, and the road he was on ran through Canosa. The European text prints the claim anyway, conceding in its own first clause that it concerns the Murgia region rather than Altamura. What the poem actually says is that the bread was by far the finest to be had, that wise travellers carried a supply for later, and that at Canusium it was gritty.

The bread was built to outlast an absence. The registration says its long keeping was what sustained peasants and shepherds through the week, or more often the fortnight, that they spent out on isolated farms. The fortnight is the length of the absence and not a promise about the loaf; no shelf life is written down anywhere. Home baking, meanwhile, was forbidden. The town's statutes of 1527, transcribed in 1954, fined citizens of any station or condition for baking bread in their own houses, and the fine was a third of the whole cost of the baking. So the dough was mixed and raised at home and carried out to a public oven. The baker pressed the initials of the head of the household into each loaf with a wooden or iron stamp before it went in, because the oven was shared and the dough was not.

In February 2025 the ministry suspended the four-wheat rule until the end of July. The 2024 drought had halved the harvest and there was not enough qualifying grain to certify the usual volume, so any durum grown in the territory would do. The consortium asked for the change; the trade press noted that its president runs an industrial bakery, and argued that a handmade product's supply should not be allowed to rewrite its standard.

Puglia's kitchen is usually explained by poverty, and the poverty is documented. The great estates were worked by labourers who lived in the towns and walked out to the fields, and when the land reform came in 1950 the province of Lecce was left out of it. On 28 December 1950 they walked fifteen kilometres and more into the Arneo and occupied it, twelve hundred at first and up to two thousand before it was over. But the region was never poor in what it grew. It produces about a million tonnes of durum wheat a year, a quarter to a third of the national crop, most of it off the Tavoliere around Foggia. Its oil went out through Gallipoli as lamp oil, inedible, to light streets in London and Amsterdam. Its wine went north in tankers. Its wool and its cheese came off flocks that wintered on the plain under a customs administration at Foggia. What the kitchen had was what stayed behind after the ships were loaded, and it was cooked in oil rather than butter.

Orecchiette, little ears, are semola, water and usually a pinch of salt, with no egg anywhere. A rope of dough is cut into pieces and dragged across a bare board with a small iron knife, the sfèrre. In Bari the drag alone makes the dome, with two fingers and no thumb. Turning the piece inside out over a thumb is the Valle d'Itria method, and in the hills the dragged shapes are flatter and wider. The dialect names change every few towns: chiancarelle, which is also the word for the roofing slabs of a trullo, and at Cisternino recch' d'privt, priests' ears. What comes off the board is a small rough cup, and the roughness is what holds a thin dressing.

The dressing is cime di rapa, a brassica grown for its flowering tops rather than for its root, cut head by head from September to May; Puglia grows about a third of Italy's crop. It is properly bitter, and the bitterness is the dish. The greens go into the boiling water, the pasta follows them in, and anchovies melt separately into warm oil with garlic and chilli. No cheese goes over it, and that is the one absolute rule. The Sunday alternative is brasciole, rolls of horse meat around lard and pecorino, four hours in tomato, the sauce eaten first and the meat after.

Bari's pasta women worked between numbers 1 and 25 of Strada Arco Basso, at tables outside their own front doors. In November 2024 a man from a home-restaurant platform filmed what he said was a pastaia holding packets of industrial orecchiette, and cardboard boxes from an Altamura pasta factory were found in bins nearby. Prosecutors opened a fraud investigation in February 2025, the Guardia di Finanza raided later that year, and three vendors were fined five thousand euro each. In June 2025 the comune published its rules. Pasta may no longer be made in the street except as a demonstration. It may be sold only if it was made indoors, in a registered kitchen with a written hygiene plan. Six women came forward to register. The rest carried their tables and frames inside. One of those who had been fined said the demand had simply been more than she could keep up with.

Burrata is a manteca made fresh. The old trick was to hide butter inside a skin of aged stretched curd so that it would keep. Andria's cheese does the same with cream, inside a fresh one. The dossier behind the protected name tells it as a story about snow: a farm cut off, milk that could not go down to the city, cream that had to be used. It marks that story as hearsay twice over, and puts the invention in the first decades of the twentieth century. The earliest printed trace it can name is from 1931. The name covers the whole of Puglia, the milk is cow's, and the cream may be pasteurised or UHT and need not come from anywhere near Andria. What has to happen on the spot is the packing, because the cheese does not travel. The leaf around it is now a green plasticised one; the asphodel leaf it imitates was the freshness gauge, waxy and green while the cheese was ready and yellowed when it was not.

It was written up in the Corriere della Sera in 1977, and a cheesemaker from Puglia was making it in Wisconsin by 2006. Certified production is under seven hundred tonnes a year. At the other end of the scale is Canestrato Pugliese, a hard sheep's cheese pressed into rush baskets that leave their weave in the rind, and there is less of it made every year.

Bari eats its seafood raw at the water's edge, at the mole the city calls N'dèrr'a la lanze, at the foot of the boats. Polpo arricciato is raw only in the sense that no heat touches it. It is beaten against stone, smashed on the rock, slid across it for a quarter of an hour and then swung in a flat basket until the tentacles tighten into ringlets. The regional register calls that cold cooking, and about two hours go into it; Bari's municipal decrees of 1513 already list pulpo rizzuto. The urchins are another matter. Since 2023 Puglia's sea urchins may not be fished at all. A restaurant in Bari may still serve them, provided they come out of somebody else's sea. The reason given in the law is the risk of extinction from massive harvesting.

Dried broad beans, peeled and soaked about ten hours, cook down in a clay pignata to a purée, with boiled wild chicory beside them and oil over both. That is fave e cicoria, and in Taranto and Brindisi it goes by 'ncapriata; a piece of Altamura bread torn into it is the usual way to eat it. The bean is on the table for a reason older than the plate. Durum wheat sown after faba beans yields about twelve per cent more, and far more in a dry year, because the bean draws less water and leaves more of it in the ground.

A tarallo is boiled before it is baked; the dough is unleavened, and it is flour, oil, white wine and salt. Focaccia barese is baked in a round tin, its tomatoes split by hand and never cut with a knife. At Martina Franca and Cisternino the butchers' shops turn into restaurants in the evening. You pick your meat at the counter, and it goes to the fornello pronto behind the shop, a stone dome where it cooks upright beside the fire. The speciality there is the bombetta, which is younger than it looks — a butcher's wife at Martina Franca is credited with it around 1980.

In Lecce the morning starts with a pasticciotto from Galatina, a shortcrust case of custard made with lard, and a coffee poured over ice. A kitchen assembled out of what was left over is now being defined, certified and in places forbidden. The women of Arco Basso still make orecchiette. They make them behind the door.
