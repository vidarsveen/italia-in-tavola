Trulli, castles and the Adriatic.

Cone-roofed houses built without mortar so they could be pulled down before the tax collector came; an emperor's eight-sided castle with no known purpose; and a city carved in golden stone. Puglia's landmarks and the odd history behind them.

Puglia's monuments are unlike anything else in Italy, because the people who built them were unlike anyone else: Norman knights, a German emperor who spoke six languages and kept a menagerie, Spanish viceroys, Greek monks and peasants who were very good at stacking stones. Four of them are in this reading: the trulli of Alberobello, Castel del Monte, the baroque of Lecce, and the coast itself, from the Gargano to the mosaic floor of Otranto.

Trulli: houses you could take apart.

A trullo is a house built of limestone slabs without mortar, with a corbelled cone for a roof, each course of flat chiancarelle stones stepped inward until the cone closes at a pinnacle. The form is ancient, related to the shepherd huts of the whole Mediterranean, but the town of Alberobello has more than a thousand of them and owes them to a tax dodge. In the seventeenth century the counts of Conversano, who owned the land, were obliged to pay the crown a levy on any new settlement they founded; they got round it by allowing their peasants to build only houses that could be dismantled in a day, so that when the inspector came the village could be turned back into a field. When Alberobello freed itself from the counts in 1797, its people kept building the way they knew. The trulli are whitewashed, their cones are grey, and many carry symbols painted in lime, crosses, hearts and stars, and a carved pinnacle that was the mason's signature. UNESCO listed the town in 1996. They are cool in summer, damp in winter, and most are now holiday rentals.

Key facts.

Alberobello: more than 1,000 trulli in the Rione Monti and Aia Piccola districts; UNESCO World Heritage 1996. The two-storey Trullo Sovrano of the 1700s is the largest.

Castel del Monte: built for Emperor Frederick II from 1240 on a hill of 540 metres near Andria; an octagon with eight octagonal towers, 25 metres high; UNESCO 1996; on the Italian one-cent coin.

Lecce: the Basilica di Santa Croce, 1549–1695, is the masterpiece of the local baroque, carved in the soft golden pietra leccese.

Otranto: the cathedral's mosaic floor, the Tree of Life, was laid by the monk Pantaleone in 1163–65; in 1480 the Ottomans sacked the town and killed 800 townspeople who refused to convert.

Castel del Monte: the emperor's octagon.

On a bare hill of the Murge, visible for thirty kilometres, stands a castle that is not quite a castle. Frederick II of Hohenstaufen, Holy Roman Emperor and king of Sicily, ordered it in 1240 and it was finished within the decade. It is a perfect octagon, with an octagonal tower at each corner and an octagonal courtyard in the middle, eight rooms on each of two floors, and almost nothing else: no moat, no drawbridge, no kitchen, no stables, and windows that face the wrong way for defence. Historians have called it a hunting lodge, an observatory, a treasury and a monument to the emperor's own learning; the octagon sits between the square of the earth and the circle of heaven, and the building's proportions have been read as astronomical, mathematical and mystical. Frederick himself, the stupor mundi, the wonder of the world, spoke Arabic and Greek, wrote a treatise on falconry and argued with the Pope; the castle is probably best understood as his portrait. It has been on the Italian one-cent coin since the euro arrived.

Lecce and the stone that carves like wood.

Lecce is called the Florence of the south, which undersells it: Florence has nothing like it. In the seventeenth century, under Spanish rule and flush with money from oil and wheat, the city rebuilt its churches and palaces in a baroque so dense with carving that the façades look woven. The reason is the stone. Pietra leccese is a soft, fine-grained golden limestone that can be cut with a saw and carved like wood when it comes out of the ground, and hardens afterwards in the air. The masons of Lecce covered every surface with garlands, cherubs, animals, saints and grotesques; the façade of Santa Croce, worked on by three generations of the Zimbalo family, is the extreme case, a wall of figures that took a century and a half. The whole old town is built of the same stone, so that at sunset it glows.

The coast.

Puglia's coast runs for eight hundred kilometres, counting both sides of the heel. In the north the Gargano is a mountain of forest and white cliffs jutting into the Adriatic, with the pilgrimage cave of Saint Michael at Monte Sant'Angelo and the Tremiti islands offshore. The Adriatic shore below Bari is low and rocky, with Polignano a Mare stacked on its cliffs and Monopoli's harbour, and the white hill town of Ostuni looking down on the olives. At the tip of the heel, Otranto is Italy's easternmost town, its cathedral floored with the Tree of Life and its chapel walled with the skulls of the martyrs of 1480. Round the point, on the Ionian side, Gallipoli sits on an island joined to the mainland by a bridge, and the Greek name, "beautiful city", is deserved. Between the two, the sea at Santa Maria di Leuca, where the Adriatic and the Ionian are supposed to meet, changes colour along a line you can almost see.

Before you move on.

Alberobello's trulli are dry-stone cone houses built to be dismantled before the tax inspector; UNESCO listed them in 1996.

Castel del Monte, Frederick II's octagonal castle of 1240, has no agreed purpose and is on the one-cent coin.

Lecce's baroque was made possible by its soft golden stone; Otranto's mosaic floor and Gallipoli mark the two seas at the tip of the heel.
