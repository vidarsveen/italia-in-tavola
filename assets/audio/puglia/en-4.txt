Trulli, castles and the Adriatic.

The count of Conversano brought about forty peasant families over from Noci to clear the Murgia and plant it. They owed him a tithe of every harvest. The stone they lifted out of the fields went into the walls of the houses they built, laid dry, without mortar, because that was what the ground gave them. The first public buildings the count ordered were a bakery, a mill and an inn.

A trullo's walls are double, a metre to a metre and a half thick, packed between the skins with rubble and red earth. The cone above them is two shells. Inside is a corbelled false dome, la candela, each ring of stone stepped in over the one below until it closes at the top. Outside is a skin of thin limestone slabs, the chiancarelle, laid to shed rain. Nothing holds any of it up but the friction between the stones, and it is built without formwork of any kind. The form is old, kin to shepherds' huts all round the Mediterranean, though the houses standing today are early modern. A metre of wall keeps the inside six to eight degrees cooler than the air outside in summer. The rain that runs off the cone is led into a cistern under the floor, and on a plateau with no surface water that cistern is what makes cooking, and settlement, possible at all.

Alberobello tells a story about why the houses were built dry. A royal pragmatic required the crown's assent before a new settlement could be founded. So the counts of Conversano let their tenants put up only what could be taken apart in a day, and a dismantling is recorded in 1644. The dossier that put the town on the World Heritage list carries that as tradition. Then it gives the scholars' view, which is that dry stone was a minimal physical response to local conditions, later exploited for fiscal purposes. It is how you build on a karst plateau littered with the stone you have just cleared off your own field. The same dossier names a second use for a house that comes apart, and it is the harder one: the technique allowed the dispossession of recalcitrant residents. A landlord could level a tenant's house at will, and in 1644 the count himself was in a prison in Madrid.

The cones of Alberobello are bare grey stone and the walls whitewashed. Many cones carry symbols painted on in white ash; a catalogue made in 1940 recorded two hundred different signs, and there is no key to them. The carved keystone at the top is usually a charm against bad luck. On 27 May 1797 seven men — two doctors, a master craftsman and four priests — put a petition to Ferdinand IV while he was at Taranto. He raised the village to a royal town, and the first mayor was elected on 22 June. New building in trulli fell away almost at once, and within weeks Antonio Francesco D'Amore had put up the first one with a floor above it. The moment they were free to build otherwise, they did.

Below the Murgia the Tavoliere was kept in grass. From 1447 a customs administration, at Lucera first and at Foggia from 1468, compelled anyone with more than twenty sheep into a system of leased winter pasture and measured drove roads. What the shepherds got for it was tax relief and discounted flour, oil, wine and salt; what came off the plain was wool, lamb, and the cheeses, caciocavallo and pecorino. Within five years the transhumant flocks had doubled to something near a million head. The system lasted until 1806.

Frederick II was the largest grain merchant in his own kingdom. The register of 1239 and 1240 shows him gathering grain from crown lands and peasant dues and selling the surplus abroad. In October 1239 he named eleven ports as the only places it could leave from. Salt was a royal monopoly, and the Constitutions of Melfi of 1231 made weights and measures uniform across the realm. In that same year, 1239, he ordered a search for the forgotten technique of making sugar — forgotten because the deportation of Sicily's Arabs, which he had ordered himself, had taken it away with them.

Castel del Monte stands on a hill of about 540 metres, visible for thirty kilometres. It is an octagon, with an octagonal tower at each corner and an octagonal courtyard in the middle. A mandate of 29 January 1240 orders material brought for it in lime and stone without delay, and that is the only near-contemporary document there is. Surveys in the 1990s found the work rudimentary and the building probably never finished: no marble cladding, no sculpture, none of the wooden walkways. There is no outer bailey, no moat, no stables, no kitchen, no storerooms and no chapel. There are six cisterns, latrines and washbasins with clay and stone piping, and five fireplaces that both heated the rooms and cooked in them. No document says Frederick ever stayed there. A companion order of the same January day provisioned the imperial houses with hens, geese and ducks.

Historians argue for a fortress, a residence, a hunting lodge and a statement of imperial power, and they reject the astronomical and the Grail readings outright. Frederick argued with the Pope, kept a menagerie of camels and leopards, and spent some thirty years on a book about hunting with birds.

Gallipoli sits on a limestone island joined to the mainland by a bridge, and its Greek name means the beautiful city. It had thirty-five oil mills cut into the rock underneath it and about two thousand cisterns, and something like seventy per cent of the Salento's oil went out through its port. It was lampante: high in acid, inedible, and it burned without smoking. It lit streets in London and Amsterdam and Stockholm, greased looms in the English mills and filled lamps in Russian churches. A notary at Nardò recorded a shipment of 520 stara, about eight and a half thousand litres, going to Venice in 1569. A dozen states kept vice-consuls in the town until 1923. Electric light ended the trade, and one of the underground mills was found again only in 2002. Monopoli, up the Adriatic coast, lived on its harbour, its fishing and the same oil.

Gregorovius called Lecce the Florence of the Baroque. Its stone is a soft yellow limestone packed with the shells of plankton, thirty to forty per cent air, and it can be cut with a saw and carved like wood. That is why the façades are worked over every surface, with garlands, cherubs, animals and saints. The masons finished it with what was on the kitchen shelf. A study of ten buildings and more than fifty samples found milk, walnut oil, egg white and prickly-pear leaf brushed onto the stone, and no beeswax or lime wash at all. Santa Croce was begun in 1549 and the cartouche on its rose window is dated 1646, ninety-seven years later. Two Zimbalos worked on it, a grandfather and a grandson, and the third hand on the front was Cesare Penna's. The money behind it was merchants' — Florentine, Venetian and Genoese houses traded in the city from the 1500s — and the oil money was Gallipoli's.

On 28 July 1480 an Ottoman fleet came over from Valona to Otranto. The town fell on 11 August, and on 14 August about eight hundred men who refused to convert were beheaded on the hill above it. Their bones are in the cathedral still, in seven glazed presses behind an altar in the right apse. Pope Francis canonised them on 12 May 2013, the first canonisation of his pontificate.

The floor they died above had been there three hundred years already. It is a mosaic of some six hundred thousand pieces of local limestone, running fifty-two metres from the door to the apse. A priest named Pantaleone laid it between 1163 and 1165 for the archbishop Jonathan. A tree of life carries Old Testament scenes, beasts, Alexander borne up by two griffins, and King Arthur riding a goat. Around them run the twelve months at their work. June reaps and July threshes, August brings in the grapes and September treads them, October ploughs and December kills the pig. The November panel has a man scattering seed from the fold of his tunic, with an axe, a drinking horn, a wine barrel, a ham and a loaf set around him. It is a farming year in stone, laid beside the expulsion from Eden, so that the work reads as the punishment and as the way back from it.
