The harbours and the interior.

There is a line across Sardinia that no army ever moved. Phoenicians, Carthaginians, Romans, Byzantines, Pisans, Genoese, Aragonese and Piedmontese all took the harbours, and none of them took the inside. The reason is not heroic. The coastal plains were malarial within living memory, so the people who lived here lived above the mosquito, in the hills, with animals that could walk. The island a visitor sees now is a coast. The island that made the food is the other one.

The north-east, the Gallura, is granite, weathered into tors and boulders, and the soil it gives is thin and acid and drains the moment it rains. The centre rises into the limestone and schist of the Supramonte and the Barbagia, a wall of grey rock and holm oak. The south-west, the Sulcis, runs down to dunes, and vines are planted straight into the sand. Three rocks, three jobs, and each one decides a different wine.

The vineyard is small, the yields low, the summers long and dry, and a wind that never stops keeps the fruit healthy. How much vineyard there is depends on who measured: two arms of the same statistical office counted it in the same year by different methods and came back with 28,190 hectares and 18,500.

For most of its history this was not a wine economy. It was wool, milk and meat, and it still is. Three million sheep graze here against 1.6 million people, about forty-five per cent of the national flock, and they give two-thirds of Italy's sheep milk. A shepherd away from the village for weeks needed food that would not spoil, which is why the island bakes a bread that keeps for months and cures cheese that gets harder rather than softer.

One cheese carries a Roman name and a Sardinian address. About ninety-two per cent of all Pecorino Romano is made on this island, against under three thousand tonnes a year in Lazio. The consortium that defends the name keeps its office at Macomer, in the province of Nuoro. The legal text that defines the zone names Sardinia first.

The cost of that is easier to see in the cheese the island makes for itself. Most of the Roman cheese leaves for the United States, which is the fixed market the whole island now depends on. Fiore Sardo, raw milk from pasture-fed ewes, smoked over scrub and aged for months, made 339 tonnes in 2023. The Roman cheese made on the same island in the same year came to thirty-six thousand. That is a hundredfold difference. Fiore Sardo also lost half its output between 2021 and 2022, so the gap is still widening. Pecorino Sardo, the everyday DOP in its mild and sharp forms, sits between them at about 1,700 tonnes.

In February 2019 shepherds began pouring milk onto the roads. They were being paid about sixty cents a litre for milk that cost nearer seventy. The blockades closed the Carlo Felice and the eastern coast road for a month. A dairy was attacked, lorries were burned at Bitti and at Torralba, and on 8 March the prefect of Sassari brokered an agreement at seventy-four cents, with seventy-two paid retroactively for February.

The agreement tied the price to what the cheese fetched: seventy-two cents a litre while Pecorino Romano sold at six euros a kilo, rising to a euro and two cents at eight-fifty. The consortium's own planning documents explain why it had collapsed in the first place. Production ran to 356,000 quintals in 2015/16 and the milk price fell to sixty-three cents the following year; the ceiling the consortium had set was 280,000 quintals and the dairies made 341,833. A cheese with a fixed market and no ceiling that holds will do this again. In 2023 the milk reached two euros. By September 2026 the shepherds were back on the road.
