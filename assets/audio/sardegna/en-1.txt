An island of shepherds and vines.

Sardinia is not a piece of Italy that floated off. It has its own language, its own prehistory and a wine landscape shaped by granite, sand and sheep. The island's geography, its pastoral economy, and two wines that exist nowhere else.

Sardinia lies closer to Tunisia than to Rome, and it behaves like it. The island has its own Romance language, older and closer to Latin than Italian; a prehistory with no parallel anywhere in Europe; and an interior that spent three thousand years ignoring whoever claimed to rule the coast. Phoenicians, Carthaginians, Romans, Byzantines, Pisans, Genoese, Aragonese and Piedmontese all took the harbours. None of them took the mountains. What that isolation produced, in the glass and on the plate, is the subject of this module.

Rock, and what grows on it.

The island is old ground. The north-east, the Gallura, is granite: pink, coarse, weathered into tors and boulders, giving thin acid soils that drain instantly and hold no water at all. The centre is limestone and schist rising into the Supramonte and the Barbagia, a wall of grey rock and holm oak that made the interior effectively unconquerable. The south-west, the Sulcis, runs down to sand: dunes and windblown beaches where vines are planted straight into it. Between them lie the Campidano plain behind Cagliari and the volcanic tablelands of the west. Rainfall is low, summers are long and dry, and the wind is constant, which keeps the vines healthy and the yields small.

Sardinia has around 26,000 hectares of vines, roughly a third of Sicily's, and about 17 DOCs to Sicily's twenty-odd. What matters more than the numbers is the mix. Three grapes carry the island: Cannonau, the red of the interior; Vermentino, the white of the north; and Carignano, the red of the southern sand. Two more are curiosities worth knowing: Nuragus, a high-yielding white of the Campidano named after the nuraghi, and Vernaccia di Oristano, which is not a table wine at all.

Sheep before vines.

For most of its history the island's economy was not wine but wool, milk and meat. Sardinia still has more sheep than people, something over three million of them against 1.6 million inhabitants, and the pastoral calendar shaped everything: the food, the calendar of feasts, the pattern of settlement in the interior, even the bread. The shepherd who left home for months at a time needed food that kept, which is why the island bakes a bread that lasts a year and cures cheeses that get harder and sharper rather than softer.

The cheese is the clearest case. Pecorino Sardo is the island's own DOP, made in a mild young form and a sharp aged one. But the bigger business is Pecorino Romano: despite the name, nearly all of it is now made in Sardinia, the protected area covering Sardinia, Lazio and the province of Grosseto. It is a Roman cheese made on a Sardinian island by Sardinian shepherds, mostly for export to America, and the price of its milk is a live political question on the island to this day.

Key facts.

About 26,000 hectares of vineyard, low yields, and a climate of long dry summers and constant wind. Around three million sheep to 1.6 million people.

Carignano del Sulcis DOC (1977): at least 85% Carignano, grown on the sandy south-west and its islands, Sant'Antioco and San Pietro. The rules require alberello bush training, not wires.

Vernaccia di Oristano DOC: aged oxidatively under a film of flor yeast in partly filled chestnut barrels, often through a solera. Dry, 15% minimum; the fortified liquoroso at least 18%.

Pecorino Romano DOP may be made only in Sardinia, Lazio and the province of Grosseto, and nearly all of it is now Sardinian.

Carignano and the sand.

In the south-west, around Sant'Antioco and Carloforte, vines grow in dunes. Sand is hostile ground for most plants, but it is hostile ground for phylloxera too: the root louse that destroyed Europe's vineyards from the 1860s cannot move through it. So the Sulcis kept its old vines. Many are ungrafted, on their own roots, and some are well over a century old, trained as free-standing alberello bushes low to the ground where the sea wind cannot get at them. The wine, Carignano del Sulcis, is dark, warm and full of black fruit and Mediterranean scrub, with softer tannins than its concentration suggests. It is the same grape as the French Carignan, which in the Languedoc is treated as a workhorse to be blended away; here it is the star.

In the glass: Carignano del Sulcis.

 Colour: Deep ruby to purple, dense.

 Nose: Blackberry, plum, myrtle and Mediterranean scrub, black pepper, a salty note from the coast.

 Palate: Full and warm, ripe soft tannin, moderate acidity, a dry savoury finish.

 Alcohol: 13.5–15%.

 Serve: 16–18 °C; drinks well from three years, Riserva keeps ten.

 At the table: Grilled lamb, porceddu, aged pecorino, tomato and aubergine dishes.

The wine that behaves like sherry.

On the west coast, in the lower valley of the river Tirso behind Oristano, the island makes something that has no business existing in Italy. Vernaccia di Oristano is put into chestnut barrels that are deliberately left part empty, and a film of flor yeast grows across the surface, exactly as it does in the sherry bodegas of Jerez. Under that film the wine oxidises slowly for years, often blended fractionally through a solera in which new wine is added at the top and drawn off years later at the bottom; one producer keeps a solera holding more than a hundred vintages. The result is dry, deep gold, and smells of almond, dried apricot and old wood. The regulations demand 29 months of ageing for the dry version and 53 for the Superiore Riserva. Almost nobody outside the island drinks it, the vineyard area has shrunk badly, and it is one of the great endangered wines of Europe.

Before you move on.

Sardinia's geology sets its wines: granite in the Gallura, limestone and schist in the mountainous interior, sand in the Sulcis.

Carignano del Sulcis grows as ungrafted old bush vines in sand, which phylloxera cannot cross; the rules still require alberello training.

The island is pastoral first: more sheep than people, Pecorino Sardo DOP, and nearly all Pecorino Romano is made here. Vernaccia di Oristano is aged under flor like sherry.
