Cannonau and Vermentino.

One red that turned out to be Grenache, and may or may not have got here first; one white that granite made famous. The two grapes that carry the island, the sub-zones that matter, and what to expect in the glass.

Two grapes account for most of what leaves the island in a bottle. Cannonau is the red: warm, herbal, dry and high in alcohol, grown all over Sardinia but at its best in the mountainous interior. Vermentino is the white: saline, bitter-edged and taut, at its most serious on the granite of the Gallura, where it holds Sardinia's only DOCG. Between them they cover more than half the island's vineyard, and both have an argument attached about where they came from.

Cannonau, or Grenache, or Garnacha.

Cannonau is genetically the same grape as French Grenache and Spanish Garnacha. That much is settled. What is not settled is the direction of travel. The conventional ampelographic view is that the variety arose in Aragon in northern Spain and spread through the Mediterranean territories the Crown of Aragon controlled, Sardinia among them from 1323. Against that, Sardinian researchers have pointed to archaeological grape remains on the island that they read as evidence the vine was here long before the Aragonese, which would make Sardinia the origin and Spain the borrower. The claim is taken seriously and is not accepted; you will see it stated flatly as fact on the island, and doubted flatly off it. Both positions are worth knowing, and neither is worth repeating as settled.

What the grape does here is less contentious. Cannonau ripens late, likes heat and drought, and gives a wine that is pale to medium in colour but high in alcohol, typically 14% and often more. The flavour is red cherry and dried strawberry over something drier and more savoury: macchia, the Mediterranean scrub of myrtle, rosemary and juniper that grows on every hillside, plus tobacco, leather and a faintly bitter herbal finish. Tannin is moderate and acidity is not high, so it drinks warm and open rather than tight.

Key facts.

Cannonau di Sardegna DOC (1972): at least 85% Cannonau, 90% for Classico. Minimum 12.5% alcohol, 13% for Riserva, which needs two years of ageing including six months in wood.

Three named sub-zones: Oliena (also labelled Nepente di Oliena), Jerzu and Capo Ferrato.

Vermentino di Gallura DOCG (1996), the island's only DOCG: at least 95% Vermentino, 13% minimum alcohol, from 23 communes of the granite north-east.

Vermentino di Sardegna DOC covers the whole island and is where most of the everyday bottles come from.

The three sub-zones.

Cannonau grows everywhere, but three places may put their own name on the label. Oliena, under the Supramonte near Nuoro, makes the most perfumed and structured version; its old name, Nepente di Oliena, comes from the drug of forgetfulness in the Odyssey and was borrowed by the poet Gabriele D'Annunzio, who wrote admiringly of the wine. Jerzu, high in the Ogliastra on the eastern slopes, is the mountain style: firmer, fresher, grown up to 700 metres and above. Capo Ferrato, on the south-east coast, is the warmest and ripest. Outside the sub-zones, the villages of Mamoiada in the Barbagia have become the island's most talked-about source, where growers work old ungrafted bush vines at altitude and make Cannonau with a lightness and perfume that surprises people expecting something heavy.

In the glass: Cannonau di Sardegna.

 Colour: Medium ruby, garnet at the rim with a few years.

 Nose: Red cherry, dried strawberry, myrtle and wild herbs, tobacco, leather.

 Palate: Warm and dry, medium tannin, moderate acidity, a herbal, faintly bitter finish.

 Alcohol: 13.5–15%.

 Serve: 16–17 °C; most drink well from two to eight years, the best Riserva longer.

 At the table: Porceddu, roast lamb and kid, game, aged pecorino, tomato-based pasta.

Vermentino and the granite.

Vermentino arrived from the Ligurian and Corsican side of the Mediterranean and found in the Gallura the soil it needed. The decomposed granite there, coarse and quick-draining, holds no water and few nutrients, so the vine struggles, yields little and concentrates what it has. The wine that results is more mineral and more structured than Vermentino grown anywhere else, with real weight in the mouth and a distinct bitter twist at the end, like the pith of a citrus fruit. Its recognition as Vermentino di Gallura DOCG in 1996 made it the island's only DOCG, and it remains so.

The rest of the island grows Vermentino too, under the broader Vermentino di Sardegna DOC. Those wines are lighter, softer and cheaper, made for drinking cold in summer with shellfish, and they are what most people have actually tasted. The difference between a Gallura and a straightforward Sardegna bottling is worth paying for once, simply to learn what the granite does. Both share the island's signature: a saltiness that seems to come from the sea air, and a bitter almond finish that stops the wine from being merely refreshing.

Who to look for.

The cooperatives matter here as they do in Alto Adige. Cantina di Santadi in the Sulcis, advised for decades by the Piedmontese oenologist Giacomo Tachis, is the reference for Carignano; its Terre Brune showed in the 1980s that the grape could make a serious wine. Sella & Mosca near Alghero is the big historic estate. Argiolas in the south is the best-known private producer, and its Turriga blend put the island on international lists. Capichera in the Gallura made Vermentino expensive and proved it could age. For Cannonau in its lighter, more mineral register, look to the small growers of Mamoiada and to Jerzu. Prices across the board remain modest relative to the mainland.

Before you move on.

Cannonau is genetically Grenache; a Spanish origin is the conventional view, and the Sardinian counter-claim is contested rather than proven.

Cannonau di Sardegna DOC (1972) needs 85% Cannonau, with sub-zones Oliena, Jerzu and Capo Ferrato; Mamoiada is the new name to watch.

Vermentino di Gallura (1996) is the island's only DOCG, made on decomposed granite, and is saltier and firmer than Vermentino grown elsewhere.
