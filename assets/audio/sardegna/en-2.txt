Four wines, four rocks.

A knee-high bush vine at Sant'Antioco still stands on its own roots. It has never been grafted, and vines like it here have stood in the sand for more than a century. Almost nowhere else in Europe can say that, because almost everywhere the root louse got there first. What saved them is the ground they stand in.

Phylloxera moves through soil, and growers here have always held that it cannot move through sand. So the dunes of the Sulcis, in the south-west, kept vines that the rest of Europe lost from the 1860s onward, and many of them still stand on their own roots. The grape is Carignano, the same variety the Languedoc treats as bulk and blends away. Here it makes a dark, warm, black-fruited wine, with tannin softer than the colour promises.

The rules keep the old habit alive in one place only. Carignano del Sulcis needs at least eighty-five per cent Carignano, but the Superiore must come from vines trained as alberello latino, the free-standing bush — a shape of plant written into law. It is a small denomination, 314 hectares and about sixteen thousand hectolitres, on Sant'Antioco, San Pietro and the mainland behind them.

In the north-east the ground is granite, weathered coarse, and it holds neither water nor much to eat. A vine there struggles, carries little fruit and concentrates what it does carry. That is the whole argument for Vermentino di Gallura, which in 1996 became the island's only DOCG and remains so. It asks for at least ninety-five per cent Vermentino, against eighty-five for the Vermentino di Sardegna grown everywhere else. In the glass the difference is weight, and a bitter twist at the end like citrus pith.

Inland the rock changes again, to limestone and schist, and the vineyards climb. Cannonau is Garnacha, matched to it by DNA in 2009; domesticated grapes grew here from about 1300 BC, and nothing yet joins the two. What the altitude does is easier to taste than the genetics are to settle. At Jerzu the vines run to seven hundred metres and above, and the wine comes back firmer and fresher than the coast makes it.

What it does here is not in doubt. Cannonau covers about 5,400 hectares, twenty-nine per cent of the island's vineyard, and it ripens late in heat and drought. The wine is only medium in colour and high in alcohol, red-cherried and dry, finishing on the herbal bitterness of the scrub it grows among. Three places may put their own name on the label, and the one to know is Oliena, under the Supramonte. Outside them all, the growers of Mamoiada in the Barbagia work old ungrafted bush vines at altitude and make a Cannonau light enough to surprise anyone expecting weight. Most Cannonau is never bottled under the denomination at all — 2,228 hectares are claimed for it against the 5,400 planted.

In the lower valley of the Tirso the casks are deliberately left part empty. A film of yeast grows across the surface of the wine and lives there, feeding on it and shielding it from the air, and what comes out is Vernaccia di Oristano. It is the same biology that makes fino sherry. The specification goes as far as to require that the ageing cellars be ventilated: the film is written into the law.

Underneath it the wine turns deep gold and smells of almond, dried apricot and old wood. It must spend two years in cask, three as Superiore and four as Riserva, and reach fifteen and a half degrees. It was Sardinia's first DOC, in 1971, ahead of Cannonau and everything else. It is also nearly gone. The whole denomination bottles about thirty-four thousand bottles a year, which is roughly what Cannonau di Sardegna makes in a week. The first wine the island protected is the one it has almost stopped making.
