Porceddu, carasau and the pastoral table.

A kitchen built by shepherds, not fishermen. Bread that keeps for a year, a pig roasted over myrtle, pasta sealed like an ear of wheat, and a cheese that is against the law.

Sardinia is an island whose traditional cooking mostly ignores the sea. The coasts were malarial and exposed to raiders until well into the twentieth century, so the population lived inland and ate what the interior produced: sheep, goats, pigs, durum wheat, cheese and wild herbs. Fish entered the repertoire late and locally, around the lagoons of Cabras and the Catalan-speaking port of Alghero. Everything else on the Sardinian table comes from a pastoral economy in which food had to travel, keep, and be cooked over a fire in the open.

Bread you can read through.

Pane carasau is the island's emblem and a piece of practical engineering. Durum wheat semolina, water, salt and yeast are worked into a disc and baked until it puffs into a balloon; the baker splits the balloon into two paper-thin sheets and bakes each again until it is crisp. Twice-baked is what carasau means. The result keeps for up to a year in dry conditions, which is why shepherds who were away from the village for months carried stacks of it. Its other name, carta da musica, sheet music, describes how thin it is: you are supposed to be able to read a score through it. Archaeological remains put the bread on the island before 1000 BC.

It is eaten dry, snapped into pieces alongside cheese and salami. Brushed with olive oil, scattered with salt and rosemary and put back in the oven, it becomes pane guttiau. Softened with broth and layered with tomato sauce, pecorino and a poached egg, it becomes pane frattau, which is the closest thing the island has to a national dish.

Porceddu.

The celebration dish is porceddu, a suckling pig of four to six kilos, split, spitted on a green pole and set beside, not over, a fire of oak or juniper so that it cooks slowly in the radiated heat for several hours while its own fat bastes it. There is no marinade and no sauce. The seasoning is salt and, at the end, a scattering of myrtle: in the old practice the cooked pig was laid on a bed of myrtle branches and covered with more, so that it took the aroma up as it rested. The skin should shatter like glass and the meat underneath should be barely seasoned. It is shepherd cooking of the most confident kind, which is to say it does almost nothing to first-rate raw material.

Key facts.

Pane carasau: durum semolina, baked twice into paper-thin crisp sheets that keep up to a year. Oiled and re-baked it is pane guttiau.

Culurgiones d'Ogliastra IGP (2015): filled with potato, pecorino, mint and oil, and closed with the spighitta pleat that looks like an ear of wheat.

Fregola: semolina rolled into small irregular pellets and toasted, giving a nutty flavour; usually cooked with clams or tomato.

Casu martzu, pecorino deliberately colonised by cheese-fly larvae, is illegal to sell in Italy and the EU on food-safety grounds. It is still made privately and traded on the black market.

Pasta, in a pastoral idiom.

Sardinian pasta is made of durum semolina and water, no egg, and it is shaped rather than cut. Culurgiones, from the Ogliastra on the east coast, are filled not with meat or ricotta but with potato mashed with pecorino, mint and olive oil, and then sealed with a pleat worked by thumb and finger that leaves a raised seam like an ear of wheat. The pleat is called the spighitta, it takes practice to do quickly, and it was understood as a small charm for the coming harvest. They are served simply, with oil or a light tomato sauce and grated pecorino, and they earned IGP protection in 2015.

Fregola is the other distinctive shape: semolina rubbed with water in a wide bowl until it forms irregular pellets, which are then toasted in the oven. The toasting is the point, giving a nutty taste and a firmness that survives long cooking, and the classic treatment is fregola con arselle, cooked with the small clams of the lagoons. Malloreddus, ridged saffron-tinted gnocchetti, come with a sausage and tomato sauce that the island calls alla campidanese.

Cheese, legal and otherwise.

Beyond Pecorino Sardo and Pecorino Romano the island makes fresh sheep ricotta, the smoked ricotta mustia, and goat's cheeses in the mountains. And it makes casu martzu, which is a wheel of pecorino whose rind is opened so that the cheese fly Piophila casei can lay in it; the larvae digest the fat and turn the interior to a soft, pungent cream. It is banned from sale in Italy and across the EU on hygiene grounds, which has not stopped it: production is estimated in the region of a hundred tonnes a year, all of it private or black market. It is worth knowing about as a piece of the island's character rather than as something to seek out.

The end of the meal.

Seadas close it: a large disc of thin semolina pastry enclosing fresh, faintly sour sheep's cheese with a little lemon zest, fried until it blisters, and served hot under bitter corbezzolo honey, made from the strawberry tree. Sweet and savoury at once, and unlike anything on the mainland. Then mirto, the dark liqueur made by macerating myrtle berries, served cold from the freezer, which is where the meal properly stops.

Before you move on.

The cooking is pastoral, not maritime: bread that keeps for a year, meat cooked over open fire, cheese in every course.

Pane carasau is baked twice into paper-thin sheets; porceddu is suckling pig roasted beside the fire and rested on myrtle.

Culurgiones (potato, pecorino, mint, IGP 2015) and toasted fregola are the island's pasta; seadas with bitter honey and mirto end the meal.
