Food that had to keep.

The cheese fly is not an accident. To make casu marzu a shepherd shortens the brining, so that the salt stops the wrong bacteria without repelling the insect. He presses the wheels only lightly. He pierces the rind and drips oil into it to soften the crust and carry the smell, and then he leaves the wheel where Piophila casei will find it. Every one of those steps is written down in a document of the Regione Sardegna. The fly is invited.

Almost everything here exists because food had to survive a season without cold. Salt does most of the work and drying finishes it, and the island's answers are severe: a bread with no crumb, cheeses that harden rather than soften, roe pressed into a brick. The cost is that some of it sits close to the line where food stops being safe, and one cheese sits over it.

Whether casu marzu is illegal is usually told wrong. No law names it. What bars its sale is the general European rule that food is unsafe if unfit to eat, with an Italian statute of 1962 against selling food infested by parasites. The traditional-foods exemptions people cite cover the dairy's walls, not live larvae. The same regional document that records the cheese as heritage says it is eaten within the family, and where the form asks whether a hygiene derogation is sought, the answer entered is no. Listed as tradition and unsellable at once — and no prosecution appears anywhere in the record.

One dish refuses all of this. Porceddu is a suckling pig of thirty to forty-five days, taken at a carcass weight between five and nine kilos. It is split and set on a spit beside the fire rather than over it, so it cooks for hours in radiated heat while its own fat bastes it. There is no marinade and no sauce, and the seasoning is salt. By the old practice the cooked pig was then laid on a bed of myrtle branches and covered with more, so that it took the scent up as it rested. Nothing about it is preserved, kept, salted down or put by. It is eaten the day the animal is killed, which on an island that puts everything else by is the whole point of a feast.

A Sardinian bread oven runs between 450 and 550 degrees, and what goes into it is durum semolina, water, salt and a natural ferment, worked into a disc. This is pane carasau. The disc inflates into a ball in seconds. The baker pulls it out, splits the scalding ball into two sheets with one movement, stacks them under a light weight and puts them back to toast until they turn gold. Carasare is that second baking, and it is what the bread is named for. The result keeps for months, which is the entire point: a shepherd away from the village carried a stack of it.

Its other name is carta da musica, sheet music, for the thinness. Brushed with oil, salted and passed over embers it becomes pane guttiau. Pane frattau starts from the same sheets, softened and layered with tomato and grated pecorino under a poached egg. It is as close as the island comes to a national dish. Nothing dates it to the Nuragic age. No Nuragic bread has ever been excavated, and the heritage document makes no claim about how old the method is.

The rules for culurgiones are narrower than the stories about them. They come from the Ogliastra and their IGP dates from 2016. Mint is allowed in the potato-and-cheese filling, not required; garlic, basil and onion are allowed too. The dough is not the plain semolina and water of the rest of the island: the rules ask for soft wheat flour alongside the semolina, and for lard or butter or oil. What is left open is the flavouring. Each parcel is sealed by hand into a raised seam like an ear of wheat, with at least ten stitches in it.

Semolina rubbed with water in a wide earthenware basin gathers into irregular beads, and the beads are then dried and toasted in the oven. That is fregola. The toasting is a required stage, and it is where the nutty smell comes from and why the beads hold their bite in a wet pan. It is often said to descend from couscous. There is no evidence for it: the document usually cited is given without a source, no scholar argues the descent, and the state's own etymology is simply the Latin for crumbling. Two Mediterranean peoples rolled semolina between their hands, which is not the same as one teaching the other.

Both of the island’s other pasta shapes are made against something. Malloreddus are little ridged shells of semolina, rolled against a basket. The name is the diminutive of malloru, a bull; a dictionary of 1851 gives malloreddu as a bullock, which predates any cookbook. Tradition puts the saffron in the dough rather than the sauce.

In Nuoro a few women still make su filindeu by pulling dough into threads with their hands. Eight doublings give two hundred and fifty-six threads, laid in three crossed layers on frames of woven asphodel and left to dry. It is made for the pilgrimage to San Francesco at Lula and served in sheep broth. One kilo feeds thirty, and nobody can make more than two kilos in a day.

The mullet of the Stagno di Cabras give the island's most famous export. The ovary is lifted whole, salted, pressed, and dried in the wind for four to fifteen days. The silvery skin is left on as the mark that nobody has tampered with it, and the best of it sells for about two hundred and fifty euros a kilo. The lagoon covers 2,200 hectares and has been managed with movable gates since around 1300.

The figure nobody prints is how little of it there is. Cabras made about five and a half quintals in 2024, and by Slow Food's count the mullet of that lagoon account for roughly three parts in a thousand of the Sardinian bottarga on sale. The rest is packed on the island from roe landed off west Africa or in the Pacific, and sells for about half as much. Sardinia is less the producer of bottarga than its address.

A Sardinian meal ends with something fried rather than baked. Seadas are a disc of thin pastry around fresh sheep's cheese with lemon zest, fried until they blister and served hot under bitter corbezzolo honey from the strawberry tree. The cheese has to be young and acidic enough to pull into threads when it melts, which is why ricotta will not do and why the IGP granted in 2023 does not allow it. The honey is scarce because the tree flowers between November and February, when the cold keeps the bees in, and its bitterness comes from a single compound, homogentisic acid. After it comes mirto, myrtle berries steeped in alcohol and sweetened, served cold; the rules forbid distilling it, so what is in the glass is an infusion and nothing else.

One last thing belongs on this table. Broad beans are eaten across the island and are the heart of favata, the Carnival dish, and for a substantial minority of Sardinian men they are dangerous. Between seven and twelve per cent carry a deficiency of an enzyme that protects red blood cells, and fresh fava beans can break those cells within a day or two of eating them. In the north of the island 948 cases of acute haemolytic anaemia were recorded between 1965 and 1979, and ninety-four per cent of them followed a meal of fresh fave. Newborn screening from 1971 cut the numbers by three quarters. The deficiency is common here because it gave some protection against malaria. That is the same malaria that kept the population off the coast, so the island's blood and its settlement pattern have one cause between them.
