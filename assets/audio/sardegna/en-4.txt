Nuraghi: the Bronze Age towers.

Seven thousand stone towers, built without mortar by a civilisation that left no writing. What the nuraghi are, what they were for, the tombs and wells that go with them, and why the island still feels like its own country.

There are more than seven thousand nuraghi standing in Sardinia, and there were probably many more. No other island in the Mediterranean is covered in monuments like this, and no other prehistoric culture in western Europe built at this scale. The people who put them up left no writing, so everything we know about them comes from the stones themselves, from their bronzes, and from what the Phoenicians and Romans said about them much later. They are the reason Sardinia feels less like a region of Italy than like a country that happens to be inside one.

What a nuraghe is.

A nuraghe is a tower of dry-stone masonry, no mortar, built as a truncated cone. Inside, the chamber is roofed by corbelling: each course of stone laid slightly inward over the one below until the walls close overhead in a tall pointed dome, the same principle as the tholos tombs of Mycenae. The simplest are a single tower with one chamber. The most ambitious stack two or three chambers vertically and add corner towers linked by curtain walls, with a courtyard and a well inside and a village of round stone huts spreading around the base.

They were built from about the eighteenth century BC through the Bronze Age and into the Iron Age, and the culture that built them persisted until Roman colonisation in 238 BC and, in the interior, well beyond. What they were for was argued about for a century: fortresses, temples, tombs, chieftains' seats, astronomical instruments. The modern consensus is more domestic than romantic. They were defensible homesteads, holding people, stores and animals, which is why so many of them sit on ordinary farmland with a view of the next one rather than on strategic heights.

Key facts.

More than 7,000 nuraghi survive. The Nuragic period runs from around the 18th century BC to Roman colonisation in 238 BC.

Su Nuraxi di Barumini: UNESCO World Heritage since 1997. Its central keep is 18.6 m high with three stacked chambers; four corner towers surround it, and the village around it was lived in from the 13th to the 6th century BC. Excavated 1950–57 by Giovanni Lilliu.

Tombe dei giganti, giants' tombs, are collective burials with a curved forecourt of standing stones. Pozzi sacri, sacred wells, are underground chambers over a spring, reached by a precise stone staircase.

Bronzetti: small lost-wax bronze figures up to 39 cm, showing archers, wrestlers, chiefs, animals and ships.

Su Nuraxi.

The site to see is Su Nuraxi at Barumini, in the flat country south of the Giara plateau. It was a mound until 1949, when heavy rain washed part of it open and Giovanni Lilliu excavated it over seven seasons. What emerged was a central keep of basalt blocks, three chambers stacked one above another to 18.6 metres, ringed by four corner towers joined by a curtain wall, with a courtyard and a well in the middle; later an outer wall with seven lobes was thrown around the whole thing, and a village of round huts grew up outside. The keep dates to somewhere between the seventeenth and thirteenth centuries BC, the village was occupied from the thirteenth to the sixth. UNESCO listed it in 1997 as the best example of the form.

Tombs, wells and bronzes.

The towers do not stand alone. Tombe dei giganti are long collective tombs with a semicircular forecourt of upright slabs and a tall central stone, sometimes with a low opening at its foot; the name is folklore, and their exact function is still argued over. Pozzi sacri are the most refined thing the culture built: a stone staircase descending through a keyhole-shaped opening to a domed underground chamber over a spring, the masonry so exactly cut that it looks like later work. The well temple of Santa Cristina at Paulilatino is the finest, and its staircase is aligned so that at certain times the moon is reflected in the water.

And there are the bronzetti, a few thousand small figures cast by the lost-wax method, up to 39 centimetres tall. They show archers with horned helmets, wrestlers, mothers holding dead sons, chieftains with staffs and cloaks, bulls, deer, and model ships with animal-headed prows. They are the closest thing to a written record the civilisation left, and they are in the archaeological museums of Cagliari and Sassari.

A country of its own.

The isolation that preserved the nuraghi preserved the language too. Sardinian is not a dialect of Italian but a separate Romance language, and the most conservative one: its Logudorese form is measurably closer to Latin than any other surviving Romance speech. It has around a million speakers, recognised by the region in 1997 and by the Italian state in 1999, and UNESCO classes it as definitely endangered because the young are not learning it. Its two main written forms are Logudorese in the north and centre and Campidanese in the south. Two other tongues sit on top: Gallurese and Sassarese in the north, which came from Corsica and are Italian in type rather than Sardinian, and Catalan, still spoken in Alghero, where the Aragonese expelled the population in 1372 and repopulated the town from Catalonia. The street signs there are bilingual to this day.

One more modern claim attaches to the interior. The mountain districts of the province of Nuoro were identified in the early 2000s as a blue zone, a place with an unusual number of male centenarians, and the finding fed a global industry of diet books. It is worth treating carefully: later reviews have questioned how well the ages were verified and pointed out that no controlled study links the local diet to the longevity. What is not in doubt is that the Barbagia kept its own way of living longer than anywhere else in Italy, and that this is the same isolation that kept the towers, the language and the bread.
