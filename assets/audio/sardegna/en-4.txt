The towers and the language.

He is thirty-nine centimetres tall, cast in bronze by the lost-wax method, and he is drawing a bow. He wears a horned helmet. A few thousand figures like him survive — archers, wrestlers, chiefs with staffs, mothers holding dead sons, bulls, deer, and little ships with animal heads on the prow. They are the closest thing to a written record that the people who built the nuraghi left, because they left no writing whatsoever.

More than seven thousand nuraghi still stand, and there were once many more. A nuraghe is a tower of dry stone, no mortar anywhere in it, built as a truncated cone. The chamber inside is roofed by corbelling: each ring of stone is laid a little further in than the one beneath, and the rings close overhead in a tall pointed dome. The weight of the stone above holds each ring in place, which is why the thing has stood for three thousand years without a single binding agent.

The simplest are one tower with one chamber. The most ambitious stack two or three chambers vertically, add corner towers joined by curtain walls, and gather a village of round huts around the base. They were built from about the eighteenth century BC onward, and the culture that built them lasted until Rome took the island in 238 BC and, inland, well past that.

What they were for was argued about for a century: temples, tombs, castles, observatories. The answer the evidence now supports is more ordinary than any of those. They were defensible homesteads — places to keep people, stores and animals safe — and the giveaway is where they sit. A fortress is placed to command ground. These sit on farmland, in sight of the next one. They are spread at the density of farms, not of strongholds.

At Barumini a grassy mound lay unremarked until heavy rain opened it in 1949. Su Nuraxi came out of it, and Giovanni Lilliu dug it over seven seasons from 1950. Its central keep is basalt, three chambers stacked to 18.6 metres, and it was raised somewhere between the seventeenth and thirteenth centuries BC. Four corner towers surround it, with a courtyard and a well between, and a seven-lobed outer wall came later. The village outside was lived in from the thirteenth century BC to the sixth, and the site went onto the UNESCO list in 1997.

Beside the towers the same people buried their dead together, in what folklore calls the tombe dei giganti. A curved row of upright slabs makes a forecourt, with a tall carved stone at the centre, sometimes pierced by a low opening at its foot. The name is folklore and the purpose of the forecourt is still argued over.

The pozzi sacri are the finest thing the culture built. A stair descends through a keyhole-shaped opening to a domed chamber over a spring, and the stone is cut so precisely that the work was long assumed to be much later than it is. The well temple of Santa Cristina at Paulilatino is the one to see; its stair is aligned so that the moon is reflected in the water at certain times.

Rome took the coast and left the mountains alone, and the speech of the mountains stayed where it was. Sardinian is a Romance language in its own right, not a dialect of Italian, and its Logudorese form keeps more of Latin than any other Romance language does. Logudorese is written in the north and centre, Campidanese in the south. About a million people speak it, the region recognised it in 1997 and the state in 1999, and UNESCO calls it definitely endangered because the young are not learning it.

Two other tongues sit on top of it. Gallurese and Sassarese in the north came across from Corsica and are Italian in type rather than Sardinian. And Alghero speaks Catalan. Aragon took the town from the Genoese Doria in 1353; the inhabitants rebelled, and in 1354 they were forced out and replaced with settlers from Catalonia. The street signs are still bilingual, which is what a population replacement looks like seven centuries on.

One modern claim attaches to the same interior. In the early 2000s the mountain districts of Nuoro and the Ogliastra were identified as a blue zone, a place with an unusual number of men reaching a hundred. The finding went round the world with a diet attached to it. Whether such ages are recorded accurately is argued over, and this zone is among the best documented of them. What is distinctive here is long life for men: outside the zone Sardinian women reach a hundred at about two and a half times the male rate, and inside it the ratio is 1.35.

The explanation is another matter, and the researchers who found it say so. Their own papers call the mechanism unknown. What their later work associates with it is the labour: herding, steep ground, and the distance people covered on foot every day. A study of diet across 377 municipalities found the long-lived districts ate more animal food than the island average, and the famous paper on red-wine compounds never mentions Cannonau at all. What is left, when the diets and the bottles are set aside, is a population of men who walked up and down that limestone all their lives.
