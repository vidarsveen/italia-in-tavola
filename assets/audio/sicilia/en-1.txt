Etna: vines on a live volcano.

Europe's most active volcano is also Italy's most exciting wine region. Why the mountain works like a Burgundy of the south, and what Nerello Mascalese tastes like.

Twenty years ago Etna wine was drunk locally and ignored everywhere else. Today the mountain is the most talked-about vineyard in Italy, and the reason is a combination almost nowhere else has: an active volcano that resurfaces its own soil, vineyards from 400 to over 1,000 metres, vines that survived phylloxera and are older than anyone alive, and a red grape with the perfume of Pinot Noir and the grip of Nebbiolo.

The mountain as a vineyard.

Etna is 3,300 metres and changes height with every eruption. Vineyards ring it on three sides, and the DOC, created in 1968 and one of Sicily's first, is a horseshoe open to the west. The north face, around Randazzo, Passopisciaro and Castiglione di Sicilia, is the coolest and makes the most structured reds. The east, above Milo, faces the Ionian sea, gets the most rain and is the only zone allowed to label Etna Bianco Superiore. The south-east, around Trecastagni and Biancavilla, is warmer and earlier.

What makes it work is altitude on a southern island. At 900 metres in Sicily the grapes ripen slowly and hold acidity, and the temperature drops sharply at night. The soil is decomposed lava, sand and ash, black, loose and mineral-rich, so free-draining that vines root deep and, crucially, so sandy that phylloxera never took hold. Many vines are ungrafted and well over a century old, trained as alberello bush vines on terraces held by dry lava walls.

Contrade: the Burgundy comparison.

The comparison people reach for is Burgundy, and for once it is not lazy. Etna's slopes are divided into contrade, historic named parcels, and roughly 130 of them can appear on labels. Each has its own lava flow, of a known date, at a known altitude, and the wines really do taste different from one to the next. Growers bottle them separately in the way a Burgundian bottles a climat. The pioneers are named: Giuseppe Benanti from the 1980s, then the Belgian Frank Cornelissen, the Tuscan Andrea Franchetti at Passopisciaro and Marco de Grazia at Tenuta delle Terre Nere, who arrived around 2000 and made the world pay attention.

Key facts.

Etna Rosso: at least 80% Nerello Mascalese, up to 20% Nerello Cappuccio. Etna Bianco: at least 60% Carricante.

Etna DOC was created in 1968, among the first in Sicily; plantings have grown from a few hundred hectares in 2000 to well over a thousand.

Vineyards reach about 1,000 metres on the north face, among the highest in Europe.

The palmento, the old stone winery with a gravity-fed lava press, is a listed feature of the landscape; EU hygiene rules ended its commercial use.

Nerello Mascalese and Carricante.

Nerello Mascalese is late-ripening, thin-skinned and pale, which surprises people who expect southern reds to be dark and heavy. It gives a wine the colour of a light Burgundy, with red cherry, orange peel, dried herbs, smoke and a distinct iron-and-ash mineral note, high acidity and fine, sandy tannins. It is often blended with a little Nerello Cappuccio, which adds colour and softness but less structure.

The white is Carricante, grown mostly on the eastern face at Milo. Young, it is lean and green-apple sharp with a saline, flinty edge; with a few years it develops a waxy, almost Chablis-like depth, and it is one of the few Sicilian whites that genuinely ages. Minnella, a local white, is allowed in small amounts.

In the glass: Etna Rosso.

 Colour: Pale to medium ruby, garnet with age.

 Nose: Red cherry, wild strawberry, orange peel, dried herbs, wood smoke, iron.

 Palate: Medium body, high acidity, fine chalky tannin, long saline finish.

 Alcohol: 13–14%.

 Serve: 16 °C; good ones drink well from four years and keep fifteen.

 At the table: Grilled tuna, pork, mushrooms, pasta alla Norma, aged ragusano cheese.

Living with the volcano.

Working here is a calculated risk. Ash falls on the leaves and has to be washed off; lava flows have taken vineyards, most recently in the 1980s; and the roads are sometimes closed. Growers accept it because the same volcano makes the soil. Every eruption adds a new layer, and the age of a lava flow is part of a contrada's identity: a young flow gives a leaner, more mineral wine, an old and weathered one something rounder. It is the only wine region in Europe where the terroir is still being manufactured.

Before you move on.

Etna's altitude, sandy lava soil and old ungrafted alberello vines give cool-climate wines on a hot island.

Etna Rosso is Nerello Mascalese: pale, high-acid, smoky and mineral, closer to Burgundy than to southern Italy.

Contrade are named parcels, each a different lava flow, and the best growers bottle them separately.
