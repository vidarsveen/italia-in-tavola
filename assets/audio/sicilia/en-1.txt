Etna: vines on a live volcano.

At Milo, on Etna's eastern slope, a grower can make a white wine whose name is unavailable across the mountain. The grapes must come from the delimited part of this one municipality. Even a neighbouring vineyard with the same grape cannot put Etna Bianco Superiore on its label. The distinction begins with a place, long before anyone opens a bottle.

Milo faces the Ionian Sea above Sicily's east coast. The vineyards climb the lower slopes of Etna, far below the summit craters. The official description places most between 300 and 900 metres, with some reaching 1,100. Height and exposure change the growing season. A map of the appellation curves around the mountain from the north towards the east and south. It takes in parts of twenty municipalities, with its western side open. A vineyard on Etna is not automatically a vineyard inside Etna DOC.

On the northern slopes, growers around Randazzo and Castiglione di Sicilia work through a long ripening season. Trecastagni lies to the south-east; Biancavilla is on the south-western side. Those positions matter more than a simple division into a cold north and a hot south. Elevation, exposure and the sea all affect the temperatures a vine experiences.

Underfoot, lava has weathered into soils of different depths and textures, mixed with material deposited by other eruptions. Some ground is sandy and loose; elsewhere roots meet rock much sooner. A fresh flow can bury a vineyard. The lava must first weather into soil. Terraces give the grower somewhere to stand and work, but narrow steps and stone walls also restrict machinery.

The names that growers put on individual bottlings are often contrade, local districts within that larger boundary. A contrada can include several vineyards and more than one producer. Its name tells you where the grapes came from; it does not guarantee one lava flow, one soil or one rank of quality. The consortium's map distinguishes 133 of these districts. Comparing bottles from different contrade lets the drinker explore how the wines differ.

Giuseppe Benanti founded his modern estate in 1988, well before the current attention to Etna. His family's work extended across several slopes, making comparisons possible within one winery. Later arrivals included Andrea Franchetti at Passopisciaro and Marco de Grazia at Tenuta delle Terre Nere. Their separate vineyard wines helped bring local names onto labels seen outside Sicily. The comparison with Burgundy comes from this attention to place, rather than from an identical system of vineyard classification.

Most Etna Rosso depends on Nerello Mascalese. Its relatively light colour can conceal substantial tannin, while its acidity gives the red cherry and dried-herb flavours definition. Skin contact and ageing change the result, so a pale wine need not be a soft one. Nerello Cappuccio can add colour and a different balance to the blend. With food, the acidity and tannin can stand beside grilled pork or the fried aubergine in pasta alla Norma.

At Milo the emphasis shifts to Carricante, whose acidity can make a young white seem tight and austere. Alongside apple and citrus aromas, the wine may develop a fuller, waxier texture with age. Etna Bianco can include Catarratto and other permitted white grapes, including local Minnella where the rules allow it. The higher Carricante requirement in Bianco Superiore narrows the blend as well as the place of origin.

Some old vines still grow on their own roots, often trained low as alberello, individual bush vines. Their survival does not mean phylloxera spared the whole mountain. The appellation's history records a severe crisis in Catania's vineyards after the insect arrived.

The old stone wineries, called palmenti, remain beside the terraces. Their basins allowed crushed grapes and juice to pass down through the building by gravity. They make the scale of the earlier harvests visible even where vines have disappeared. Outside, the terrace walls still have to be repaired by someone who can lift and fit the lava stones.
