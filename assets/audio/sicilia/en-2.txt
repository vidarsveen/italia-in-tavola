Nero d'Avola, Grillo and Marsala.

Off the volcano, Sicily is a whole wine country: a dark southern red, crisp whites made possible by refrigeration, one DOCG, and the fortified wine an Englishman invented in 1773.

Sicily grows more vines than any other Italian region and for most of the twentieth century sold nearly all of it in bulk, shipped north to give body to thin wines in France and Piemonte. The turnaround since about 1990 has been dramatic, and it has come from three things: better clones and lower yields, refrigeration in the cellar, and a decision to bet on Sicilian grapes rather than Chardonnay and Merlot.

Nero d'Avola.

Nero d'Avola, "the black of Avola", is the island's signature red and its most planted. Grown badly it is jammy and dull; grown well, especially in the limestone hills of the south-east around Noto and Pachino, it gives a wine of black cherry and plum with liquorice, tobacco and a savoury, slightly bitter finish, with firmer acidity than its ripeness suggests. Its old name, Calabrese, is thought to come not from Calabria but from a dialect phrase meaning "grape of Avola".

The island's only DOCG is here: Cerasuolo di Vittoria, promoted in 2005, a blend of 50–70% Nero d'Avola with 30–50% Frappato. Frappato is pale, light and intensely perfumed, all strawberry and violets, and the blend is the island's most charming red: bright and cherry-scented, meant to be drunk within a few years and served slightly cool.

The whites.

Sicily's whites were once oxidised and flat, because the grapes arrived warm and the cellars were warmer. Temperature control changed everything. Grillo, a nineteenth-century crossing of Catarratto and Zibibbo bred for Marsala, turned out to make excellent dry white: citrus, white peach, herbs and a saline finish, with enough body to handle a little oak. Catarratto is the most planted white and the workhorse. Inzolia (Ansonica) is softer and nuttier. Carricante belongs to Etna, and on the far western island of Pantelleria the aromatic Zibibbo, or Muscat of Alexandria, is grown in hollows dug to shelter each bush vine from the wind, a technique on UNESCO's intangible heritage list since 2014.

Key facts.

Sicily has one DOCG (Cerasuolo di Vittoria), around 23 DOCs, and the island-wide Sicilia DOC created in 2011.

Roughly a quarter of the island's vineyard is now organic, the highest share in Italy, helped by dry, windy summers.

The alberello of Pantelleria, bush vines grown in wind-sheltering hollows, joined UNESCO's intangible cultural heritage list in 2014.

Grillo was created around 1873 by crossing Catarratto with Zibibbo, and was bred for Marsala rather than for table wine.

Marsala.

In 1773 an English merchant named John Woodhouse, sheltering from a storm in the port of Marsala on the west coast, tasted the local wine, which was aged in barrel and topped up in a solera-like way. He thought it could substitute for Port and Madeira in the British market, added spirit to survive the voyage, and shipped fifty barrels. It worked. Nelson ordered it for the Mediterranean fleet in 1798, and by the nineteenth century Marsala was one of Europe's great fortified wines, with Ingham, Whitaker and later the Sicilian Florio building an industry.

Then it was ruined by success: cooking Marsala, sweetened and salted so it could avoid drinks tax, destroyed the name. Serious Marsala still exists and is worth knowing. It is classified by colour (oro, ambra, rubino), by sweetness (secco, semisecco, dolce) and by age: Fine at one year, Superiore at two, Superiore Riserva at four, Vergine at five and Vergine Stravecchio at ten. Vergine is dry, unsweetened and nutty, closer to an amontillado sherry than to anything in the kitchen cupboard.

The sweet wines.

Two dessert wines deserve the last word. Passito di Pantelleria is Zibibbo dried in the sun on the volcanic island between Sicily and Tunisia, giving an intense amber wine of apricot, fig, honey and orange peel; it is the classic partner for cannoli and for the island's own blue cheese. And Malvasia delle Lipari, from the Aeolian islands, is lighter, floral and less sweet, tasting of dried apricot and Mediterranean scrub.

In the glass: Cerasuolo di Vittoria.

 Colour: Bright cherry red, light to medium.

 Nose: Sour cherry, strawberry, violet, pomegranate, a little white pepper.

 Palate: Medium body, fresh acidity, soft tannin, juicy and clean.

 Alcohol: 13–13.5%.

 Serve: 14–16 °C, slightly cool; drink within five years.

 At the table: Caponata, grilled vegetables, tuna, sausage, tomato pasta.

Before you move on.

Nero d'Avola is Sicily's signature red; blended with Frappato it makes Cerasuolo di Vittoria, the island's only DOCG.

Grillo, Catarratto and Inzolia make the whites; refrigeration is what made them good.

Marsala was invented for the British market in 1773; look for Superiore Riserva or Vergine, not the cooking version.
