Nero d'Avola, Grillo and Marsala.

A bottle of Marsala can be dry. That is easy to miss if the only one you know stands beside the cooking oil. In a glass of Marsala Vergine, the nuttiness comes with the warmth of added spirit and years in wood. The rules forbid the additions of cooked must, concentrated must and fortified grape must allowed in other styles. Before choosing food for it, the drinker needs to know which Marsala is in the glass.

The English merchant John Woodhouse arrived at Marsala in 1773 and began exporting the local wine with added spirit. Florio's account of its archives distinguishes that trade from the stories surrounding his arrival. Whether the spirit chiefly protected the cargo or adjusted the wine to British taste remains less certain. Wine was already being made and aged here; Woodhouse found a market for a particular version of it.

A surviving naval order gives the trade a firmer date. In 1800, Admiral Nelson ordered five hundred large wine casks, called pipes, of Marsala for his fleet. Benjamin Ingham and the Whitakers developed the business after Woodhouse, and Vincenzo Florio founded his own cellars in 1833. Florio built on the waterfront, where barrels could reach the ships and buyers that made the trade possible.

Fortification increases a wine's alcohol by adding grape spirit. It does not automatically make the wine sweet. Marsala's label separates sweetness from colour and ageing: a dry wine and a sweet one can both be called Superiore. Cooked must is part of the amber style, while Vergine excludes it. The simplest useful comparison is between a dry Vergine and a sweeter Marsala, served separately rather than hidden in a sauce.

The same western vineyards also supply whites intended to be drunk young. Grillo, associated with Marsala, makes dry wine with citrus and herbal aromas and enough substance for a meal. Genetic work identifies its parents as Catarratto and Zibibbo, also known as Muscat of Alexandria. The aromatic parent does not make every Grillo smell like a sweet Muscat.

Growers also use Catarratto for dry whites, while Inzolia, known as Ansonica elsewhere, can give a softer, nutty impression. Temperature control helps preserve fresh aromas during winemaking. It is one part of the change towards bottled wines, alongside vineyard choices and decisions about harvest and ageing. The old picture of an island that produced only anonymous bulk wine leaves out the trade in Marsala itself.

Across the island in the south-east, Nero d'Avola can give dark cherry and plum flavours with a savoury finish. Its other name, Calabrese, still appears in the official vocabulary. Avola and the neighbouring country around Noto and Pachino belong to the grape's southern geography, but it is grown much more widely.

Near Vittoria, winemakers combine Nero d'Avola with Frappato, a lighter grape with red-fruit perfume. In Cerasuolo di Vittoria, Nero d'Avola supplies much of the depth while Frappato brings fragrance and freshness. The permitted proportions leave room for different balances. A wine with half Frappato can be quite different from one with thirty per cent, even before soil and cellar choices enter.

Further west, on Pantelleria, farmers prune Zibibbo into low bushes inside hollows dug in the ground. The hollows help shelter the plants from wind and are maintained as part of the yearly work. UNESCO recognised this agricultural practice in 2014.

Zibibbo is Muscat of Alexandria, and it can become dry wine as well as Passito di Pantelleria. For passito, drying removes water from the grapes and concentrates what remains. At Donnafugata, dried berries are added to fermenting fresh must for Ben Ryé. The result combines sweetness with aromas of apricot and citrus peel. Malvasia delle Lipari, from the Aeolian Islands off Sicily's north coast, offers another island tradition of sweet wine from dried grapes.

The vines on Pantelleria stay close to the ground through the growing season. At harvest the worker bends into each hollow to cut the bunches by hand.
