Rice, aubergines and the Sicilian kitchen.

A cook closes a handful of rice around ragù, coats it in breadcrumbs and lowers it into hot oil. The outside must become crisp before the filling escapes. Inside, the rice has to hold its shape without becoming a solid lump. An arancino depends on those small decisions, whether the person selling it uses that name or calls it an arancina.

In Palermo, the feminine form and the round shape are familiar; in Catania, the masculine form and pointed versions are common. The boundary is less tidy than the usual argument suggests. The Accademia della Crusca accepts both names and traces the written evidence back to a Sicilian dictionary of 1857. That entry describes a sweet rice preparation shaped like an orange. It establishes a name and a dish, without establishing today's ragù filling or an unbroken recipe from medieval times.

Rice, saffron and frying are often gathered into a single story about Arab rule. Sicily's Islamic period changed agriculture and the movement of ingredients, but an ingredient's arrival does not date a finished dish. The arancino in a present-day shop has its own history of changing fillings and forms. Ragù and peas make one version; ham and cheese make another, commonly called al burro.

The rice is cooked and cooled before shaping. Cooling makes it easier to handle, while the coating holds a dry surface against the oil. Too much wet filling weakens the enclosure. The home cook's difficult moment is closing the rice evenly around the centre, with no thin patch through which the sauce can break. The first bite should reach filling as well as rice.

In caponata, aubergine is fried before it meets the other vegetables and the sweet-sour dressing called agrodolce. Frying browns the surface and gives the flesh a soft interior. Vinegar supplies acidity; sugar tempers it. Celery, olives and capers interrupt the softness with crunch, salt and sharper flavours. Recipes vary across the island, so a single list of ingredients cannot settle which version is authentic.

The finished caponata is left to cool rather than rushed from the pan to the table. Its flavours can settle together without more cooking. For pasta alla Norma, the fried aubergine instead joins tomato, basil and grated ricotta salata, the salted cheese that gives the dish its finish. The aubergine supplies richness; the tomato keeps each mouthful from becoming heavy. The name belongs to Catania and Bellini's opera, though the familiar tale about a writer naming it at dinner is usually told without a contemporary record.

With pasta con le sarde, the cook has another balance to manage. Sardines bring oily richness, wild fennel an anise scent, and raisins a little sweetness. Pine nuts add texture, and many versions include saffron. Toasted breadcrumbs can finish the dish. Fennel stalks and fronds are different from the mild bulb sold as a vegetable, so a substitution changes the flavour.

Palermo's markets make a meal possible without a dining room. At Ballarò and Capo, a bun can hold panelle, chickpea-flour fritters whose cooked batter sets before it is sliced and fried. A piece of sfincione offers something softer: thick bread with onion, tomato, anchovy and breadcrumbs. The topping soaks into the raised dough instead of sitting on a thin pizza base.

Other stalls use parts of an animal absent from many restaurant menus. Pani ca' meusa is a spleen sandwich, commonly including lung, heated in fat and served in a roll. Stigghiola brings intestines to the grill. These are specific preparations with different textures and cooking methods, rather than interchangeable examples of using leftovers. At Trapani, couscous with fish broth belongs to another coastal kitchen. Steamed semolina absorbs the broth while the fish can be served alongside.

As with arancini, the contrast between a crisp outside and a moist centre matters at the pastry counter. A cannolo begins as a tube of fried dough. Sweetened sheep's-milk ricotta is the traditional filling, often with candied peel or pistachio at the ends. Moisture gradually moves from the filling into the shell, so filling close to serving helps preserve the crunch.

In cassata, traditionally associated with Easter, ricotta goes with sponge cake, marzipan and candied fruit. The almond paste also appears as frutta martorana, shaped and painted to resemble fruit. Its association with Palermo's Martorana convent survives in the name.

For breakfast, the contrast may be cold granita and a soft brioche col tuppo, with its little raised knot. Stirring during freezing limits the growth of large ice crystals; the texture varies by flavour and local practice. Lemon, almond and coffee give very different breakfasts. The old trade in mountain snow belongs to the history of cooling food, but it does not supply a dated recipe for every modern granita.

The bun softens where it meets the granita.
