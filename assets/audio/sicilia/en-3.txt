Arab Sicily: citrus, sugar and street food.

Two hundred years of Arab rule left Sicily with rice, sugar, citrus, aubergines and almonds. What that produced is the most distinctive regional kitchen in Italy.

Sicily has been Greek, Roman, Byzantine, Arab, Norman, Swabian, French and Spanish, and the kitchen kept something from each. The deepest layer is the Arab one. Between 827 and 1091 the island was ruled from Kairouan and then Palermo, and the Emirate of Sicily brought irrigation, and with it rice, sugar cane, citrus, aubergines, spinach, saffron, cinnamon, raisins and almonds. Nearly every dish this reading describes depends on one of them.

The sweet and sour tradition.

The signature of Sicilian cooking is agrodolce, sweet and sour: sugar or raisins against vinegar. Caponata is the emblem, fried aubergine with celery, onion, olives, capers and tomato, finished with vinegar and sugar and eaten at room temperature, better on the second day. Sarde a beccafico are sardines stuffed with breadcrumbs, pine nuts and raisins and rolled to look like the little songbirds the nobility ate. Pasta con le sarde, the dish of Palermo, combines fresh sardines, wild fennel, pine nuts, raisins and saffron on bucatini: Arab pantry, Sicilian sea, in one bowl.

Two more dishes carry the island's name abroad. Pasta alla Norma, from Catania, is tomato, fried aubergine, basil and salted ricotta, named after Bellini's opera by a local writer who called it a masterpiece. And in Trapani, on the west coast facing Tunisia, they eat couscous, steamed by hand and served with fish broth, the most direct survival of North Africa on an Italian table.

Street food.

Palermo has one of Europe's great street-food cultures, sold in the markets of Ballarò, Capo and Vucciria. Arancini are balls of saffron risotto rice around a filling of ragù and peas (al ragù) or ham and cheese (al burro), breaded and deep-fried. Note the argument: they are arancine, feminine and round, in Palermo, and arancini, masculine and often conical, in Catania, and people care.

Beyond them: panelle, chickpea-flour fritters in a bun; sfincione, a thick spongy pizza with onion, anchovy, breadcrumbs and no mozzarella; pani ca' meusa, a roll of spleen and lung boiled and dipped in lard, eaten schietto (plain) or maritata (married, with ricotta and caciocavallo); and stigghiola, grilled skewers of intestine. It is offal-heavy, cheap and unapologetic.

Key facts.

Arab rule (827–1091) brought irrigation, citrus, sugar cane, rice, aubergine, saffron and almonds to the island.

Pistachios of Bronte, grown on Etna's lava, are harvested only in odd-numbered years and are among the world's most expensive.

Sicilian blood oranges (Tarocco, Moro, Sanguinello) get their colour from anthocyanins produced by cold nights near Etna.

Sea salt from Trapani, evaporated in the windmill-driven pans along the western coast, has been produced since Phoenician times.

Ricotta, sugar and ice.

Sicilian pastry is the best in Italy and it is built on sheep's-milk ricotta and on sugar. Cannoli are tubes of fried dough, blistered with wine and lard in the dough, filled to order with sweetened ricotta and finished with pistachio or candied peel; filled in advance they go soft, which is how to spot a bad one. Cassata is a dome of sponge, ricotta, marzipan and candied fruit, an Easter cake whose green marzipan and baroque decoration come straight from the Arab and Spanish courts. Frutta martorana, marzipan modelled and painted as fruit, was made by nuns at the Martorana convent in Palermo.

And granita, which descends from Arab sharbat and the medieval trade in snow carried down from Etna and the Madonie in insulated pits. It is not a slush of ice shavings but a slowly churned, smooth semi-frozen water ice, and in Sicily it is breakfast: almond, lemon, coffee, mulberry or pistachio, eaten with a brioche col tuppo for dipping.

Before you move on.

Arab rule brought citrus, sugar, rice, aubergine, saffron and almonds, and with them the sweet-sour agrodolce style.

Caponata, pasta alla Norma, pasta con le sarde and Trapani couscous are the defining dishes.

Cannoli, cassata and granita are built on sheep's ricotta, sugar and the old trade in mountain snow.
