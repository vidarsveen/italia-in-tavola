Temples, Normans and the Baroque.

In the walls of Agrigento's Temple of Concordia, openings cut for a church interrupt the Greek masonry. The building survived through use and alteration. Its conversion in the sixth century gave a pagan temple another congregation, centuries after the people who raised its columns had gone. What stands on the ridge is both a Greek building and the result of decisions made much later.

Ancient Akragas, now Agrigento, was one of the Greek cities established in Sicily after settlers began arriving in the eighth century BC. The area called the Valley of the Temples follows a ridge rather than the bottom of a valley. Concordia dates from the fifth century BC. Its later use as a church helped keep its structure intact, though preservation across the site has many causes and many periods of repair.

At Segesta, unfinished details invite a different question: how far did the builders get? The unfluted columns preserve a stage normally removed during finishing. At Selinunte, fallen blocks and reconstructed columns show the visitor different stages of damage and restoration.

On the east coast, Syracuse grew into a major Greek city. Its theatre still provides a setting for performances, while the ancient theatre at Taormina was extensively remodelled by the Romans. Inland, the Villa Romana del Casale at Piazza Armerina preserves elaborate mosaics from a late Roman residence. Calling it simply a hunting lodge reduces a much larger household to the subject of some of its pictures.

The Normans who conquered Sicily in the eleventh century commissioned buildings from craftsmen with different traditions. In Palermo's Cappella Palatina, Christian mosaics share the interior with a painted wooden ceiling made by Islamic craftsmen. Its muqarnas are recessed, interlocking forms that break the surface into small compartments. Craftsmen from different traditions worked on the same interior.

Under Roger II, that court also supported the geographer al-Idrisi. His work, completed in 1154, describes a settlement at Trabia with running water, mills and a trade in itriya, a form of pasta. The products went by ship to Muslim and Christian destinations. This is evidence of organised production and export. It does not identify the first person to invent pasta, or turn every later Sicilian pasta dish into a Norman recipe.

The water and mills belong in the same account as the cargo. Grain had to become flour before it became a food that could travel. The description places the work outside the royal dining room, in a producing settlement along the coast.

At Monreale, the mosaic-covered cathedral and its carved cloister extend the story of royal patronage beyond Palermo. With Cefalù and seven monuments in Palermo, it forms a UNESCO site of nine components. The group includes a bridge as well as churches and palaces. Its unity lies in the combination of building and decorative traditions, rather than in every monument serving the same purpose.

After the earthquake of 1693, towns in the south-east rebuilt in different ways. At Noto, rebuilding took place on a new site. At Catania it largely stayed on the old one, while Ragusa developed both an upper town and the rebuilt settlement of Ragusa Ibla. There was no single response imposed on every town. The similar dates and the spread of late Baroque architecture connect places that made different decisions about their ground.

In Modica and Scicli, staircases, church fronts and balconies shape the streets as much as individual buildings do. The rebuilt towns remained places to live and work. Their churches faced markets, houses and workshops, and the squares people cross on an ordinary morning are part of the same rebuilding. The architecture belongs to that daily movement as well as to a photograph of a façade.

At Taormina, the view reaches beyond the ancient stage towards Etna. The theatre combines Greek origins with Roman changes; behind it, the volcano continues to alter its own summit. Etna entered UNESCO's natural World Heritage List in 2013 for its geological importance. The cultivated slopes below belong to a different scale of work: terraces, vineyards and villages between the sea and the craters.

From a seat above the stage, the gaps in the masonry leave the mountain visible.
