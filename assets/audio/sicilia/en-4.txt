Temples, Normans and the Baroque.

Three thousand years of conquest left the island covered in monuments: Greek temples, Arab-Norman churches with gold mosaics, and a whole baroque region rebuilt after an earthquake.

Sicily sits in the middle of the Mediterranean, which has been its fortune and its misfortune. Everyone who wanted to control the sea wanted the island, and each conqueror built. The result is that a single day's drive takes you from a Greek temple older than the Parthenon's rivals, to a Norman cathedral covered in Byzantine gold and inscribed in Arabic, to a town of honey-coloured baroque built in one go after an earthquake.

Greek Sicily.

Greek colonists arrived from the eighth century BC and made Syracuse one of the largest and richest cities of the Greek world, rival to Athens, home to Archimedes and to a Greek theatre still used for classical drama each summer. Their temples survive better here than in Greece. At Agrigento, ancient Akragas, the Valley of the Temples runs along a ridge above the sea with the remains of seven Doric temples; the Temple of Concordia, from about 440 BC, is almost complete because it was turned into a church in the sixth century AD and so was never quarried for stone.

Elsewhere: Selinunte, the largest archaeological park in Europe, its temples thrown down by earthquake and left as they fell; Segesta, a single unfinished temple on an empty hill; and, inland at Piazza Armerina, the Villa Romana del Casale, a late-Roman hunting lodge whose 3,500 square metres of floor mosaics were preserved by a landslide.

Arab-Norman Palermo.

The Normans took Sicily from the Arabs between 1061 and 1091, and then did something unusual for the eleventh century: they kept the administration, the architects and the craftsmen they found. Under Roger II, crowned king in 1130, the court at Palermo worked in Arabic, Greek and Latin, and the buildings mix all three traditions. The Cappella Palatina in the royal palace has Byzantine gold mosaics, a Fatimid painted wooden muqarnas ceiling and Latin dedications. The cathedral at Monreale, finished about 1182, holds 6,500 square metres of mosaic telling the whole Bible, and its cloister has 228 paired columns, each carved differently.

Key facts.

Seven Arab-Norman monuments in Palermo, plus the cathedrals of Cefalù and Monreale, became a UNESCO World Heritage site in 2015.

The Valley of the Temples has been UNESCO-listed since 1997; the late baroque towns of the Val di Noto followed in 2002.

Roger II's court produced the Tabula Rogeriana of 1154, the most accurate world map for the next three centuries, made by the Arab geographer al-Idrisi.

The 1693 earthquake killed roughly 60,000 people and destroyed at least 45 towns in south-eastern Sicily.

The Val di Noto.

On 11 January 1693 an earthquake estimated at magnitude 7.4 destroyed the whole south-east of the island. The rebuilding, over the following decades, produced something rare: a group of towns designed and built in a single style at a single moment. Noto was moved to a new site and laid out on a grid, its churches and palaces in a soft golden limestone that turns amber in low sun, with curved façades, wide external staircases and balconies held up by carved grotesques. Ragusa Ibla, Modica, Scicli and Catania, the last rebuilt in black lava and white limestone, belong to the same moment.

Etna and Taormina.

Above all of it stands Etna, a UNESCO natural World Heritage site since 2013 and the most active volcano in Europe, erupting most years. Greek myth put the forge of Hephaestus beneath it and the giant Typhon under the mountain; Empedocles was said to have jumped into the crater. From the Greek theatre at Taormina, cut into the hillside in the third century BC and rebuilt by the Romans, the volcano frames the stage, which is why the view is on every poster of Sicily. It is the island in one image: a Greek ruin, a Roman remodelling, a live volcano behind, and vines on its slopes.

Before you move on.

Agrigento, Selinunte, Segesta and Syracuse preserve Greek Sicily better than most of Greece does.

Arab-Norman Palermo, Monreale and Cefalù mix Byzantine mosaic, Islamic craft and Norman structure; UNESCO-listed in 2015.

The 1693 earthquake produced the baroque towns of the Val di Noto, built in one style in one generation.
