Sangiovese: one grape, many faces.

Pour a Chianti Classico and a Brunello di Montalcino side by side and most people would guess they came from different countries. One is lighter and sharper, and smells of sour cherries and dried herbs. The other is darker and denser, and built to wait ten years or more in a cellar. They are the same grape, Sangiovese, grown an hour's drive apart. It is the most planted vine in Italy. Its wine tastes of the hill it grew on and of how carefully it was farmed, so a poor site or careless work shows in the glass.

Sangiovese is not an easy grape to grow. It buds early, so a spring frost can take the crop. It ripens late and its skins are thin, so a wet September can rot it on the vine. And it yields generously if it is allowed to, which is why so much Tuscan wine in the 1970s was thin and sour: the vines were carrying far more fruit than they could ripen. But a grape that reacts to everything also reacts to place. On a cool clay hillside at 500 metres it makes a pale, nervous, herbal wine. On the warm limestone slopes south of Montalcino the same vine makes something dark and long-lived.

The family has several members, and the names matter on a label. Sangiovese Grosso, the large-berried strain, is called Brunello in Montalcino and Prugnolo Gentile in Montepulciano. The smaller-berried Sangiovese Piccolo dominated old Chianti. Modern clonal research has blurred these lines, but the names survive, on the bottles and in local pride.

Sangiovese is high in acidity and tannin and only moderate in colour and body. It is medium ruby, and it turns brick at the rim as it ages, so a Sangiovese that looks black has usually been blended with something else. It smells of sour cherry above all, then of dried herbs, tomato leaf, leather and violets, and an old one smells of tea and tobacco. In the mouth it is savoury and firm. The acidity is bright, which is why it wants food, and the tannin runs from soft in a Morellino to austere in a young Brunello.

Four Tuscan wines are Sangiovese named after the place it grows. In the hills between Florence and Siena it is Chianti Classico, the historic heart, which has a chapter of its own. South of Siena the country is warmer and drier. There the hill town of Montalcino makes Brunello, the most powerful and expensive of the four, and Montepulciano, to its east, makes Vino Nobile, which is gentler and less famous. Down on the Maremma coast the grape gives Morellino di Scansano, a warm, plummy wine for drinking young.

On its own Sangiovese can seem thin and sharp. With food it tastes quite different, because the acidity cuts the fat of a bistecca and the richness of a ragù, and protein softens the tannin. Tuscans do not drink it as an aperitif. Over centuries it was shaped for a table of grilled meat, beans, bread and olive oil, and that table is the last chapter of this region.

Put the two glasses back side by side and the difference is no longer a puzzle. One grew in the higher, cooler hills between Florence and Siena. The other grew south of Montalcino, where the slopes are warmer and drier and the sea air reaches in from the Maremma.
