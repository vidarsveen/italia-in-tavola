Sangiovese: one grape, many faces.

Chianti, Brunello and Vino Nobile are all the same grape grown in different places. What Sangiovese tastes like, why it changes so much, and how to read it in the glass.

Ask what Tuscany's wine is and the answer is one word: Sangiovese. It is the grape of Chianti, of Brunello di Montalcino, of Vino Nobile di Montepulciano and of Morellino di Scansano, and it is the most widely planted variety in Italy. Yet a Chianti Classico and a Brunello can taste as different as two wines from different countries. Understanding why is the first lesson of Tuscany.

A grape that reports on its site.

Sangiovese is not an easy grape. It ripens late, buds early, has thin skins that rot in a wet September, and gives generously if you let it, which is why so much Tuscan wine was thin and sour in the 1970s. It is also one of the most transparent grapes in the world: it shows exactly where it was grown and how carefully. On a cool clay hillside at 500 metres it makes a pale, nervous, herbal wine; on the warm limestone slopes south of Montalcino it makes something dark, dense and long-lived. Same grape, different face.

The family has several members. Sangiovese Grosso, the large-berried strain, is called Brunello in Montalcino and Prugnolo Gentile in Montepulciano. The smaller-berried Sangiovese Piccolo dominated old Chianti. Modern clonal research has blurred these lines, but the names survive on labels and in local pride.

What it tastes like.

Sangiovese is a wine of acidity and tannin rather than colour and weight. The colour is medium ruby, turning brick with age; a Sangiovese that looks black has usually been blended with something else. The nose is sour cherry above all, with dried herbs, tomato leaf, leather, violets and, in aged wines, tea and tobacco. The palate is savoury and firm, with a bright acidity that makes it one of the world's great food wines and tannins that range from soft in Morellino to austere in a young Brunello.

Key facts.

Sangiovese covers about 10% of Italy's vineyard, more than any other grape; two-thirds of it is in Tuscany.

The name is usually explained as sanguis Jovis, the blood of Jupiter, though the derivation is uncertain.

Soils: galestro (friable marl) and alberese (limestone) in Chianti Classico; volcanic and clay soils in Montalcino; sandy clay in Montepulciano.

Outside Tuscany it is the grape of Romagna, Umbria's Montefalco Rosso and much of the Marche.

The Tuscan map.

Four names carry the grape's reputation. Chianti Classico, the hills between Florence and Siena, is the historic heart and the subject of the next reading. Montalcino, a hill town south of Siena, makes Brunello, the most powerful and expensive expression. Montepulciano, to its east, makes Vino Nobile, gentler and less famous. And the Maremma coast makes Morellino di Scansano, a warm, plummy Sangiovese for drinking young, alongside the Bordeaux-style Super Tuscans of Bolgheri.

In the glass: Sangiovese.

 Colour: Medium ruby, brick at the rim with age.

 Nose: Sour cherry, dried herbs, violet, leather, tomato leaf.

 Palate: High acidity, firm tannin, medium body, savoury finish.

 Alcohol: 13–14.5%.

 Serve: 16–18 °C; decant Brunello and Riservas.

 At the table: Grilled meat, tomato-based dishes, pecorino, bean and bread soups.

Why it needs food.

Sangiovese on its own can seem thin and sharp; with food it comes alive. That acidity cuts through the fat of a bistecca and the richness of a ragù, and the tannin is softened by protein. Tuscans have never drunk it as an aperitif. It is a wine designed, over centuries, for a table of grilled meat, beans, bread and olive oil, and the fourth reading is about that table.

Before you move on.

Sangiovese is Tuscany's one great grape: Chianti, Brunello, Vino Nobile and Morellino are all Sangiovese from different places.

Expect sour cherry, herbs, high acidity and firm tannin; medium colour, never black.

It is a food wine: acidity and tannin are made for meat, beans and bread.
