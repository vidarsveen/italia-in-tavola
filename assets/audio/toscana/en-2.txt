Chianti Classico and the black rooster.

For most of the twentieth century Chianti was a squat bottle in a straw basket with a candle stuck in its neck, on a trattoria table somewhere abroad. The wine inside was often poor. It was Sangiovese cropped too heavily, stretched with white grapes, and made under rules that had been written for volume. The wine began in the hills between Florence and Siena, and for the last forty years the growers there have worked to separate it from that bottle. They changed the rules, and Chianti Classico became one of Italy's finest wines.

Florence and Siena fought over these hills for centuries, and the legend says the border was settled by a race. Each city would send out a rider at cockcrow, and the frontier would be drawn where the two met. Siena chose a white rooster and fed it well. Florence chose a black one and starved it, so it crowed long before dawn, and the Florentine rider was almost at Siena's walls when they met. The Gallo Nero, the black rooster, became the emblem of the Chianti League in the fourteenth century and of the wine's consortium in 1924. Since 2005 it has been printed on the neck of every bottle of Chianti Classico.

The Chianti zone as a whole is enormous. It runs across most of central Tuscany, and its wine, plain Chianti, can be pleasant and cheap. Chianti Classico is only the original hill zone, about 7,000 hectares of vines among nine villages, and since 1996 it has been a separate denomination with its own rules.

In 1872 Baron Bettino Ricasoli, who had twice been prime minister of Italy, wrote down the recipe that became Chianti. It was mostly Sangiovese, with some Canaiolo for softness and a little white Malvasia so the wine could be drunk young. In 1967 that formula was made law, but the law allowed up to thirty per cent white grapes, most of them Trebbiano, and that came close to ruining the wine's reputation. Today's rules run the other way. A Chianti Classico is at least 80 per cent Sangiovese, it has had no white grapes at all since 2006, and the rest comes from Canaiolo, Colorino or international varieties.

As in the Langhe, the talk here is now of villages. Drive up to Radda and Gaiole, high and cool, and the wines are fine, taut and long-lived. Drop into the golden bowl of Panzano, the Conca d'Oro, and they are riper and rounder. Greve, closer to Florence, is warmer, and Castelnuovo Berardenga in the south, near Siena, is the ripest of all. Since 2021 the top tier, Gran Selezione, may carry its village on the label. That is the first step towards a map like Burgundy's, and the best producers already bottle single vineyards.

The fiasco, the round-bottomed bottle wrapped in sala straw, began as a practical invention: cheap blown glass that could not stand up on its own. It made Chianti the most recognised wine in the world, and then it became an embarrassment, the emblem of the wine's decline. Almost no serious producer uses it today, though a few keep it as a nod to history. If you see one, expect simple Chianti, not Classico. A bottle from Radda now carries a black rooster on its neck and no straw at all.
