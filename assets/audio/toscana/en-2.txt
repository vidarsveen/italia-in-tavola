Chianti Classico and the black rooster.

The wine that came in a straw flask, fell from grace and climbed back: the Chianti Classico zone, its rules, its rooster and how to choose a bottle today.

For much of the twentieth century Chianti meant a squat bottle in a straw basket, a candle stuck in its neck in a trattoria abroad. The wine inside was often poor: over-cropped Sangiovese stretched with white grapes, made by rules that were written for volume. The story of the past forty years is how the historic heart of the zone, Chianti Classico, separated itself from that image and became one of Italy's finest wines.

A border, a rooster and a race.

The zone lies in the hills between Florence and Siena, two republics that fought over it for centuries. The legend says the border was settled by a race: each city would send a rider at cockcrow, and the frontier would be drawn where they met. Siena chose a white rooster and fed it well; Florence chose a black one and starved it, so it crowed long before dawn and the Florentine rider was almost at Siena's walls when they met. The Gallo Nero, the black rooster, became the emblem of the Chianti League in the fourteenth century and of the wine's consortium in 1924.

The Chianti zone as a whole is enormous, running across most of central Tuscany, and its wine, plain Chianti, can be pleasant and cheap. Chianti Classico is only the original hill zone, about 7,000 hectares of vines among nine villages, and since 1996 it has been a separate denomination with its own rules.

The rules, then and now.

In 1872 Baron Bettino Ricasoli, later prime minister of Italy, wrote down the recipe that became Chianti: mostly Sangiovese, some Canaiolo for softness, and a little white Malvasia to make the wine drinkable young. That formula, with too much white grape, was made law in 1967 and nearly killed the wine's reputation. Today's rules are the opposite: at least 80% Sangiovese, no white grapes at all since 2006, the rest from Canaiolo, Colorino or international varieties.

Three tiers of Chianti Classico.

Annata: the everyday wine, released a year after harvest. Fresh, cherry-scented, for tomato and pasta.

Riserva: 24 months' ageing, more depth; the mid-week bottle of a serious cellar.

Gran Selezione: since 2014, the top: estate-grown grapes, 30 months' ageing, from 2021 at least 90% Sangiovese, and the option to name one of eleven villages (Unità Geografiche Aggiuntive) such as Radda, Gaiole, Panzano or Lamole on the label.

The villages.

Like the Langhe, Chianti Classico is learning to talk about places. Radda and Gaiole, high and cool, give fine, taut, long-lived wines. Panzano's golden bowl, the Conca d'Oro, gives riper, rounder ones. Castellina sits between them; Greve is warmer and closer to Florence; Castelnuovo Berardenga in the south, near Siena, is the ripest of all. The 2021 village names are the first step toward a Burgundy-style map, and the best producers already bottle single vineyards.

The straw flask.

The fiasco, the round-bottomed bottle wrapped in sala straw, was a practical invention: cheap blown glass that could not stand up alone. It made Chianti the most recognised wine in the world and then became an embarrassment, a symbol of the wine's decline. Almost no serious producer uses it today, though a few keep it as a wink to history. If you see one, expect simple Chianti, not Classico.

In the glass: Chianti Classico Riserva.

 Colour: Medium ruby with a garnet rim.

 Nose: Sour cherry, dried herbs, violet, leather, a hint of oak.

 Palate: Medium body, bright acidity, firm but polished tannin, savoury length.

 Alcohol: 13.5–14.5%.

 Serve: 17 °C; drink at 4–12 years.

 At the table: Bistecca alla fiorentina, pappardelle with wild boar ragù, aged pecorino.

Before you move on.

Chianti Classico is the historic hill zone between Florence and Siena; plain Chianti is a much larger, simpler area.

The rules moved from Ricasoli's white-grape blend to at least 80% Sangiovese with no white grapes; three tiers, Annata, Riserva, Gran Selezione.

Village names on the label (Radda, Gaiole, Panzano…) are the new way to read style.
