Brunello, Nobile and the Super Tuscans.

In 1944 Marchese Mario Incisa della Rocchetta planted Cabernet Sauvignon on his estate, Tenuta San Guido, near Bolgheri, on the Maremma coast, because he liked Bordeaux and the gravelly soil reminded him of the Médoc. The wine was for the family, and for a quarter of a century it went no further than his own table. Then his nephew Piero Antinori persuaded him to sell the 1968 vintage, in 1971. No Tuscan rule allowed Cabernet, so the wine went out as vino da tavola, table wine, the lowest category there was. It sold for more than Barolo. It was called Sassicaia.

Inland, two hill towns south of Siena make Sangiovese under rules stricter than Chianti's. Montalcino, the first of them, sits inside its walls forty kilometres from Siena, where it is drier and warmer than in Chianti and the sea air of the Maremma reaches it. In the 1870s Ferruccio Biondi-Santi isolated a strain of Sangiovese Grosso there that he called Brunello, the little brown one, and he made a wine to age rather than to drink young. His 1888 was still alive a century later. For most of the twentieth century Brunello was a handful of estates. Then in 1980 it became the first Italian wine to be awarded DOCG, and the handful grew to a hundred producers by the 1990s, and then to two hundred. In 2008 some of them were found to have blended in other grapes. The scandal ended with the rule reaffirmed: Brunello is Sangiovese and nothing else. It is still released five years after the harvest. The same vineyards also give Rosso di Montalcino, released after a year, which is the way to taste Montalcino without waiting.

Twenty kilometres east, the Renaissance town of Montepulciano makes Vino Nobile, noble wine, a name recorded in the seventeenth century when it supplied the papal court. The grape is Sangiovese again, called Prugnolo Gentile here, at least 70 per cent, with Canaiolo and Mammolo allowed. It is rounder than Chianti Classico and softer than Brunello, and often the best value of the three. Do not confuse it with Montepulciano d'Abruzzo, which is a grape, not a place.

Antinori followed his uncle's wine in 1971 with Tignanello: Sangiovese with Cabernet, no white grapes, aged in French barriques, and sold as table wine too, because it broke the Chianti rules. Solaia, Ornellaia and Masseto came after it, table wines priced like the finest Bordeaux. By the 1990s the law had caught up with them. Bolgheri got a DOC of its own. The rebels changed Italian wine on the way, and they forced Chianti Classico to rewrite its own rules.

Tuscany is red-wine country, but two other wines belong here. Vernaccia di San Gimignano, from the town of towers, was Italy's first DOC in 1966, a dry, slightly bitter white that Dante mentioned. And Vin Santo, holy wine, is made from Trebbiano and Malvasia dried on mats through the winter, then aged for years in small sealed casks under the roof. It comes out amber, nutty and sweet, and it is poured at the end of a Tuscan meal with almond cantucci for dunking.

The wine the marchese made for his own table now has an appellation of its own. And a Tuscan wine that follows no traditional recipe can still go out as IGT Toscana, the category invented for wines like his.
