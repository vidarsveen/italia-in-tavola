Brunello, Nobile and the Super Tuscans.

Tuscany's three other great reds: the Sangiovese aristocrat of Montalcino, its gentler cousin at Montepulciano, and the Bordeaux-blend rebels who broke the rules and won.

If Chianti Classico is Tuscany's everyday aristocrat, three other wines make up its nobility. Two are Sangiovese grown south of Siena, in Montalcino and Montepulciano, under rules stricter than Chianti's. The third is a family of wines that had no rules at all when they began, made from French grapes on the coast, sold as table wine, and priced like the finest Bordeaux. They are called the Super Tuscans, and they changed Italian wine.

Brunello di Montalcino.

Montalcino is a walled hill town forty kilometres south of Siena, drier and warmer than Chianti, with the sea's influence from the Maremma. In the 1870s Ferruccio Biondi-Santi isolated a strain of Sangiovese Grosso he called Brunello, "the little brown one", and made a wine to age rather than drink young; his 1888 was still alive a century later. For most of the twentieth century Brunello was a handful of estates. In 1980 it became the first Italian wine to be awarded DOCG status, and by the 1990s it was a hundred and then two hundred producers.

The rules of Montalcino.

Brunello di Montalcino: 100% Sangiovese, released five years after the harvest (six for Riserva), of which at least two in wood.

Rosso di Montalcino: the younger wine from the same vineyards, released after a year: the way to taste Montalcino without waiting.

The 2008 "Brunellogate" scandal, when producers were found blending in other grapes, ended with the 100% rule firmly reaffirmed.

Best vintages need 10–15 years and live for 30.

Vino Nobile di Montepulciano.

Twenty kilometres east, the Renaissance town of Montepulciano makes Vino Nobile, "noble wine", a name recorded in the seventeenth century when it supplied the papal court. The grape is Sangiovese again, locally Prugnolo Gentile, at least 70%, with Canaiolo and Mammolo allowed. It is aged two years (three for Riserva) and sits between Chianti Classico and Brunello in weight: rounder than the first, softer than the second, and often the best value of the three. Do not confuse it with Montepulciano d'Abruzzo, which is a grape, not a place.

The rebels of Bolgheri.

In 1944 Marchese Mario Incisa della Rocchetta planted Cabernet Sauvignon at his estate, Tenuta San Guido, on the Maremma coast near Bolgheri, because he liked Bordeaux and the gravelly soil reminded him of the Médoc. The wine, Sassicaia, was for family use until his nephew Piero Antinori persuaded him to sell the 1968 vintage in 1971. It had no denomination, because no rules allowed Cabernet in Tuscany, so it was labelled vino da tavola, table wine, the lowest category. It sold for more than Barolo.

Antinori followed in 1971 with Tignanello, Sangiovese with Cabernet and no white grapes, aged in French barriques, also sold as table wine because it broke the Chianti rules. Others followed: Solaia, Ornellaia, Masseto. By the 1990s the law caught up: Bolgheri DOC was created in 1994, Sassicaia was given its own appellation in 2013, and the anonymous IGT Toscana category was invented for wines that follow no traditional recipe. The rebels became the establishment, and along the way they forced Chianti Classico to reform its own rules.

In the glass: Brunello di Montalcino.

 Colour: Medium to deep garnet.

 Nose: Dried cherry, plum, leather, tobacco, balsamic herbs, dried flowers.

 Palate: Full body, powerful tannin, high acidity, very long finish.

 Alcohol: 14–15%.

 Serve: 18 °C, decanted for two hours; at ten years or older.

 At the table: Roast lamb, wild boar, aged pecorino di Pienza, bistecca.

A white and a sweet one.

Tuscany is red-wine country, but two others belong in the course. Vernaccia di San Gimignano, from the town of towers, was Italy's first DOC in 1966: a dry, slightly bitter white that Dante mentioned. And Vin Santo, "holy wine", is made from Trebbiano and Malvasia dried on mats through the winter, then aged for years in small sealed casks under the roof. Amber, nutty and sweet, it is poured at the end of every Tuscan meal with almond cantucci for dunking.

Before you move on.

Brunello: 100% Sangiovese from Montalcino, five years before release, Italy's first DOCG. Rosso di Montalcino is the early-drinking version.

Vino Nobile: Sangiovese from Montepulciano, gentler and better value; not related to Montepulciano d'Abruzzo.

Super Tuscans began as rule-breaking table wines at Bolgheri and Antinori; they ended up with their own appellations and reformed Chianti.
