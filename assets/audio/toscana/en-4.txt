Bread, beans and the Florentine steak.

At eleven in the morning a queue forms at a cart beside the market in Florence. The trippaio behind it lifts a piece of lampredotto out of a pot where it has simmered for hours with tomato and celery. It is the abomasum, the fourth stomach of the cow. He chops it on a board, dips a bun in the broth, spoons on green sauce and chilli oil, and hands it over in a square of paper. A builder in dusty boots eats his standing up, and so does the lawyer behind him. The most expensive dish in this city is a steak the size of a book. The cheapest are this bun and a bowl of yesterday's bread, and the same rule makes all of them: nothing is wasted, and every ingredient has to be good. Tuscans call their cooking cucina povera, the food of the poor, and they say it with pride. It is a kitchen of bread, beans, oil and greens, with offal on weekdays and meat on feast days.

The sit-down version is trippa alla fiorentina, tripe stewed in tomato and finished with Parmesan. Rome has the same habit and calls it the fifth quarter. The poor of the city ate what the rich would not touch, and made it into something the rich now stand in line for.

Bite into Tuscan bread and something is missing. Pane toscano is made without salt, and nobody has settled why. One story blames a medieval salt tax. Another says Pisa once blockaded Florence's salt and the city learned to do without. The plainest explanation is that everything eaten with the bread is salty already: prosciutto, pecorino, the fennel salame called finocchiona, anchovies. Florentines are content to blame Pisa. Whatever the reason, unsalted bread goes stale fast, often within a day, so half the Tuscan kitchen exists to use up yesterday's loaf.

In winter that means ribollita, a soup of cannellini beans, cavolo nero, carrot and onion thickened with stale bread. The name means re-boiled: it is cooked, left overnight and boiled again until a spoon stands up in it. When the tomatoes come, the bread is cooked with them, garlic and basil into a porridge called pappa al pomodoro. In summer it is soaked, squeezed and tossed with tomato, cucumber and onion as panzanella. And in November, when the new oil comes from the mill green and cloudy, a slice of bread is grilled, rubbed with garlic and drowned in it; that slice is called fettunta, the oiled slice. The oil is pressed from olives picked early, and it is peppery enough to make you cough. Tuscans take the cough as a sign of quality.

Tuscans are nicknamed mangiafagioli, bean-eaters, and the name was earned. Beans were the protein for most of the region's history, and they still are: white cannellini, and the smaller yellow zolfini, simmered until soft and dressed with nothing but oil, salt and pepper. A dish of them goes beside much else, and it goes beside the steak.

The steak begins with an animal. The Chianina is the huge white ox of the Val di Chiana, south of Arezzo, and it is one of the oldest cattle breeds in the world. From it the butcher cuts a T-bone at least four fingers thick and over a kilo, and hangs it to dry-age. The griller lays it over oak or olive-wood embers for a few minutes a side, then stands it on the bone so the heat reaches the middle. The outside chars and the inside stays red, al sangue. Salt and pepper go on afterwards, with a thread of oil, and never lemon. It is served for two or more and sliced from the bone at the table, with the beans beside it and nothing else. The wine is Chianti Classico, whose acidity was made for this fat. Ask for it well done and a Florentine waiter will look at you sadly.

For nearly five years the city could not sell it. Italy found its first case of mad cow disease in January 2001. From the first of April that year the European Union banned the sale of beef on the bone from any animal older than twelve months, because the spinal column was where the risk sat. A bistecca is beef on the bone from an animal of about eighteen months. Florence protested to Brussels, and the ban held until the end of 2005. The bone came back in stages, and from 2008 the steak was what it had always been.

Tuscany is not pasta country in the way Emilia is, but it has shapes of its own. In the hills around Siena, pici are rolled between the palms into thick, uneven strands and dressed with garlic and tomato, or with fried breadcrumbs. Pappardelle are cut wide because they are made for ragù di cinghiale, the wild boar that comes out of the Chianti woods in autumn. Autumn is the season of game here, pheasant and hare as well as boar, and of porcini from the Apennines. On the coast at Livorno the fishermen's stew is cacciucco, at least five kinds of fish cooked with garlic, chilli and tomato and poured over garlic bread. Meals end with almond cantucci dipped in Vin Santo.

From every road into Florence the first thing you see is the dome. Filippo Brunelleschi raised the dome of the cathedral between 1420 and 1436 without scaffolding from the ground. He built two shells, one inside the other, and laid four million bricks in a herringbone pattern so that each ring held itself up as it rose. It is still the largest masonry dome ever built. An hour to the west, Pisa's bell tower has leaned since 1178, when the builders reached the third storey and felt the ground give way under it. Engineers steadied it between 1990 and 2001, and it now tilts a safe four degrees. One is genius on a grand scale, and the other is a beautiful mistake that everyone has learned to love. The city that raised the one, and has never tired of the other's trouble, sits down each day to a steak the size of a book or a bowl of yesterday's bread.
