Bread, beans and the Florentine steak.

Tuscan cooking is the art of making poverty taste rich: unsalted bread, beans, olive oil, wild herbs and one enormous steak, eaten in the shadow of Brunelleschi's dome.

Tuscans call their own cooking cucina povera, poor cooking, and say it with pride. It is a kitchen of bread, beans, olive oil, greens and offal, with meat for feast days, and it became fashionable across the world precisely because it wastes nothing. Its two poles are a bowl of ribollita, which costs almost nothing, and the bistecca alla fiorentina, which is the most expensive thing on the menu, and both taste of the same principle: few ingredients, all of them excellent.

Bread without salt.

The first surprise is the bread. Pane toscano is made without salt, a habit variously blamed on a medieval salt tax, a Pisan blockade of Florence's salt supply, or simply on the saltiness of everything eaten with it: prosciutto, pecorino, finocchiona, anchovies. It goes stale fast, which is the point: half of Tuscan cooking is a way of using yesterday's bread. Ribollita, "re-boiled", is a soup of cannellini beans, cavolo nero, carrot, onion and stale bread, cooked, left, and boiled again until it is thick enough to stand a spoon in. Pappa al pomodoro is bread cooked to a porridge with tomatoes, garlic and basil. Panzanella is bread soaked, squeezed and tossed with tomatoes, cucumber and onion for summer. And fettunta, "oiled slice", is the simplest of all: grilled bread rubbed with garlic and drowned in new oil in November.

Key facts.

Tuscan olive oil is picked early and green; it is peppery enough to make you cough, which Tuscans take as a sign of quality.

Cannellini and zolfini beans are the region's protein; Tuscans are nicknamed mangiafagioli, bean-eaters.

The Chianina, the huge white cattle of the Val di Chiana, is one of the oldest breeds in the world.

Pecorino Toscano DOP is milder than the Roman kind; the version from Pienza is the most prized.

The steak.

Bistecca alla fiorentina has rules. It must be a T-bone or porterhouse from a Chianina steer, at least four fingers thick and weighing over a kilo, dry-aged. It is grilled over oak or olive-wood embers, a few minutes a side and then standing on the bone, so that the outside chars and the inside stays red: al sangue, and a Florentine waiter will look at you sadly if you ask for it well done. Salt and pepper go on after cooking, with a thread of oil; lemon never. It is served for two or more, sliced from the bone at the table, with cannellini beans and nothing else. The wine is Chianti Classico, whose acidity was made for this fat.

Lampredotto and the fifth quarter.

Florence's street food is offal. From carts around the markets the trippai sell lampredotto, the abomasum or fourth stomach of the cow, simmered for hours in broth with tomato and celery, chopped, piled into a bun that has been dipped in the broth, and dressed with green sauce and chilli oil. Florentines of every class eat it standing up at eleven in the morning. Trippa alla fiorentina, tripe stewed in tomato with Parmesan, is the sit-down version. This is the same logic as Rome's fifth quarter: the city's poor ate what the rich would not, and made it into something the rich now queue for.

Pasta, game and the coast.

Tuscany is not a pasta region in the way Emilia is, but it has its shapes: pici, thick hand-rolled strands from the Siena hills, with garlic and tomato or with breadcrumbs; and pappardelle, wide ribbons made for ragù di cinghiale, the wild boar that roams the Chianti woods and ends up in every trattoria. Game is autumn: pheasant, hare, boar, and porcini mushrooms from the Apennines. The coast at Livorno adds cacciucco, a fish stew of at least five kinds of fish with garlic, chilli and tomato, poured over garlic bread.

Under the dome.

All of this is eaten in the shadow of the building that announces Florence from every road: Brunelleschi's dome, raised over the cathedral between 1420 and 1436 without scaffolding from the ground, a double shell of four million bricks laid in a herringbone pattern that still makes it the largest masonry dome ever built. An hour west, Pisa's bell tower has leaned since builders reached the third storey in 1178 and found the ground giving way; it was stabilised between 1990 and 2001 and now tilts a safe four degrees. The two buildings bracket the region the way ribollita and bistecca bracket its food: one is genius on a grand scale, the other a beautiful mistake that everyone has learned to love.

Before you move on.

Tuscan cooking is cucina povera: unsalted bread, beans, olive oil and greens, turned into ribollita, pappa al pomodoro and panzanella.

The bistecca is Chianina T-bone, thick, rare, salted after, served with beans and Chianti Classico.

Lampredotto is Florence's street food; cantucci and Vin Santo end every meal.
