Vineyards between two cultures.

One region, two provinces, two languages and a wine industry that went from bulk red to Italy's best whites in a generation. The Adige valley, the pergola and the terrace, and the sparkling wine of Trento.

Trentino-Alto Adige is one region on the map and two in every other way. The southern province, Trentino, around the city of Trento, is Italian-speaking and has been Italian in feeling since the Renaissance. The northern province, Alto Adige, which its majority calls Südtirol, was Austrian until 1919 and still speaks German first: road signs, menus and wine labels come in two languages, and a third, Ladin, survives in the Dolomite valleys. What the two share is the valley of the river Adige and its side valleys, where vines grow on every slope that faces the sun, from 200 metres near Trento to over 1,000 in the mountains.

A narrow valley, a big change.

The wine country is small. Alto Adige has some 5,500 hectares of vines, Trentino about 10,000, together less than a tenth of Puglia. It is also steep: the valley floor is taken by apple orchards and the vines are pushed up the sides, onto terraces in the Val di Cembra held by hundreds of kilometres of dry-stone wall, and onto the wooden pergola that is the traditional training here, a roof of vines on posts that lifts the grapes into the air and lets the farmer work underneath. For most of the twentieth century the region made light, pale red from the Schiava grape, called Vernatsch in German, sold by the tanker to Austria and Germany as Kalterersee and Santa Maddalena. Then, from the 1980s, the cooperatives and a few estates pulled out Schiava and planted white grapes on the best slopes, and within twenty years Alto Adige had become the source of Italy's most reliable white wines.

Key facts.

About 98% of Alto Adige's wine is DOC, the highest share of any Italian region; roughly 70% is made by cooperatives, which here are among the best producers, not the worst.

Vineyards run from about 200 to 1,000 metres and beyond; the Alps shelter the valley from the north, and the climate is sunnier and drier than the latitude suggests.

Alto Adige DOC covers the whole province with sub-zones such as Santa Maddalena, Lago di Caldaro, Terlano, Valle Isarco and Val Venosta. Trentino DOC does the same in the south, with Teroldego Rotaliano DOC for the Piana Rotaliana.

Trento DOC (1993) was Italy's first appellation reserved for traditional-method sparkling wine; the producers market it as Trentodoc.

Trento and its bubbles.

Trentino's great wine is sparkling. In 1902 Giulio Ferrari, who had studied in Champagne, planted Chardonnay above Trento and made a bottle-fermented wine to match what he had drunk in France; he ran the house alone for fifty years and sold it in 1952 to a local wine merchant, Bruno Lunelli, whose family still owns it. The mountain climate gives Chardonnay and Pinot Nero the acidity that sparkling wine needs, and today some sixty houses make Trentodoc, with Ferrari as the biggest and best-known. It is the wine of Italian state occasions and of every bar in Trento. Alongside it Trentino grows Teroldego, a dark, plummy red native to the gravel plain of the Piana Rotaliana at the mouth of the Val di Non, Marzemino, the light red that Don Giovanni calls for in Mozart's opera, and Nosiola, a white that is dried on racks through the winter to make the honeyed Vino Santo of the Valle dei Laghi.

In the glass: Trentodoc Brut.

 Colour: Pale straw with a fine, persistent bead.

 Nose: Green apple, white flowers, bread crust, a mountain freshness.

 Palate: Dry, crisp, high acidity, lean and precise, long clean finish.

 Alcohol: 12–12.5%.

 Serve: 6–8 °C; non-vintage within three years of disgorgement, Riserva keeps a decade.

 At the table: Speck, canederli, lake fish, mountain cheese; the whole meal in Trento.

Two languages on the label.

In Alto Adige every wine has two names. Schiava is Vernatsch, Lago di Caldaro is Kalterersee, Santa Maddalena is St. Magdalener, Pinot Bianco is Weissburgunder, and the label may print either or both. The estates are mostly German-speaking, the cooperatives bilingual, and the wine culture is Austrian in its habits: whites served young and cold, reds light and drunk with lunch, a Buschenschank or farm tavern in every village in autumn. The south, in Trento, is Italian in the same way: aperitivo, pergola and polenta. The region's cooking follows the same line, and the reading after next is about it. Before you move on.

Trentino is Italian-speaking and Alto Adige (Südtirol) German-speaking; both grow wine on the steep sides of the Adige valley, on pergolas and terraces.

Alto Adige turned from bulk Schiava red to Italy's best whites from the 1980s; 98% is DOC and most is made by excellent cooperatives.

Trentodoc, begun by Giulio Ferrari in 1902 and Italy's first sparkling DOC (1993), is Trentino's great wine; Teroldego, Marzemino and Nosiola are its natives.
