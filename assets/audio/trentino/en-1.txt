Vineyards between two cultures.

New Vernatsch left Bolzano in rail tank wagons every December, in the same year it was picked. It travelled unbottled, the pale, light red that Italians call Schiava, and most of it went to Switzerland. There it was put into small bottles of two decilitres, labelled Kalterersee or St. Magdalener, and sold cheaply in discount shops. In 1973, the busiest year, a Swiss importer counted about 230,000 hectolitres of wine from the Tyrol crossing into Switzerland. In the middle of the 1980s Vernatsch still covered two thirds of the vineyards of South Tyrol.

Kalterersee is the German name of the Lago di Caldaro, a small lake south of Bolzano. In the north road signs and menus come in two languages, and almost every wine has two names in the same way. Schiava is Vernatsch, Santa Maddalena is St. Magdalener and Pinot Bianco is Weissburgunder, and a label may print either name or both. The region has two provinces. Trentino, around Trento in the south, speaks Italian. Alto Adige, around Bolzano in the north, is South Tyrol, or Südtirol, to its German-speaking majority. Until the First World War both provinces were part of the Austrian Tyrol, and in 1919 both went to Italy.

What the two provinces share is the valley of the Adige and the valleys that run into it. The valley floor is taken by apple orchards, so the vines grow on the slopes, from about 200 metres near Trento to over 1,000 in the mountains. The Alps to the north shelter them, and the climate is sunnier and drier than the latitude suggests. The traditional way to train a vine here is the pergola, a roof of vines on posts and wires. It lifts the grapes into the air, shades them from the summer sun and the wind, and lets the grower work standing underneath. About three quarters of Trentino's vineyards are still trained that way. In the Val di Cembra, north-east of Trento, the pergolas stand on terraces between 450 and 900 metres, held up by some 700 kilometres of dry-stone wall.

The trade in bulk Vernatsch began to fail in the early 1980s. Its Swiss customers were growing old, and then the Austrian glycol scandal of 1985 and the Italian methanol scandal of 1986 made cheap wine harder still to sell. Some of the cooperatives had already begun to change. In 1977 Hans Terzer, who was twenty-one, became cellar master of the cooperative at San Michele Appiano, which was in debt and made bulk Vernatsch. He cut the yields, and the growers called it a sacrilege. He had Vernatsch pulled out and Chardonnay and Sauvignon planted in its place, and his first wines under the name Sanct Valentin came out in 1986. Alois Lageder's Löwengang Chardonnay, first made in 1984, was among the first white wines of South Tyrol to succeed abroad. Today Vernatsch covers about 650 hectares, and three fifths of the vineyards grow white grapes. The cooperatives still make seven bottles in ten, and they are now among the best producers in the province.

Trento had turned to sparkling wine long before, because the mountain gives Chardonnay and Pinot Nero the high acidity that a sparkling wine needs. Giulio Ferrari was born in 1879 at Calceranica, south-east of Trento. He studied at the agricultural school of San Michele all'Adige, then at Montpellier and Geisenheim, and he spent time at Épernay, in Champagne. In 1902 he founded a firm to make wine sparkle by a second fermentation in the bottle, called it Fabbrica Champagne G. Ferrari, and sold Chardonnay vines from his nursery. Fifty years later Ferrari was seventy-three, had no heirs and made about 9,000 bottles a year. Of the people who wanted to buy the firm he chose Bruno Lunelli, who owned a wine shop in Trento. Lunelli went deep into debt to pay for it, and his family still owns Ferrari. In 1993 Trento DOC became Italy's first appellation reserved for wine made sparkling in the bottle, and today about seventy houses make it.

North of Trento lies the Campo Rotaliano, a plain of gravel that the river Noce laid down where it leaves the Val di Non for the Adige. Its grape is Teroldego, a dark red that tastes of plums. In 1984 Elisabetta Foradori, who was about twenty and had just finished at San Michele, made her first vintage on her family's estate there. Against the commercial clones of the time, she began in 1985 to select vines of her own from the estate's old Teroldego. Marzemino is a lighter red from the Vallagarina, south of Trento, and Mozart's Don Giovanni calls for it at his last supper. Versa il vino! Eccellente marzimino!, he tells his servant: pour the wine, excellent Marzemino. Soon afterwards the statue of the Commendatore arrives.

Trentino's own white grape is Nosiola. In the Valle dei Laghi, west of Trento, the bunches are laid on racks called arele after the harvest and left to dry through the winter. The rules do not allow them to be pressed before February, and by custom they are pressed in Holy Week, the week before Easter. By then they have lost most of their water, and no more than three tenths of their weight may become wine. The wine then often spends six to ten years in small casks before it is sold as Vino Santo, sweet and tasting of honey.
