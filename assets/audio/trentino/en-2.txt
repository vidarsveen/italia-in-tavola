Gewürztraminer, Lagrein and the aromatic north.

A village on the wine road named a grape that the whole world grows; a dark red grows only in the gravel of Bolzano; and a valley makes whites that age for twenty years. The grapes of Alto Adige and who makes them well.

Alto Adige's reputation rests on white wine, and on one white grape above all. Gewürztraminer, the "spiced Traminer", takes its name from Tramin, a village on the Weinstrasse south of Bolzano, where the Traminer family of grapes was recorded in the Middle Ages. The grape wandered to Alsace, Germany and the New World and is grown everywhere, but here, on the slopes above the village where it began, it makes a wine that is dry, powerful and unmistakable: lychee, rose petal, ginger and orange peel, an oily weight in the mouth, and low acidity that the altitude keeps in check.

The aromatic whites.

Gewürztraminer is the loudest, but the region's whites are a chorus. Pinot Bianco, Weissburgunder, is the quiet workhorse, and in the sub-zone of Terlano, on porphyry soil north of Bolzano, it makes a wine that ages for decades; the Terlano cooperative keeps bottles from the 1950s that are still alive. Pinot Grigio here is a proper wine, not the thin thing sold under that name elsewhere. Sauvignon is sharp and green and increasingly the region's calling card. In the Valle Isarco, the narrow valley that runs north-east from Bolzano towards the Brenner pass, the vineyards climb to 900 metres and grow Germanic grapes that nowhere else in Italy bothers with: Kerner, Sylvaner, Müller-Thurgau and Riesling, all bone dry, all with a mineral cut from the altitude. The Val Venosta, west towards Switzerland, is drier still and makes Riesling at 1,000 metres.

Key facts.

Gewürztraminer: named after Tramin; dry in Alto Adige, 14% alcohol is normal; lychee, rose and ginger; the best from the slopes of Tramin and Söll.

Lagrein: a native red related to Syrah, grown on the warm gravel of Bolzano's Gries district; deep purple, plum and chocolate, firm tannin. Lagrein Kretzer is its rosé.

Schiava (Vernatsch): pale, light, cherry-scented, the traditional red; Santa Maddalena is the best of it, with a little Lagrein blended in.

Pinot Nero from Mazzon above Egna is Italy's most consistent; Terlano's Pinot Bianco and Sauvignon age for twenty years.

Lagrein: Bolzano's own red.

Alto Adige's serious red is Lagrein, and it is grown almost nowhere else. It wants warmth, and it gets it in the basin of Bolzano, one of the hottest towns in Italy in summer, on the gravel washed down by the Talvera river into the district of Gries, where the Benedictine abbey of Muri-Gries has made it for a century and a half. The wine is deep purple, almost black, with plum, blackberry, chocolate and a bitter note at the end, firm tannin and a freshness that surprises in a wine so dark. The old style was rustic; the modern one, aged in barrique, is one of northern Italy's finest reds and still cheap for what it is. The rosé version, Lagrein Kretzer, is the colour of a cherry and the wine Bolzano drinks in the summer heat. The grape's genetic relatives are Syrah and Teroldego; the family, it turns out, is alpine.

In the glass: Alto Adige Gewürztraminer.

 Colour: Deep straw to pale gold.

 Nose: Lychee, rose petal, ginger, orange peel, honeysuckle; intense.

 Palate: Dry, full and oily, low acidity, spicy, a long perfumed finish with a faint bitterness.

 Alcohol: 13.5–15%.

 Serve: 10–12 °C; best from one to five years, a few keep longer.

 At the table: Speck and Graukäse, spiced dishes, foie gras, strong cheese, Asian food; too big for delicate fish.

Who to look for.

The cooperatives lead: Cantina Terlano for Pinot Bianco and Sauvignon, Cantina Tramin for Gewürztraminer (its Nussbaumer is the reference), Colterenzio, San Michele Appiano, Nals Margreid. Among the estates, Alois Lageder pioneered biodynamics and single-vineyard bottlings, Elena Walch and Hofstätter work in Tramin, and Muri-Gries is the Lagrein reference. In the Valle Isarco, Kuenhof, Köfererhof and the Abbazia di Novacella, a monastery that has made wine since 1142, set the standard. In Trentino, Elisabetta Foradori rescued Teroldego from a century of overcropping and made it a serious wine, and Ferrari, Cesarini Sforza and the cooperative Cavit carry Trentodoc. Prices are moderate everywhere; the region has never learned to charge what Piedmont does.

Before you move on.

Gewürztraminer is named after Tramin and is made dry and powerful here: lychee, rose and ginger at 14%.

Lagrein is Bolzano's dark native red from the gravel of Gries; Kretzer is its rosé; Schiava is the pale traditional red.

Terlano's Pinot Bianco and Sauvignon, the Germanic whites of the Valle Isarco, and Foradori's Teroldego are the region's other benchmarks.
