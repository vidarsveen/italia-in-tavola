Gewürztraminer, Lagrein and the aromatic north.

The Benedictine monks of Muri were given forty-eight hours to leave. In January 1841 the Swiss canton of Aargau had abolished its monasteries. On the morning they went, Father Johannes Evangelist Kuhn wrote in his diary: O eine traurige und lange Nacht, oh, a sad and long night. Soldiers were quartered in the empty abbey and drank their way through its cellars, and one report said the guards were staggering at their posts. The monks spent four years in exile at Sarnen. In 1845 the Austrian emperor, Ferdinand I, gave their abbot a vacant house of Augustinian canons at Gries, just outside Bolzano, together with its vineyards.

Lagrein was already an old vine at Gries. A white Lagrein is named in a gift to the parish church there in 1318, and a red one in the laws that the rebel leader Michael Gaismair drew up for the Tyrol in 1526. The name probably comes from the Vallagarina, the valley south of Trento. DNA has found the grape's relations close by: one of its parents is Teroldego, and Marzemino is a close relation. Through Teroldego it is also a cousin of Syrah. Lagrein needs warmth to ripen. Bolzano lies in a basin at 262 metres and is one of the hottest cities in Italy in summer, and Gries sits on the gravel that the Talvera river washed down into it.

The monks went on making wine. By 1900 they were sending St. Magdalener, Lagrein and Ruländer, the German name for Pinot Grigio, over the Brenner in bulk. In 1926 one of them, Father Rudolf Grüter, chaired a new cooperative set up to protect genuine Lagrein. The abbey began to bottle its wine in the 1960s, and in 1985 it put Lagrein first. Today more than half its bottles are Lagrein. The wine is deep purple, almost black. It smells of plum, blackberry and chocolate and has a bitter note at the end, with firm tannin and a freshness that is surprising in a wine so dark. The old style was rustic, and the modern one is often aged in barrique. Lagrein Kretzer is the rosé of the same grape, the colour of a cherry, and it is what Bolzano drinks in the summer heat.

South of Bolzano, on the wine road, lies the village of Tramin, Termeno in Italian, and Gewürztraminer, the spiced Traminer, is named after it. In the Middle Ages wine from Tramin was sold as Traminer across the German-speaking lands. In 1346 the town law of Munich listed the wines of Bolzano and Tramin and explained that each wine is named after the place where it grows. That wine, however, was probably not made from the grape now called Traminer. The consortium of Alto Adige's wines thinks it was a white wine made from White Lagrein, the grape named at Gries in 1318. The grape called Traminer is first recorded at a monastery in Stuttgart in 1483, and its pink, perfumed form was first written down as Gewürztraminer in the Rheingau in 1827. It reached South Tyrol only in 1848, when Archduke Johann planted Red Traminer at Appiano, and soon afterwards Tramin. The village gave the grape its name, and the grape came to the village from the north.

On the terraces above the village, up to about 600 metres, it makes a big wine. It smells of lychee, rose petal, ginger and orange peel. It is oily in the mouth, low in acidity and often high in alcohol. Most bottles keep a little sugar, usually five to ten grams a litre, and the consortium counts them as the only "sweet" wines of Alto Adige. In 1965 the whole province grew only fifty hectares of Gewürztraminer, and it now grows more than six hundred. The village cooperative, Cantina Tramin, was founded in 1898, and it makes its Nussbaumer from vines at Söll, above the village.

North-east of Bolzano the Valle Isarco climbs towards the Brenner pass, and its vineyards reach about 900 metres. They grow the grapes of Germany and Austria, Sylvaner and Kerner, Müller-Thurgau and Riesling, and make them bone dry, with a mineral edge from the altitude. Near Bressanone, the abbey of Novacella was given farms with vineyards when it was founded in 1142, and it still makes wine. West of Merano the Val Venosta runs towards Switzerland. It gets about 500 millimetres of rain a year, less than any other valley in South Tyrol. Its best Rieslings grow at around 700 metres.

Up the valley from Bolzano towards Merano, on soils of weathered porphyry, the cooperative of Terlano was founded in 1893. It has bottled Pinot Bianco on its own since 1927. Sebastian Stocker was its winemaker from 1955 to 1993, at a time when the cooperative sold its wine young. He believed Terlano's whites could age. On his own, in secret and without anyone's permission, he hid bottles of every vintage in the cellar, five hundred a year by the cooperative's count. He also left wine on its lees for years before bottling, and the cooperative later made his method a range of its own. Since 1979 the cooperative has kept some of its white wine in steel tanks on the fine lees for ten to thirty years before bottling it as Rarità. Its cellar now holds about a hundred thousand bottles, thirteen metres underground, and vintages from the 1950s are still alive when they are opened. The oldest bottle down there is from 1893.
