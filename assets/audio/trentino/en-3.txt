Speck, dumplings and strudel.

The one Italian kitchen that cooks with butter, smoke and rye. Speck and how it is cured, the dumplings that use up yesterday's bread, apples by the million, strudel, and the autumn ritual of new wine and chestnuts.

Cross the line from Veneto into Trentino and the food changes faster than the scenery. Olive oil gives way to butter, wheat to rye and buckwheat, tomatoes to cabbage and potatoes, and the smell in the air is wood smoke and cured pork. This is Alpine cooking, Austrian in its bones, and in Alto Adige it is unapologetically so: the menu says Knödel before it says canederli. It is the food of people who spent six months of the year in snow and had to preserve everything, and it is at its best in a farmhouse tavern or a mountain hut at 2,000 metres.

Speck.

Speck Alto Adige is the region's ham and its emblem. A boned leg of pork is rubbed with salt, pepper, juniper, bay and other spices, rested for three weeks, then lightly smoked over beech wood at a low temperature, below 20 °C, in alternating days of smoke and fresh air, and hung to cure for at least twenty-two weeks. The rule the makers quote is "little salt, little smoke, plenty of fresh mountain air". The result sits between prosciutto and Black Forest ham: darker and firmer than prosciutto, only faintly smoky, with a dark rind and a fat cap that the locals eat and visitors cut off. It has had protected status since 1996. It is sliced thin with rye bread and horseradish, cubed into dumplings, wrapped around cheese, and eaten at the Marende, the afternoon snack of speck, cheese, bread and wine that is the region's fourth meal.

Key facts.

Speck Alto Adige IGP (1996): boned pork leg, salt and spices, cold-smoked, at least 22 weeks' curing.

Canederli or Knödel: dumplings of stale bread, milk, egg and speck, spinach or cheese, boiled and served in broth or with butter and Parmigiano.

Alto Adige grows about a tenth of Europe's apples on 18,000 hectares of the valley floor; Trentino's Val di Non has the Melinda DOP. Fifteen or more varieties, Golden Delicious above all.

Cheeses: Graukäse, the sour, rindless grey cheese of the Valle Aurina; Puzzone di Moena, the washed-rind "stinker" of the Val di Fassa; Trentingrana and Vezzena in Trentino.

Canederli and the bread economy.

Nothing was thrown away in a mountain farmhouse, and the dumpling is what happened to yesterday's bread. Canederli are made from cubes of stale white bread soaked in milk, bound with egg and flour, and studded with speck, or with spinach, or with cheese; they are rolled the size of a tennis ball, boiled, and served in beef broth or dry with melted butter, sage and grated cheese. Kaspressknödel are flattened and fried. Schlutzkrapfen, half-moon ravioli filled with spinach and ricotta, are the region's pasta. Gerstsuppe, barley soup with speck and root vegetables, is on every menu; so are Gröstl, a fry-up of potato, onion and leftover beef, and polenta, which the Italian side eats with everything. Trentino adds strangolapreti, "priest-stranglers", dumplings of bread and spinach; canederli under the same name; carne salada, beef cured in salt and herbs and sliced raw or seared; and tortel di patate, a crisp potato pancake eaten with beans and cured meats.

Apples and strudel.

The floor of the Adige valley is one orchard from Merano to Trento, and Alto Adige alone grows about a tenth of all the apples in Europe, nearly a million tonnes a year, in neat rows under hail nets. The Val di Non in Trentino grows the Melinda apples with the blue sticker. The natural end of that is strudel: paper-thin dough, stretched over the back of the hands until the pattern of the tablecloth shows through, rolled around apples, cinnamon, raisins, pine nuts and breadcrumbs fried in butter, and served warm with vanilla sauce or cream. Kaiserschmarrn, a thick pancake torn to pieces in the pan and dusted with sugar, eaten with cranberry jam, is the other dessert, and Krapfen, filled doughnuts, the third. Every one of them comes from Vienna and every one of them is better here.

Törggelen.

In October and November the farms of the Valle Isarco and the wine road open their Buschenschank, the farm tavern, for Törggelen: a walk through the vineyards and chestnut woods followed by a long table of new wine, the cloudy, half-fermented Sußer, with barley soup, Schlutzkrapfen, smoked sausages with sauerkraut, and at the end roasted chestnuts and Krapfen. The name comes from the Torggl, the wine press, and the custom from the days when the wine merchants of Bolzano came out to taste the new vintage and the farmers fed them. It is the region's harvest festival, done privately, at wooden tables, and it is the best way to eat here.

Before you move on.

The region's cooking is Alpine and Austrian: butter, rye, cabbage, potatoes, smoke and cured pork.

Speck Alto Adige is salted, cold-smoked and aged 22 weeks; canederli turn stale bread into dumplings with speck, spinach or cheese.

Apples by the million tonne, strudel and Kaiserschmarrn, and the autumn Törggelen of new wine and chestnuts complete the table.
