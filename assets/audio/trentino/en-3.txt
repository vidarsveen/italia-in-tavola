Speck, dumplings and strudel.

On the wall of the chapel of Hocheppan castle, on a hill above Appiano south-west of Bolzano, a woman is working at a large pan over an open fire. A painter put her there early in the thirteenth century, in a scene of the Nativity. She has taken a round dumpling out of it and she is tasting it. For a long time she has been called the oldest picture of a Knödel in South Tyrol. Some art historians think the painter misread an older Byzantine picture, in which maids offer golden balls to the Christ Child.

A farmhouse in the mountains could not afford to waste bread, and the dumpling is what became of yesterday's loaf. For canederli, Knödel in German, cubes of stale white bread are soaked in milk and bound with egg and a little flour. They are mixed with speck, or spinach, or cheese, rolled to the size of a tennis ball and simmered. They come in beef broth, or dry with melted butter, sage and grated cheese. Kaspressknödel, made with cheese, are pressed flat and fried. Sunday's leftover beef went into Gröstl, fried with boiled potatoes and onion. The rest of the kitchen was built the same way, on food that kept through a long winter. It cooks with butter where the south of Italy uses olive oil, with rye and buckwheat beside the wheat, and with cabbage and potatoes more than tomatoes.

The pork went into Speck Alto Adige. The leg is boned and rubbed with salt, pepper and herbs such as juniper and bay. Then it is smoked, over wood that has no resin in it and at no more than 20 °C. The smoke is cold enough to flavour the meat without cooking it, and spells of smoke and of fresh air follow each other in turn. The ham then hangs for between twenty and thirty-two weeks, depending on its size, until it has lost more than a third of its weight. The consortium's rule for it is little salt, little smoke and plenty of fresh mountain air. Speck is darker and firmer than prosciutto and only faintly smoky, somewhere between prosciutto and Black Forest ham. It has a dark rind and a cap of fat that people here eat and visitors cut off. It is sliced thin with rye bread and horseradish, cut into cubes for dumplings and wrapped round cheese. And it is eaten at the Marende, a word that comes from the Latin merenda. That was the farmworkers' meal at about four in the afternoon: speck, sausage, cheese, a hard, flat rye bread called Schüttelbrot, and wine.

The dairy wasted as little as the kitchen. Cream was churned into butter, and the sour skimmed milk left over was made into Graukäse, grey cheese, which is sour and has almost no fat. It comes from the Valle Aurina, in the far north of the province.

In Alto Adige the menu says Knödel before it says canederli. In Trentino it says canederli, and polenta goes with nearly everything. Trentino's dumplings of bread and spinach are called strangolapreti, priest-stranglers. The usual story ties the name to the prelates at the Council of Trent, in the sixteenth century, who are said to have eaten them too greedily. Other stories blame husbands who wished the priests would choke, or a priest who choked at an inn, and none of them has a document behind it. In the Val di Non, grated potato is salted and fried in lard in a copper pan as tortel di patate, and eaten with beans and cured meats. Around Tenno, Arco and Riva, near Lake Garda, beef is cured with salt, pepper, garlic and herbs to make carne salada, which is sliced and eaten raw or quickly seared.

The floor of the Adige valley is one orchard from Merano to Trento, in neat rows under hail nets. Alto Adige grows about a million tonnes of apples a year on some 18,000 hectares. That is half of Italy's apples and nearly one in ten of Europe's, and Golden Delicious is the main variety. The Val di Non, in Trentino, has the only apple in Italy with a protected designation of origin, the Mela Val di Non DOP. It is sold under the name Melinda, and since 1993 the apples have carried a blue sticker.

The oldest known strudel recipes are Viennese, in a handwritten cookbook of 1696, and neither of them uses apples: one is filled with turnip and one with milk cream. The pastry probably reached Vienna from Hungary during the wars against the Turks, and spread from there. In the Tyrol it is filled with apples. The dough is stretched over the backs of the hands until the pattern of the tablecloth shows through. It is rolled round sliced apples, cinnamon, raisins, pine nuts and breadcrumbs fried in butter, then baked and served warm with vanilla sauce or cream. Kaiserschmarrn came from Austria too. It is a thick pancake torn to pieces in the pan and dusted with sugar. Vienna eats it with stewed plums, and South Tyrol with lingonberry jam and apple purée.

From early October until Advent the farms of the Valle Isarco and the wine road hang a Buschen, a bunch of twigs, over the door and open their Buschenschank. That is the season of Törggelen. The name comes from the Torggl, the wine press, and from the Latin torculum. Some say the custom began with wine merchants from Bolzano who came out to taste the new wine and were given a meal. Others say it began with trade between wine growers in the valley and cattle farmers higher up, or with a meal for the people who had helped with the harvest. No one can show which is right. People walk up through the vineyards and the chestnut woods to a long wooden table. The new wine comes cloudy and barely fermented, as Suser. With it come barley soup, smoked sausages with sauerkraut, and Schlutzkrapfen, half-moons of rye dough filled with spinach and curd cheese. Last of all come Krapfen, sweet filled doughnuts, and roasted chestnuts.
