The Dolomites: mountains of stone and light.

Déodat de Dolomieu, a French geologist on his way to Rome in 1789, crossed the Tyrol and noticed a pale rock that looked like limestone. Limestone fizzes when acid touches it. This rock hardly fizzed at all. He described it in a letter printed in the Journal de Physique in 1791, and the next year Nicolas-Théodore de Saussure named it dolomie after him. The mountains built of it took the name later. It became fixed in 1864, when two English travellers, Josiah Gilbert and G. C. Churchill, published a book called The Dolomite Mountains.

Dolomite is calcium magnesium carbonate, and the mountains are the remains of reefs in a tropical sea. Between about 240 and 220 million years ago, microbes and algae, with sponges and some corals, built platforms of rock in warm, shallow water. The collision of Africa and Europe later pushed the platforms up. Ice then cut them into the towers and walls that rise straight out of green meadows, without the foothills of the rest of the Alps. The rock is pale and grey at noon, and it takes the colour of the low sun. On a clear evening, for a few minutes, the walls turn pink and orange. The Ladin valleys call it enrosadira, and their legend of King Laurin explains it. Laurin put a curse on his rose garden so that no one would see it by day or by night, and he forgot the dusk and the dawn.

Below the walls lie the high pastures. Around 1600 Marx Sittich von Wolkenstein wrote a description of the Tyrol, and in it he gave the numbers for the Alpe di Siusi, the Seiser Alm, under the Sciliar. About 1,500 cows and 600 oxen grazed there in summer, and a hundred huts on the meadow made cheese. Around the feast of St James, in late July, about four thousand men and women came up for four or five weeks to cut the hay. Hay barns still stand across the meadow, and cattle still graze it in summer.

From 1915 to the autumn of 1917 the front line between Italy and Austria-Hungary ran along these ridges. Both armies cut paths, fixed ladders and cables to the rock, dug tunnels and built cableways to move soldiers and guns across peaks where no one had fought before. Every man up there had to be fed. From 1916 the Austrian lieutenant Leo Handl had tunnels dug by hand inside the Marmolada glacier, a city in the ice with passages named after places in Vienna, among them Kärntnerstraße and Kaffee Zentral. More than 700 men held the Marmolada line. By one account drawn from Handl's, the line needed two tonnes of food a day, besides coal, wood and explosives. Four hundred porters, most of them Bosnians, carried it up in loads of thirty or forty kilos. What reached the posts could be poor. An Austrian soldier on Monte Cristallo remembered cold meat soup that stank, half a slice of hard bread and coffee made from a substitute. Walkers now climb many of those ridges on via ferrata, iron ways.

The paths lead to rifugi, mountain huts where walkers sleep in bunks. They are supplied by goods cableway, by helicopter at the start of the season, and on people's backs. At 2,500 metres they serve the food of the valleys below, canederli, barley soup and strudel.

On 19 September 1991 Erika and Helmut Simon were walking near the Similaun, on the ridge between Alto Adige and Austria. Above the Val Senales, at 3,210 metres, they found a body coming out of the ice. The man, now called Ötzi, had died about 5,300 years ago, shot with an arrow whose head is still in his left shoulder. The ice had kept his leather clothes, his grass cape, his bow and his copper axe, and it had kept what was in his stomach. In 2018 a team of scientists published their study of it. Between half an hour and two hours before he died, he had eaten a large meal, and nearly half of it was fat. The fat and much of the meat came from ibex. There was red deer as well, whole grains of einkorn wheat, and scraps of bracken, which may have been wrapped round the food. The meat had never been heated much, and he had eaten it either fresh or dried. Charcoal found earlier in his gut would fit meat dried slowly over a fire, and the authors noted that drying meat in smoke or in the open air is a simple way to keep it. Whether he carried meat dried in smoke, no one can say.

Ötzi lies in a refrigerated room in the archaeology museum in Bolzano. In Trento, the other capital, the prince-bishops lived in the Castello del Buonconsiglio. In its Torre Aquila, around 1400, a painter known as Master Wenceslas painted the months of the year for Prince-Bishop George of Liechtenstein. March was lost in a fire. In June the cattle are milked, and butter and cheese are made. July is haymaking and August the grain harvest. In October the grapes are picked and crushed, and the wine press is painted in detail. In November the swineherds drive their pigs in through the city walls.
