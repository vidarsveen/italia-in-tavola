The Dolomites: mountains of stone and light.

Coral reefs turned to rock, named after a French mineralogist, glowing pink at sunset. What the Dolomites are made of, why they look like that, who lives in their valleys, and the man found in the ice.

The Dolomites do not look like other mountains. They rise from green meadows as vertical towers, walls and pinnacles of pale rock, grey at noon and pink or orange at sunset, without the foothills or the gradual approach of the rest of the Alps. The reason is what they are made of. Two hundred and fifty million years ago this was a tropical sea, and the peaks are its coral reefs, turned to a magnesium-rich limestone called dolomite, lifted three thousand metres into the air when Africa pushed into Europe, and carved by ice. The rock is named after the Frenchman Déodat de Dolomieu, who described it in 1791; the mountains took the name from the rock.

Pale mountains, pink at dusk.

The Ladin people called them Monti Pallidi, the pale mountains, and have a legend about it: a prince married a princess from the moon, and to stop her pining for its pale light the dwarves spun moonlight over the peaks. The scientific version is that dolomite reflects the low sun in reds and violets, a phenomenon called enrosadira in Ladin, and that it happens for a few minutes every clear evening. The highest summit is the Marmolada, 3,343 metres, with the range's only large glacier, shrinking fast; the most photographed are the Tre Cime di Lavaredo, three blades of rock above Misurina, and the Sassolungo, Sella and Odle groups above the Val Gardena. In 2009 UNESCO listed nine separate Dolomite systems as a single World Heritage Site, for their beauty and for the geology they display.

Key facts.

Dolomite is calcium magnesium carbonate, the fossil reef of a Triassic sea; named after Déodat de Dolomieu (1791). UNESCO World Heritage 2009, nine systems across Trentino, Alto Adige, Belluno and Friuli.

Marmolada 3,343 m; Tre Cime di Lavaredo 2,999 m; the Sella Ronda links four passes around the Sella massif for skiers in winter and cyclists in summer.

Ladin is a Romance language of about 30,000 speakers in the Val Gardena, Val Badia, Val di Fassa, Livinallongo and Cortina; it is an official language in the Ladin valleys of Alto Adige.

Ötzi, a man of about 3300 BC, was found in melting ice on the Similaun in 1991 and lies in the archaeological museum in Bolzano with his copper axe and his clothes.

The Ladin valleys.

Five valleys around the Sella massif speak Ladin, a language descended from the Latin of Roman soldiers and older than Italian or German in these mountains. The Val Gardena, the Val Badia and the Val di Fassa are its heart; Ortisei, the main village of the Val Gardena, has three names, Urtijëi in Ladin, St. Ulrich in German, and its people have carved wood for three hundred years, first religious figures and toys sold across Europe by pedlars, now the sculpture that fills every shop window. The Ladins run the hotels, the lifts and the mountain huts that make the Dolomites the most comfortable mountains in the world to walk in: a network of rifugi where you sleep in a bunk and eat Knödel at 2,500 metres, and the via ferrata, iron cables and ladders bolted to the rock that let a walker climb what only a mountaineer could.

The via ferrata have a darker origin. From 1915 to 1917 the front line between Italy and Austria-Hungary ran along these ridges, and both armies built ladders, tunnels and cable ways to move soldiers and guns across peaks where nobody had ever thought of fighting. Men died of cold and avalanche more than of bullets; the Marmolada glacier hid an Austrian "ice city" of barracks dug into it. The tunnels in the Lagazuoi and the trenches on the Tre Cime are open to walk through. When the war ended, the whole of South Tyrol, German-speaking as it was, went to Italy, which is why the region has two languages and a long history of arguing about it, settled by an autonomy statute in 1972 that gave the province its own money and its own schools.

The iceman and the two capitals.

In September 1991 two hikers on the Similaun, on the ridge between Alto Adige and Austria, found a body melting out of the ice. Ötzi had died there about 5,300 years ago, shot in the back with an arrow, and the ice had kept him, his leather clothes, his grass cape, his bow, and a copper axe that rewrote the history of metalworking in Europe. He lies in a refrigerated room in the archaeological museum in Bolzano, the most visited museum in the region. Bolzano itself is a handsome market town under vineyards, with arcaded streets, a Gothic cathedral, and Piazza Walther, where the Christmas market is the oldest in Italy. Trento, the other capital, is a Renaissance city of painted façades where the Council of Trent sat from 1545 to 1563 and redefined the Catholic Church; the Castello del Buonconsiglio, the prince-bishops' palace, holds the finest cycle of medieval frescoes in the Alps, and Renzo Piano's science museum, the MUSE, stands by the river.

Before you move on.

The Dolomites are fossil coral reefs of dolomite rock, named after Dolomieu, glowing pink at dusk; UNESCO listed nine systems in 2009.

Marmolada is the highest at 3,343 m; the Ladin valleys around the Sella keep their own language, the rifugi and the via ferrata built in the First World War.

Ötzi, found in the ice in 1991, lies in Bolzano; Trento's Council of 1545–63 and the Buonconsiglio castle mark the Italian-speaking capital.
