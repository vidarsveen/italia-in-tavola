The landlocked vineyard.

The green heart of Italy is the one region on the peninsula that never sees the sea. What that does to the wine, the four appellations that matter, and the white from a town built on tufa.

Every other region of mainland Italy touches salt water somewhere. Umbria does not. It sits in the middle of the peninsula, wrapped by Tuscany, the Marche and Lazio, a country of hills, oak woods, olive terraces and one large lake, drained by the young Tiber on its way to Rome. Italians call it il cuore verde d'Italia, the green heart, and the phrase is not just tourism: this is the wettest, greenest and least built-up part of central Italy. For wine that geography matters more than it sounds. Without the sea there is nothing to soften the temperature, so nights are cold and days are hot, and the wines come out darker, firmer and more tannic than their Tuscan neighbours a few hills away.

Hills, tufa and the Tiber.

Umbria has no mountains of its own to speak of until the east, where the Sibillini rise towards the Marche and the land goes suddenly bare and high. Everything west of that is hill country between 200 and 500 metres, and the vineyards sit on three quite different soils: heavy clay and limestone in the centre around Montefalco and Torgiano; volcanic tufa in the south-west around Orvieto, where the same soft rock the town is built on runs under the vines; and sandier lake soils around Lago Trasimeno, the fourth-largest lake in Italy and the only body of water big enough to moderate anything. The region grows about 12,000 hectares of vines, a modest figure, and drinks a great deal of what it makes.

Key facts.

Umbria is the only region of peninsular Italy with no coastline and no border with another country; about 12,000 hectares of vineyard.

Two DOCGs: Montefalco Sagrantino (1992) and Torgiano Rosso Riserva (1990), the latter at least 70% Sangiovese with three years of ageing, six months of it in bottle.

Orvieto DOC is the volume white, mostly Trebbiano (locally Procanico) and Grechetto, with Verdello, Drupeggio and Malvasia permitted.

Native grapes to know: Sagrantino, Grechetto and Trebbiano Spoletino, a distinct old white rescued from vines trained up field maples near Spoleto.

Orvieto: the wine that used to be sweet.

The best-known Umbrian white comes from a town standing on a plug of volcanic rock above the Paglia valley, riddled underneath with caves cut into the soft tufa. Those caves are the reason Orvieto tasted the way it did for most of its history. Grapes were laid in the cool, damp grottoes, Botrytis cinerea settled on them, and the wine that resulted was golden, honeyed and abboccato, gently sweet. That was the wine the popes drank when they took refuge here, and the one Signorelli is said to have been paid in while he frescoed the cathedral. Today less than five per cent of Orvieto is made off-dry or sweet. The rest is dry, pale and brisk, which is what the market wanted in the 1970s, and the appellation has spent the years since trying to be more interesting than that: better Grechetto, less Trebbiano, old vines, and a small revival of the noble-rot style by growers who think the caves were right all along.

In the glass: Orvieto Classico.

 Colour: Pale straw, sometimes with a faint green rim.

 Nose: White peach, almond, hawthorn blossom, a chalky, faintly smoky note from the tufa.

 Palate: Dry, medium body, brisk acidity, a bitter-almond finish that is the region's signature.

 Alcohol: 12–13%.

 Serve: 10–12 °C; drink within two or three years, though good Grechetto-led bottles keep longer.

 At the table: Freshwater fish from Trasimeno, strangozzi with truffle, chicken and herb dishes, young pecorino.

Torgiano and the man who proved a point.

South-east of Perugia, where the Chiascio meets the Tiber, a single village produced Umbria's first serious modern red. Torgiano was made a DOC in 1968 and its Riserva was raised to DOCG in 1990, and the appellation is inseparable from the Lungarotti family, who bottled estate wine here when almost everyone in Umbria was still selling in bulk, and whose Rubesco Riserva showed that Sangiovese grown inland could age for decades. The village also has one of the best wine museums in Italy, in a seventeenth-century palazzo, and a second museum devoted entirely to olive oil.

Around Lago Trasimeno the wines are lighter: Gamay del Trasimeno, which is not Gamay at all but Grenache, makes a pale, peppery red, and Colli del Trasimeno covers the rest. In the north, Colli Altotiberini follows the upper Tiber towards Città di Castello. And along the eastern edge, from Spoleto up to Trevi, sits the belt of olive groves that produces the oil the whole region cooks with: Umbria DOP, green, bitter and peppery, from Moraiolo, Frantoio and Leccino trees planted on terraces that are older than most of the vineyards.

Why the wines taste inland.

Put an Umbrian red beside a Tuscan one of the same grape and the Umbrian is usually deeper in colour, higher in tannin and slower to open. Part of that is the continental swing between day and night, which builds colour and phenolics; part is the clay; and part is simply the grapes the region kept. Where Tuscany went almost entirely over to Sangiovese, Umbria held on to a black-skinned, thick-pipped local variety that nobody else wanted, because it made a wine so tannic it was drunk sweet, at Easter, in small glasses. Before you move on.

Umbria is the only landlocked region of peninsular Italy; hot days and cold nights give darker, firmer wines than Tuscany's.

Orvieto, on volcanic tufa, was historically a sweet noble-rot wine and is now almost entirely dry, from Trebbiano and Grechetto.

The two DOCGs are Montefalco Sagrantino (1992) and Torgiano Rosso Riserva (1990); Umbria DOP olive oil is the region's other great product.
