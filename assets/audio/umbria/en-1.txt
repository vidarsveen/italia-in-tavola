The landlocked vineyard.

A traditional wine cellar in Orvieto goes down into the rock in three floors. At the top the grapes were crushed, the juice ran down clay pipes to ferment on the floor below, and the new wine was moved lower still to mature. Deep in the tufa the cellar stayed cold. As winter came on the fermentation slowed, and it often stopped before all the sugar had turned to alcohol. So for most of its history Orvieto was abboccato, gently sweet, golden and honeyed, and often faintly sparkling. In 1931 a ministry inspector, Giorgio Garavini, came to draw the boundaries of the wine zone, and he recorded that the abboccato was both the most prized style and the most common.

Some of the sweetness came from the vineyards. In autumn fog lies in the valleys around the town and an afternoon wind dries it off, and that alternation brings on the noble rot, Botrytis cinerea, which shrivels the berries and concentrates their sugar. Garavini noted it in 1931. Growers usually picked early to avoid it, and in the second half of the twentieth century Orvieto became a dry, pale, brisk white. In 1981 Claudio Barbi, at Decugnano dei Barbi, let the rot take his grapes and made a sweet wine he called Pourriture Noble, by his own account the first of its kind in Italy. The appellation now has a muffa nobile style of its own.

Orvieto is still Umbria's best-known white. Its grapes are Trebbiano, which is called Procanico here, and Grechetto. The wine was already part of a painter's wages in 1499. On 5 April that year Luca Signorelli agreed to finish a chapel in the cathedral, for 200 ducats for the vault and 600 for the walls. He was also to have lodging and, every month, two measures of wine and two of grain. Orvieto is often called the wine of the popes, and several popes lived in the town. What a pope's cellarer did write down, though, is praise for a red. Sante Lancerio, cellarer to Paul III, wrote that the pope liked the red wine of Sucano, two miles from Orvieto, especially when he was staying there.

Orvieto stands on its rock above the Paglia in the south-west corner of Umbria, the only Italian region with neither a coast nor a border with another country. The Tiber runs down through it towards Rome, between hills of about 200 to 500 metres. The Sibillini mountains close it off to the east. With no sea to hold the warmth, nights are cool even after hot days. The soils change from one district to the next. Orvieto has volcanic tuff and old marine clay, Montefalco has marl, clay and sand, and around Lago Trasimeno the vines grow on lake sediments. The average vineyard holding is little more than a hectare.

The village of Torgiano lies south-east of Perugia, on the hill where the Chiascio runs into the Tiber. When Umbrian wine was still mostly sold in bulk, Giorgio Lungarotti took his land there out of sharecropping, farmed it himself from the late 1940s and planted vineyards given over to vines alone. His first bottled wines came from the 1962 harvest. The red was made mainly from Sangiovese, and his wife, Maria Grazia, named it Rubesco, from the Latin for to blush. The first Rubesco Riserva from the Monticchio vineyard followed in 1964, and bottles of the 1975 and 1977 were still alive when they were opened in the 2020s. In 1974 the couple opened a wine museum in the village, and in 2000 a museum of the olive and its oil followed, in an old mill.

Around the lake, west of Perugia, the wines are lighter. A red grape has been grown there as Gamay del Trasimeno since the late nineteenth century, and genetic tests in the 2000s showed that it is Grenache. It probably took the name because it was trained as low bush vines, as Gamay is in Beaujolais, and it makes a pale, peppery red.

The slopes between Assisi and Spoleto are covered with olive trees for forty kilometres and more, and in 2018 they became the first Italian site on the FAO's list of globally important agricultural heritage. Umbria cooks with their oil. It is sold as Umbria DOP, the first DOP to cover a whole region, and it tastes green, bitter and peppery. Nearby, between Montefalco, Spoleto and Trevi, farmers once trained a white grape up elms and maples, so that the vine and the tree shared the field. The grape is Trebbiano Spoletino, and the Collerisana estate at Spoleto first bottled it in 1992. In 2007 the Antonelli estate took cuttings from old vines that were still climbing their trees.
