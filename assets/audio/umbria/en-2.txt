Sagrantino: Italy's most tannic red.

In the farmhouses around Montefalco the Sagrantino grapes were not pressed at the harvest. Farmers laid them on racks of reeds, the camorganne, and left them to dry until Christmas. Then they pulled the berries off their stalks by hand and pressed them in a small press, and the sweet wine aged for at least two years. Every farmer kept a few of these vines. The wine had no market. Families opened it at Christmas and again on Easter morning.

The name Sagrantino is usually taken from sacro, sacred, because the wine belonged to religious feasts. A story has Franciscan friars bringing the vine home from preaching in Asia Minor, and the rules of the appellation repeat it, but nothing confirms it, and no study has yet found the grape's parents. The written record starts in 1549, with an order for Sagrantino must that names Guglielmo, a Jewish merchant of Trevi, and his wife Stella. In 1598 a lawyer from Assisi, Bartolomeo Nuti, described how red wine was made at Foligno, with Sagrantino put into the cask to give it colour and flavour. The vines grew on the hills around Montefalco, a town between Perugia and Spoleto, and up the walls of its houses.

Pressed at the harvest and fermented dry, the same grape makes a very different wine. Sagrantino has small berries with thick skins, and nearly all its tannin comes from those skins, which give it up within five days of fermentation. In a study of 133 Italian red wines published in 2021, the Sagrantinos had more than twice as much of one measure of tannin as the next group, the Cabernet Sauvignons. Young and dry, it is hard work in the mouth. In 1925, at a wine show in Montefalco, Senator Rolandi Ricci of the Scacciadiavoli estate entered a Sagrantino Asciutto, a dry one, but dry Sagrantino did not reach the shops until the early 1970s.

By then the vine had almost gone, and fewer than ten hectares were left. Through the 1970s the local cooperative and one grower, Lodovico Mattoni, went on making it dry. Arnaldo Caprai, a textile manufacturer, bought a small farm at Montefalco in 1971, and his son Marco took it over in 1987. With Leonardo Valenti of the University of Milan, Marco began in 1989 by counting the vines that had survived, then selected clones from them and changed the way they were trained. The dry wine became a DOCG in 1992, when 66 hectares were planted. Marco Caprai's "25 Anni", from the 1993 vintage, was released in 1996 for the estate's twenty-fifth year, and it won three glasses, the top mark of the Gambero Rosso guide. There are about 390 hectares now.

The Bea family works differently. It has records in Montefalco going back to the sixteenth century, and Paolo Bea first bottled its wine in 1980. His son Giampiero farms without herbicides and ferments with the grapes' own yeasts, and he does as little to the wine as he can and ages it for a long time. Between them, the polished wines of Caprai and the traditional ones of Bea set the two styles of the appellation.

A dry Sagrantino is still dark, almost black, when it is released, and its tannin dries the whole mouth. Fat and protein soften that grip, which is why the wine goes with roast lamb, wild boar, pigeon, aged pecorino and meat cooked over wood. Ten years is not old for a good bottle, and the best keep for twenty. For everyday drinking the same hills make Montefalco Rosso, which is mostly Sangiovese with a tenth to a quarter Sagrantino, and ready to drink years earlier.

The passito is still made, from grapes dried without heat. The tannin stays in it under the sweetness, with dried figs, cherries in spirit and bitter chocolate. In Montefalco it goes with hard almond biscuits, or with sharp sheep's cheese at the end of a meal, and it is still the wine of Easter morning.
