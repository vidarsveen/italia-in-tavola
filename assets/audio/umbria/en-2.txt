Sagrantino: Italy's most tannic red.

A grape so tough it was drunk sweet for centuries, rescued by one estate in the 1970s and now Italy's most tannic red. The rules, the two styles, and how to drink something built like this.

There is a measurable sense in which Sagrantino is the most tannic wine grape in Italy, and probably one of the most tannic in the world: analyses of its polyphenol content put it above Nebbiolo, above Tannat, above anything Tuscany grows. Thick skins, small berries, a high pip-to-pulp ratio. For most of its recorded history nobody tried to make a dry table wine out of it, because the result was undrinkable young. Instead the growers of Montefalco, a small hill town between Perugia and Spoleto, dried the bunches and made a sweet passito for feast days. The dry wine that now carries the name is barely fifty years old.

Where the grape came from.

Nobody is certain. The name points at the sacristy, sacer, and the usual story has Franciscan friars bringing the vine from Asia Minor, which is the kind of story medieval wines attract and which nothing confirms. What is documented is that a black grape called Sagrantino has been grown on these particular hills for centuries and effectively nowhere else, that it was used for the sweet wine of Easter and of weddings, and that by the 1960s it had almost gone: a few dozen hectares, mostly interplanted with other vines, in a region that was losing its farmers to the cities.

The revival is usually credited to Arnaldo Caprai, whose family bought land at Montefalco in 1971, and to his son Marco, who from the late 1980s worked with the university of Milan on clonal selection, canopy management and long maceration, and who pushed the dry style at export markets. Others were working in parallel and in a very different key: Paolo Bea, a grower whose wines are made with minimal intervention and long ageing, became the reference for those who found the polished version too modern. Between them the two styles defined the appellation. It became a DOC in 1979 and a DOCG in 1992, and the planted area went from almost nothing to around 400 hectares.

Key facts.

Montefalco Sagrantino DOCG (1992): 100% Sagrantino, from a small zone in the province of Perugia; about 400 hectares planted.

Ageing: at least 37 months before release, of which at least 12 in oak. The passito also needs 37 months but has no oak requirement.

Passito: bunches are air-dried for at least two months before pressing, giving a sweet red that was the original wine of the district.

Montefalco Rosso DOC is the everyday red of the same zone: mostly Sangiovese with a small share of Sagrantino for grip, drinkable years earlier and a fraction of the price.

Drinking something built like this.

A young Sagrantino is a difficult glass: opaque purple-black, with blackberry, plum skin, tar, violets and a tannin that dries the whole mouth and keeps going after you have swallowed. Time helps, and so does food with fat and protein in it. The wine wants roast lamb, wild boar, pigeon, a slab of aged pecorino, anything cooked over wood. It does not want fish, vegetables or a sunny afternoon. Ten years is not old for a good bottle; twenty is normal for the best. Decanting an hour ahead makes a real difference.

The passito is the older tradition and worth seeking out. Dried grapes push the sugar up, the tannin stays, and the result is a sweet wine with grip, tasting of dried figs, cherry in spirit and bitter chocolate, drunk in Montefalco with hard almond biscuits or, more traditionally, with the sharp local sheep's cheese at the end of a meal. It is one of the very few sweet reds in Italy that is not simply sweet.

In the glass: Montefalco Sagrantino.

 Colour: Opaque purple-black, slow to turn garnet even with age.

 Nose: Blackberry, damson, violet, tar, bitter chocolate, dried herbs and earth.

 Palate: Full body, very high tannin, firm acidity, dense dark fruit, a long dry finish that grips.

 Alcohol: 14–15%.

 Serve: 18 °C, decanted an hour ahead; drink from eight years, the best keep twenty.

 At the table: Roast lamb, wild boar, game birds, aged pecorino, anything grilled over embers.

What Montefalco is for.

The town itself is worth the climb. It is called la ringhiera dell'Umbria, the balcony of Umbria, because from its walls you can see Perugia, Assisi, Spello, Trevi, Spoleto and most of the valley in between, and the terrace is lined with the olive groves and vineyards that pay for the view. The dry Sagrantino made here is not an easy wine and was never meant to be a fashionable one. It is the clearest example in Italy of a grape that survived only because a handful of families kept planting something the market had no use for, and then found a market for it.

Before you move on.

Sagrantino carries more tannin than any other Italian grape and grows essentially only around Montefalco.

It was a sweet passito wine for centuries; the dry version dates from the 1970s and became a DOCG in 1992, needing 37 months of ageing with 12 in oak.

Serve it with fat and protein, decant it, and give it eight years; Montefalco Rosso is the Sangiovese-led everyday wine of the same hills.
