Norcia: truffles, salumi and the norcino.

In Rome a pork butcher's shop can still carry the sign norcineria, and the man behind its counter is a norcino. Both words come from Norcia, a town in eastern Umbria at 604 metres, under the Sibillini mountains. Its men went to Rome and Tuscany every winter for the pig-killing season, from Saint Andrew's day at the end of November to the feast of Saint Anthony Abbot, the patron of pigs, in mid-January. In 1615 they founded a confraternity in Rome, and in 1677 two guilds were united inside it: the pork dealers, and the surgeons from Norcia.

The two trades had a skill in common. In the valleys around Norcia men castrated pigs and other animals, and some of them carried the same technique over to people. The surgeons of Preci, a village near the Benedictine abbey of Sant'Eutizio, cut out bladder stones, repaired hernias and treated cataracts, and they were most in demand in the sixteenth century. Preci then belonged to Norcia, so its surgeons were called norcini too. One of them, Durante Scacchi, published a book of surgery in 1596. A story in Preci has it that his brother was called to England around 1588 to operate on Queen Elizabeth's eyes, but no English record mentions it.

Cured pork needs cold, dry air, and Norcia has it. Prosciutto di Norcia IGP may only be made above 500 metres, in parts of Norcia, Preci, Cascia and two smaller villages nearby. The pigs can come from anywhere, as long as they are heavy. Each leg is salted twice, with sea salt mixed with pepper and sometimes garlic, so the ham tastes faintly spiced. The trotter is cut away and the ham is shaped like a pear, and it cures for at least twelve months. Beside the hams the shops hang mazzafegato, a liver sausage made sweet or salted, and coppa di testa, boiled meat from the pig's head seasoned with orange peel.

On 30 October 2016 an earthquake centred near Norcia brought down the nave of the basilica of San Benedetto and left only its front standing. Tradition says Saint Benedict was born in the town, and the rebuilt basilica reopened nine years later to the day. Many norcinerie could not use their shops. They sold at fairs in other towns and by post, and people all over Italy ordered from them directly to help.

In winter the woods around Norcia and Spoleto give the black truffle, Tuber melanosporum. It grows among the roots of oaks, hop hornbeam and hazel in limestone soil, and trained dogs find it. Umbrian law allows it to be dug from 1 December to 15 March. Its scent is less overwhelming than that of the white truffle of Alba, but it survives gentle heat, so Umbria cooks with it. The truffle is grated generously into warm oil with garlic and often an anchovy, and turned through pasta, or it is beaten into eggs or laid on toasted bread. A plate of pasta alla norcina is a different dish: crumbled Norcia sausage in cream, with truffle when somebody can afford it.

The hunters used to sell their truffles through middlemen. In 1951 three men of Norcia held the town's first truffle show, before Christmas, so that hunters could sell straight to buyers at a fair price. The fair has since moved to the last weekend of February and the first of March, and it is now called Nero Norcia.

Lower down, in the valleys, the pasta is flour and water with no egg, rolled out and cut into thick strands. Spoleto calls the strands strangozzi, and they look like shoelaces; Terni calls them ciriole and Perugia umbricelli. The flat bread is just as plain. A torta al testo is a round of dough cooked dry on a hot disc of stone or iron, the testo, and then split open to hold ham, cheese or greens.

A porchetta is a whole pig, boned, packed with garlic, pepper and wild fennel, and roasted. Umbria and Lazio both claim to have invented it, and the herb tells the two apart, because the rules for Porchetta di Ariccia, south of Rome, require rosemary. Around Lake Trasimeno the same seasoning goes into a carp, roasted with lard, garlic and wild fennel and called regina in porchetta, the queen in porchetta. The carp came from outside the lake: twenty-six were brought from Lake Bracciano in 1710 to make Trasimeno yield more fish.

Above Norcia the road climbs to Castelluccio, a village at 1,452 metres. Below it lies the Piano Grande, a flat plain at about 1,270 metres ringed by bare mountains. Small, thin-skinned lentils grow on it. They hold their shape and cook without soaking, and they are eaten with sausage or in soup with pasta. No weedkiller is sprayed on the fields, so wild flowers come up among the lentil plants, and from late May they open in turn. The yellow of charlock comes first, then red poppies, then white chamomile and ox-eye daisies, and the blue cornflowers last, in early July. The colours now bring more visitors than the lentils do. Almost every one of those flowers is a weed, competing with the lentils for the same field.
