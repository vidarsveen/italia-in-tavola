Norcia: truffles, salumi and the norcino.

A mountain town so good at butchering pork that its name became the Italian word for a pork butcher. Truffles, lentils grown on a high plain, hams, and the plainest pasta in Italy.

In Rome, in Florence, in half the cities of Italy, the shop that sells cured pork is called a norcineria, and the man behind the counter a norcino. Both words come from one small town in the mountains of eastern Umbria: Norcia, at 604 metres under the Sibillini, birthplace of Saint Benedict, and for centuries the place that supplied Italy with men who knew how to take a pig apart. The reputation was so complete that norcini travelled to work the winter slaughter in households all over the peninsula, and so specialised that the same families were also called on for surgery, since they owned the sharp knives and the steady hands.

Pork, salt and cold air.

What made Norcia good at this was altitude. Cold, dry mountain air is what cured pork needs, and the town had it, along with oak and beech woods where pigs could be fattened on acorns. Prosciutto di Norcia IGP is the protected ham: salted, rested, and aged at least twelve months, firmer and more savoury than the sweet hams of the Po valley, with a distinct pepperiness. Beside it the norcini make capocollo, lonza, coppa di testa, and a range of salami, some of them studded with the region's black truffle. The town's other speciality, unusually for Italy, is wild boar, which the Sibillini have in numbers.

Key facts.

Norcineria is the standard Italian word for a pork butcher's shop, taken from Norcia; Prosciutto di Norcia IGP is aged a minimum of twelve months.

The prized winter truffle is Tuber melanosporum, the black truffle, harvested from November to March; Umbria also has the summer scorzone and, around Città di Castello, some white truffle.

Lenticchia di Castelluccio di Norcia IGP grows on a high plain at about 1,400 metres; the lentils are small, thin-skinned and cooked without soaking.

Norcia was badly damaged by the earthquakes of 2016, which brought down the nave of the Basilica of San Benedetto; the rebuilt basilica reopened in October 2025.

The black truffle.

Umbria is truffle country, and the one that matters commercially here is the black winter truffle, Tuber melanosporum, which grows in symbiosis with oak and hazel on limestone soils and is hunted from November to March with dogs. It is a different proposition from the white truffle of Alba: less overwhelming in scent, but it takes heat, so it can be cooked rather than only shaved raw. The Umbrian way is generous and plain, a great deal of truffle grated into warm olive oil with a little garlic and anchovy and turned through pasta, or beaten into scrambled eggs, or laid on toasted bread. Around Norcia and Spoleto whole restaurants are built on it, and the February truffle fair fills the town square.

Lentils, and the flowering of a high plain.

An hour above Norcia the road climbs to Castelluccio, a village on a ridge above the Piano Grande, a flat upland basin at around 1,400 metres ringed by bare mountains. The plain grows the most famous lentils in Italy: tiny, thin-skinned, holding their shape, cooked straight from the bag without soaking, and eaten with sausage, with pasta, or as the compulsory New Year's Eve dish with cotechino. For a few weeks in late June and early July the fields flower all at once, and the plain turns into bands of red poppy, blue cornflower, white and yellow, which draws more visitors now than the lentils do.

The rest of the Umbrian table.

Umbrian cooking is the plainest in central Italy, which is a compliment. The pasta is flour and water rolled by hand into thick strands, called strangozzi or strozzapreti in the south of the region and umbricelli around Perugia and the lake; there is no egg in it and no filling. Meat is grilled over wood or turned on a spit: porchetta, a whole pig boned, packed with wild fennel, garlic and pepper and roasted, is an Umbrian invention as much as a Roman one, and the fennel is the tell. The lake gives carp, tench and eel; the hills give lamb, pigeon and game. Bread is famously made without salt, like Tuscany's, and the olive oil is used with a heaviness that would alarm a Ligurian.

Sweet things belong to Perugia. The city has made confectionery since the nineteenth century and industrially since 1907, when Perugina was founded; in 1922 the firm launched a hazelnut chocolate wrapped around a whole nut, which it eventually named Bacio, the kiss, and wrapped in a printed love note. Perugina belongs to Nestlé now, the factory is still in Perugia, and the city fills up every October for the Eurochocolate festival.

Before you move on.

Norcia gave Italian the word norcineria; its IGP ham is cured twelve months in cold mountain air.

The black winter truffle takes heat and is used generously, and Castelluccio's high plain grows Italy's most prized lentils.

Umbrian cooking is plain and wood-fired: strangozzi without egg, porchetta with wild fennel, unsalted bread and a great deal of olive oil.
