Assisi and the hill towns.

A basilica built in two storeys over a saint who wanted nothing, frescoed by Giotto and shaken down by an earthquake. Assisi, Orvieto, Gubbio, Spoleto and the towns on the hills between them.

Francesco di Pietro di Bernardone, a cloth merchant's son from Assisi, gave away his inheritance in the town square around 1206 and spent the rest of his life in deliberate poverty. He died in 1226 and was made a saint two years later, and the year after that the church began building him a basilica: two churches stacked one on top of the other against the hillside, decorated over the next century by the best painters in Italy. The irony is not lost on anyone, and it is the first thing every guide in Assisi says.

The basilica.

Work began in 1228 and both churches were consecrated by Pope Innocent IV in 1253. The Lower Church is dark, low and vaulted, painted by Cimabue, Simone Martini and Pietro Lorenzetti, with the saint's tomb in the crypt below it. The Upper Church is the opposite: tall, light, French Gothic, with a cycle of twenty-eight frescoes on the life of Saint Francis running round the nave at eye level, traditionally given to the young Giotto and argued over by art historians ever since. Whoever painted them, they are one of the hinges of European art: the figures have weight, the buildings have depth, and the story is told as if it happened to people.

On 26 September 1997 an earthquake struck Umbria and the Marche. Part of the Upper Church vault came down, killing two friars and two surveyors who were inside assessing the damage from an earlier shock that morning, and destroying frescoes by Cimabue and in the vault bays. The restoration, which pieced together tens of thousands of fragments, took two years, and the basilica reopened in 1999. Assisi and the Franciscan sites were added to the UNESCO World Heritage list in 2000.

Orvieto, Gubbio, Spoleto.

Orvieto is the most theatrical town in Umbria: a flat-topped rock rising sheer from the plain, with a cathedral whose façade is a wall of mosaic, carved marble and gold that looks, at midday, like a jewelled screen. It was begun in 1290 to house a relic of a eucharistic miracle at nearby Bolsena, and finished across three centuries. Inside, the chapel of San Brizio holds Luca Signorelli's Last Judgement, painted from 1499, a mass of muscular nudes, devils and resurrections that Michelangelo studied before the Sistine ceiling. Underneath the town runs a labyrinth of Etruscan and medieval caves, and the deep spiral well of San Patrizio, sunk in the 1520s with two separate staircases so mules could go down and come up without meeting.

Gubbio, in the north, is grey stone and steep, stacked up the side of Monte Ingino under a huge fourteenth-century palace. It holds the Iguvine Tables, seven bronze tablets inscribed in the ancient Umbrian language, which are the main surviving record of that people. Every 15 May the town runs the Corsa dei Ceri, in which teams carry three enormous wooden pillars, each crowned with a saint, at a run up the mountain. Spoleto, in the south, has a Romanesque cathedral, a Roman theatre, and the Ponte delle Torri, an aqueduct and footbridge striding eighty metres above a wooded gorge, which Turner painted and Goethe admired.

The middle of everything.

Umbria's position has made it a corridor and a battlefield as often as a retreat. In 217 BC Hannibal trapped a Roman army in the mist against the shore of Lake Trasimeno and destroyed it, one of the worst defeats Rome ever suffered; the villages near the site are still called Sanguineto and Ossaia, blood and bones. The Romans also cut the Cascata delle Marmore, diverting a marshy river over a cliff near Terni in the third century BC and creating what is still one of the tallest waterfalls in Europe, now switched on and off for the hydroelectric station and for visitors.

Perugia, the capital, sits high above the Tiber with Etruscan walls at its base and an escalator running up through the foundations of a papal fortress into the medieval town. It has two universities, one of them for foreigners, a jazz festival in July that takes over the whole city, and the chocolate factory from the last reading. Between all of these, the hill towns of the valley, Spello, Bevagna, Montefalco, Trevi, sit within sight of one another among the olives, which is the view the wine comes from.
