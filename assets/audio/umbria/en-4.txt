Assisi and the hill towns.

One year Christmas fell on a Friday, a day when the friars fasted, and Brother Morico asked Francis of Assisi whether they should go without meat. The friars' Rule let them eat whatever was set before them, but it had them fast on Fridays and from All Saints to Christmas. Francis told Morico it was a sin to call the day the child was born a day of fasting. “It is my wish that even the walls should eat meat on such a day,” he said, and if they could not eat it, they should at least be rubbed with it on the outside. His companions remembered more. At Christmas he wanted the rich to feed the poor, the oxen and donkeys to have extra grain and hay, and grain scattered along the roads so that the larks would have plenty to eat.

Francis was the son of a cloth merchant in Assisi. In 1206, in front of the town's bishop, he gave up his inheritance, and he lived the rest of his life in poverty. Twenty years later he lay dying at the Porziuncola, a small church below the town, and asked the friars to write to Jacopa de' Settesoli, a noblewoman in Rome. He wanted grey cloth for a habit, and some of the sweet she used to make for him when he stayed in Rome. His companions' account explains that the Romans called it mostacciolo, and that it was made of almonds with sugar or honey. Jacopa knocked at the door before the letter had been sent. She made the sweet, and he could barely taste it. He died on 3 October 1226.

Pope Gregory IX made him a saint in Assisi on 16 July 1228, and the next day he laid the foundation stone of the Basilica of San Francesco. Brother Elias directed the work. The builders raised two churches, one on top of the other, against the slope of the hill, and both were consecrated in 1253. The lower church is low and dark, painted by Cimabue, Simone Martini and Pietro Lorenzetti, and the saint's tomb lies beneath it. The upper church is a tall, light, early Gothic hall. Twenty-eight scenes of Francis's life run round its walls, and whether the young Giotto painted them has been argued for a century.

The town of Orvieto stands on a flat-topped block of tufa above the plain, and over nearly three thousand years its people cut more than twelve hundred caves, tunnels, wells and cisterns into the rock. A town on a rock had to be able to survive a siege. After the Sack of Rome in 1527 Pope Clement VII fled to Orvieto and had a well cut 54 metres down through the rock. The Pozzo di San Patrizio has two spiral ramps, so that the mules carrying water up never met those going down. For meat, families bred pigeons in caves lined with rows of small square niches, and they spread the droppings on their orchards and vines. Another cave, near the cathedral, held an olive press. In Orvieto pigeon is still roasted on a spit and basted from the pan underneath, the leccarda, with wine, sage and olives.

In 1540 Perugia went to war over salt. Pope Paul III had raised its price during a famine, and when the increase was announced in the city that February, Perugia rebelled. Papal troops under the pope's son arrived in April, and on 3 June the city surrendered and lost its self-government. The pope had the houses of the Baglioni, the family that had dominated the city, pulled down, and a fortress built over them, the Rocca Paolina. Perugians like to say they have baked their bread without salt ever since. No source supports it, and Florentines ate unsalted bread long before; around 1316 the exiled Dante wrote of how other people's bread tastes of salt.

The city's chocolate is younger. Perugina was founded in 1907, and Luisa Spagnoli, the wife of one of its founders, ran its production. In 1922 she took broken hazelnuts left over from other sweets and made a chocolate of them, with gianduja and a whole hazelnut, covered in dark chocolate. The lump looked like a clenched fist, and it was sold as the Cazzotto, the punch, until Giovanni Buitoni, the firm's young managing director, renamed it Bacio, the kiss. The notes inside the wrappers came in the 1930s, from the art director Federico Seneca.

In the north-east, Gubbio keeps seven bronze tablets, the Iguvine Tables, written between the third and the first century BC in the Umbrian language. They are the rules of a college of priests, the Atiedian Brothers, and they set out what the gods were to be given. Oxen, sows and lambs are sacrificed, cakes called ficla and strucla are offered and wine is poured, and the brothers' shares of the meat are divided on bread. Every 15 May the town runs the Corsa dei Ceri, and three teams carry wooden pillars of close to three hundred kilos, each crowned with a saint, at a run up Monte Ingino. On the same day the runners eat salt cod and lamb's offal under the arcades.

Gubbio also has a story of Francis and hunger. In the Fioretti, written down more than a century after his death, a wolf is killing animals and people outside the town. Francis makes peace with it on one condition: the people of Gubbio will feed it for the rest of its life, so that hunger never drives it to kill again. It is a legend. In it the wolf lives two more years in Gubbio, going from door to door, and the townspeople feed it.
