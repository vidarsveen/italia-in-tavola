Europe's highest vineyards.

From Courmayeur, at the head of the valley, a cable car climbs to Punta Helbronner at more than 3,400 metres. Look down from the top and the whole upper valley is below you. There is the glacier, then the pine woods and the villages, and then a narrow green shelf of vines a couple of thousand metres under the ice. Those vines belong to Morgex and La Salle. Valle d'Aosta is the smallest wine region in Italy, and a single large estate in Puglia can make more wine than the whole valley. What it has instead is height. The valley climbs from the Po plain to the two highest mountains in western Europe, and the vines climb with it, onto terraces that in places sit above a thousand metres.

In the second half of the nineteenth century an aphid from North America, phylloxera, ate the roots of almost every vine in Europe. The cure was to graft European vines onto American rootstock, and that is how virtually all the world's wine is grown today. The vineyards of Morgex and La Salle were never touched. The louse cannot complete its life cycle in ground as cold as this, so the vines here still stand on their own roots, a piede franco, as every vine did before 1860. Some of them are very old. The grape they carry, Prié Blanc, is grown nowhere else in any quantity.

The way the vines are trained is the other oddity. Elsewhere in the Alps the pergola is tall, and in Bordeaux the vines grow along neat wires. Here the growers build a pergola bassa, a low, flat canopy on posts of stone or wood, not much more than a metre above the soil. After sunset the stones give back the warmth of the day, and a vine held that low sits in it, below the worst of the wind. It also means the whole vineyard is worked bent double. None of it is efficient, and none of it can be done by machine.

The growing season is short and the nights are cold, so the grapes gather sugar slowly and keep their acidity. The ultraviolet light is fierce, which thickens the skins, and the air is dry, which keeps rot away. What comes out at Morgex is a very pale white, low in alcohol, that smells of mountain herbs and green apple. It finishes salty and almost stony, and its acidity is high from the first sip to the last. It is a light wine with a lot of tension, and that makes it the natural partner of the fatty, salty food the same valley eats.

A base wine that is high in acid and low in alcohol is what good sparkling wine is made from, so Morgex makes one. The local cooperative, Cave Mont Blanc de Morgex et La Salle, gives its wine a second fermentation in the bottle by the traditional method. One of its cuvées is aged in a cellar high on the mountain. There is also an ice wine, made from grapes left on the vine until they freeze. At this height that is a reasonable thing to attempt. Almost anywhere else in Italy it would be absurd.

Wine grows here on sufferance. Seen again from the cable car, the vineyard is a thin green line far below the ice. In a cold year its grapes do not ripen. In a warm one they make a wine that no warmer region can copy.
