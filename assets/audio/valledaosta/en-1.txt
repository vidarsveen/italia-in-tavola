Europe's highest vineyards.

At Morgex and La Salle the vines grow at 900 to 1,300 metres on their own roots, because the louse that destroyed Europe's vineyards could not survive the cold. What Prié Blanc is, and why the wine tastes of altitude.

Valle d'Aosta is the smallest region in Italy and makes the least wine of any of them. The whole valley has around four hundred hectares of vines, and only a fraction of that is bottled under the region's own appellation; a single large estate in Puglia can out-produce the lot. What it has instead is altitude. The valley climbs from the Po plain to the two highest mountains in western Europe, and the vines climb with it, onto terraces that in places sit above a thousand metres. At the top of the valley, at Morgex and La Salle under Mont Blanc, is a vineyard that is unlike anywhere else in Italy.

Above the louse.

In the second half of the nineteenth century phylloxera, an aphid from North America, ate the roots of almost every vine in Europe. The cure was to graft European vines onto American rootstock, which is how virtually all the world's wine is now grown. The vineyards of Morgex and La Salle were never touched. The louse cannot complete its life cycle in ground that cold, so the vines here still stand on their own roots, a piede franco, as every vine did before 1860. Some of them are very old, and the grape they carry, Prié Blanc, grows nowhere else in any quantity.

The training system is the other oddity. Instead of the tall pergola of the Alps or the neat wires of Bordeaux, the growers here use a pergola bassa, a low horizontal canopy on stone or wooden posts, held not much more than a metre above the soil. Down there the vine sits in the warmth radiating back off the stones after sunset, and it is below the worst of the wind. It also means the whole vineyard is worked bent double. Nothing about this is efficient, and none of it can be mechanised.

Key facts.

Valle d'Aosta DOC (February 1971) covers the entire region and contains seven named sub-denominations, an unusual arrangement: one appellation for a whole region, subdivided by village.

Blanc de Morgex et de La Salle: 100% Prié Blanc, from vineyards at roughly 900 to 1,300 metres, on ungrafted vines. Made dry and still, as a traditional-method sparkling wine, and as an ice wine.

The region has around 400 hectares of vines in total, and bottles only a small share of its production under the DOC.

These are routinely described as the highest vineyards in Europe. Others make the same claim, so treat it as a strong contender rather than a settled record.

What altitude does to a wine.

Height changes wine in ways you can taste. The growing season is short and the nights are cold, so sugar accumulates slowly while acidity stays high; the ultraviolet light is fierce, which thickens skins; and the air is dry, which keeps rot away. The result at Morgex is a white of about 11 degrees of alcohol, very pale, smelling of mountain herbs, white flowers and green apple, with a saline, almost stony finish and acidity like a wire running down the middle. It is not a wine of weight. It is a wine of tension, and it is the natural partner of the fatty, salty food the same valley eats.

Because the base wine is naturally high in acid and low in alcohol, it makes very good sparkling wine. The local cooperative, Cave Mont Blanc de Morgex et La Salle, ferments a traditional-method version a second time in the bottle, and one of its cuvées is aged in a cellar high on the mountain. There is also an ice wine, made from grapes left to freeze on the vine, which is a reasonable thing to attempt at this height and an absurd one almost anywhere else in Italy.

In the glass: Blanc de Morgex et de La Salle.

 Colour: Very pale straw, almost water-white, with a green glint.

 Nose: Green apple, white flowers, alpine herbs, crushed stone.

 Palate: Light, dry, high acidity, low alcohol, a saline and slightly bitter finish.

 Alcohol: 10.5–12%.

 Serve: 8–10 °C; drink young, within two or three years, unless it is the sparkling version.

 At the table: Fontina and other mountain cheese, lard on rye bread, river trout, fonduta.

A vineyard you can see from a cable car.

The scale of the place is easier to grasp from above. From Courmayeur, at the head of the valley, a cable car climbs to Punta Helbronner at over 3,400 metres, and from it the whole upper valley is laid out: the glacier, the pine, the villages, and then a narrow green shelf of vines a couple of thousand metres below the ice. Wine grows here on sufferance. In a cold year the grapes do not ripen, and in a warm one they make something that no warmer region can copy.

Before you move on.

Valle d'Aosta is Italy's smallest wine region, with about 400 hectares, under one DOC created in 1971 and divided into seven sub-denominations.

At Morgex and La Salle, Prié Blanc grows at 900–1,300 metres on ungrafted vines, because phylloxera cannot survive the cold; the low pergola keeps the fruit near the warm stone.

The wine is pale, light, high in acid and saline, and the same base makes a fine traditional-method sparkling wine.
