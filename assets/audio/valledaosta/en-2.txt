Alpine whites and mountain reds.

A wine list in Aosta can run from Blanc de Morgex to Vien de Nus, with Fumin, Petite Arvine and Torrette in between, and nobody at the table finds it odd. The names sound French because the region is officially bilingual, French and Italian, and a Franco-Provençal patois is still spoken. Most of the red grapes behind those names are almost unknown outside the valley. They all grow under a single appellation, Valle d'Aosta DOC, which divides into seven named sub-denominations, most of them a village or two across. Drive down the valley from Mont Blanc towards the Po and the wine changes every twenty minutes. The high ground gives white. The warm central basin around Aosta gives red, and lower down, where the air is milder and Piedmont is close, the grape is Nebbiolo.

Below Morgex the road reaches Arvier, where a steep amphitheatre of terraces faces the sun and holds its heat. It is called Enfer d'Arvier, the hell of Arvier, and its wine must be at least 85 per cent Petit Rouge, the workhorse grape of the central valley. Petit Rouge gives a medium-bodied red with red fruit, violets and a faintly herbal, peppery edge, and in that heat it comes out deeper and riper. Further down, around Aymavilles and Aosta, the same grape makes Torrette, the label you will see most often. Torrette needs only 70 per cent Petit Rouge, and the rest may come from the valley's other native grapes.

One of those other grapes, Fumin, is dark, tannic and slow to open. For a long time it was blended away in small quantities, and now it is bottled on its own and aged in wood. Cornalin, which the Swiss call Humagne Rouge, is deep and spicy. None of the valley's native reds makes a big wine. They are firm, dry and cool, and their acidity keeps them fresh.

East of Aosta two villages have their own names on the label. Nus has a red grape that belongs to the village, Vien de Nus, and a white that is stranger. Its name, Malvoisie, sounds like Malvasia, but the wine is made entirely from Pinot Grigio, and it comes out golden and sometimes sweet. At Chambave, a few kilometres further on, Moscato Bianco ripens on the sunny side of the valley, and there it is made dry, which Moscato almost never is.

Alongside Prié Blanc the valley grows Petite Arvine, a grape of the Swiss Valais just over the mountains. Here it gives a white that smells of grapefruit and rhubarb and finishes salty, and it has become the region's most admired white. Chardonnay, Müller-Thurgau and more Pinot Grigio are grown as well, all made in a lean alpine style.

Near the foot of the valley the air is milder and the Piedmontese border is close, and the red grape changes. Arnad-Montjovet is Nebbiolo, and so, just below it, is Donnas, where the grape is known as Picotendro. It is lighter and more nervous than the Nebbiolo of the Langhe.

Almost everything here is small. Plantings are measured in hectares rather than hundreds of hectares, and the cooperatives matter as much as they do in Alto Adige. Cave Mont Blanc makes the high whites at Morgex, and there are cooperatives at Chambave and at Donnas. The regional agricultural college in Aosta, the Institut Agricole Régional, trains the growers and also makes very good wine of its own. Prices are higher than the wines' fame would suggest, and very little leaves Italy. If you find a bottle outside the valley, it is usually because somebody went looking for it.
