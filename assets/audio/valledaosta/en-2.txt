Alpine whites and mountain reds.

Below the ice the valley turns into a ladder of tiny appellations, each a village or two wide. Petit Rouge, Fumin, Cornalin, Petite Arvine and a Nebbiolo the locals call Picotendro, and the seven names on the labels.

Drive down the valley from Mont Blanc towards the Po and the wine changes every twenty minutes. The high ground gives white; the warm central basin around Aosta gives red from grapes almost nobody outside the valley has heard of; and the lower valley, where the air is milder and the Piedmontese border is close, gives Nebbiolo. All of it sits inside a single appellation, Valle d'Aosta DOC, which then splits into seven named sub-denominations, most of them a village or two across. The bottles are small in number and are mostly drunk within the region.

The native reds.

The valley's own red grapes are its real interest. Petit Rouge is the workhorse and the backbone of the central valley: it gives a medium-bodied red with red fruit, violets and a faintly herbal, peppery edge. Fumin is the serious one, dark, tannic and slow to open, which for a long time was blended away in small quantities and is now bottled on its own and aged in wood. Cornalin, known in Switzerland as Humagne Rouge, is deep and spicy. Vien de Nus belongs to the village of Nus. Between them they make wines that taste like the mountains they come from: not big, but firm, dry and cool, with acidity that keeps them awake.

The most common label is Torrette, made around Aosta and Aymavilles from at least 70% Petit Rouge, with the valley's other natives permitted alongside it. Enfer d'Arvier, "the hell of Arvier", is a steep amphitheatre of terraces that traps the sun, and it makes a deeper, riper Petit Rouge from at least 85% of the grape. At the bottom of the valley, Donnas is Nebbiolo, at least 85% of it, known locally as Picotendro, aged for a minimum of two years with ten months in wood. It is a lighter, more nervous Nebbiolo than Piedmont's, and it is the wine that tells you the Langhe are not far away. Arnad-Montjovet, just above it, is Nebbiolo again, with a shorter ageing requirement.

Key facts.

The seven sub-denominations: Blanc de Morgex et de La Salle, Enfer d'Arvier, Torrette, Nus, Chambave, Arnad-Montjovet and Donnas.

Torrette and Chambave rosso: at least 70% Petit Rouge, five months' minimum ageing. Enfer d'Arvier: at least 85% Petit Rouge.

Donnas: at least 85% Nebbiolo, minimum 24 months' ageing of which 10 in wood. Arnad-Montjovet: at least 70% Nebbiolo, five months.

Two curiosities: Nus Malvoisie is not Malvasia at all but 100% Pinot Grigio, made golden and sometimes sweet; Chambave Moscato is 100% Moscato Bianco made dry, which Moscato almost never is.

The whites, and the ones with French names.

Alongside Prié Blanc the valley grows Petite Arvine, a grape of the Swiss Valais just over the mountains, which here gives a white with grapefruit, rhubarb and a salty finish, and which has become the region's most admired white. Müller-Thurgau, Chardonnay and Pinot Gris fill out the vineyards, all of them made in a lean alpine style. The names on the labels are a reminder of where you are: the region is officially bilingual, French and Italian, and a Franco-Provençal patois is still spoken, so a wine list can run from Blanc de Morgex to Vien de Nus without anyone finding it odd.

In the glass: Torrette.

 Colour: Medium ruby, not deep, garnet at the edge with a few years.

 Nose: Red cherry, raspberry, violet, dried alpine herbs, white pepper.

 Palate: Medium body, fresh acidity, fine dry tannin, a bitter-herb finish.

 Alcohol: 12–13.5%.

 Serve: 15–16 °C, cooler than you would think; drink from two to six years.

 At the table: Carbonada, polenta concia, mocetta, roast kid, mountain cheese.

Who makes it.

Almost everything is small. The cooperatives matter here as they do in Alto Adige: Cave Mont Blanc at Morgex for the high whites, La Crotta di Vegneron at Chambave, the Caves Coopératives de Donnas for Nebbiolo. Among the private estates, Les Crêtes and Grosjean are the names most often seen abroad, and the Institut Agricole Régional, the regional agricultural college in Aosta, both trains the growers and makes very good wine of its own. Plantings are measured in hectares rather than hundreds of hectares, prices are higher than the wines' fame would suggest, and very little leaves Italy. If you see a bottle outside the valley, it is usually because somebody went looking for it.

Before you move on.

One DOC covers the whole region and splits into seven sub-denominations, each a village or two wide.

Petit Rouge is the backbone of the central valley (Torrette, Enfer d'Arvier); Fumin and Cornalin are the serious natives; Donnas is a light, nervous Nebbiolo called Picotendro.

Petite Arvine is the rising white; Nus Malvoisie is really Pinot Grigio and Chambave Moscato is made dry.
