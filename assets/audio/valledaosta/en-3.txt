Fontina and the table of the Alps.

A cheese made twice a day from one breed of cow, and a kitchen of butter, rye and cured fat built for a place with a six-month winter. Fonduta, carbonada, polenta concia and the cup passed round the table.

The cooking of Valle d'Aosta is the cooking of a place that used to be cut off for half the year. There is almost no olive oil, very little fish, and nothing that could be called light. What there is instead is butter, rye bread, potatoes, chestnuts, cured pork fat, game, and above all cheese: the valley's kitchen is built around Fontina in the way that Emilia's is built around Parmigiano. It is a small cuisine, French and Italian at once, and it makes complete sense the moment the temperature drops.

Fontina.

Fontina has been made in this valley since at least the twelfth century, and a fresco of about 1480 in the castle of Issogne shows a cheese that looks very much like it. The modern rules, protected since 1996, are strict about three things. The milk must come from Valdostana cows, the local red-and-black-pied breed, grazing in the valley. It must be whole and raw, never pasteurised, from a single milking, and it must reach the vat within about two hours, which is why cheese is made twice a day, morning and evening. And the wheels must ripen for at least three months in cold, damp cellars, where they are turned, brushed and salted by hand until the rind goes from tan to orange-brown.

The result is semi-soft and elastic, mild and nutty when young, more savoury and complex with age, and it melts better than almost any other cheese, which is the point of half the dishes below. The summer cheese, made up at the alpage while the herds are on high pasture, tastes of the flowers the cows have been eating and is worth seeking out. Fontina is also the base of fonduta alla valdostana, in which the cheese is soaked in milk, then whisked with egg yolks and butter into a warm cream, poured over polenta or toast and, in the grand version, finished with white truffle from just over the border in Piedmont. It is not Swiss fondue; there is no wine or kirsch in it, and you eat it with a spoon.

Key facts.

Fontina DOP (protected 1996): raw whole milk from Valdostana cows, one milking, into the vat within about two hours, minimum three months' ripening. Around 45% fat in dry matter.

The consortium's stamp shows the Matterhorn with the word FONTINA; without it, the cheese sold as "fontina" elsewhere is an imitation, often pasteurised and made far away.

Lard d'Arnad is a DOP: pork back fat layered with salt, herbs and spices in wooden containers and matured for months, eaten in translucent slices on dark bread.

Jambon de Bosses, a DOP raw ham from Saint-Rhémy-en-Bosses on the road to the Great St Bernard pass, is cured with mountain herbs.

The winter dishes.

Carbonada is the valley's stew: beef, traditionally salt-cured, cooked very slowly with onions, red wine and spices until it is almost black, and served over polenta. Polenta concia is polenta cooked and then beaten with butter and cubes of Fontina until it is rich enough to stand a spoon in. Seupa à la Vapelenentse, from the village of Valpelline, is a baked soup of stale rye bread, cabbage and Fontina soaked in broth and browned in the oven, which is what a peasant kitchen does with three things it always had. Mocetta is salted and air-dried meat, once chamois or ibex, now usually beef or goat, sliced thin with honey and rye bread. Chestnuts, potatoes and game fill out the rest.

Bread here is rye, baked hard and once made only a few times a year in a communal village oven, then stored on wooden racks and softened in soup or broth when needed. Several villages still have the old oven and fire it up for a festival. Along with the cheese and the cured fat, it explains the region's classic merenda, the afternoon plate of rye bread, lard, mocetta and Fontina with a glass of red, which is the same idea as the South Tyrolean Marende a couple of valleys east.

The cup that goes round.

Meals end with the coppa dell'amicizia, the friendship cup: a low, round wooden bowl with a lid and several spouts, filled with hot coffee, grappa, sugar and lemon peel, set alight briefly and then passed from hand to hand around the table, each person drinking from the spout nearest them. Tradition says it must not be put down until it is empty. The related grolla, a taller carved wooden goblet, is the same idea with more ceremony. Alongside it comes génépy, a pale green liqueur made by steeping the alpine wormwood of the same name, bitter and herbal and reckoned locally to be good for altitude, digestion and most other complaints.

Before you move on.

Fontina DOP is raw whole milk from Valdostana cows, one milking, three months minimum; the Matterhorn stamp is what makes it the real thing.

Fonduta is Fontina with milk, egg yolk and butter, not Swiss fondue; carbonada, polenta concia and seupa à la Vapelenentse are the winter dishes.

Lard d'Arnad, Jambon de Bosses, mocetta and rye bread make up the merenda; the meal ends with the coppa dell'amicizia and a glass of génépy.
