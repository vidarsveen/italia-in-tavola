Fontina and the table of the Alps.

Twice a day, in the morning and again in the evening, the Valdostana cows are milked and the milk goes straight into the cheese vat. It has to. The rules for Fontina say the milk must be whole and raw, from a single milking, and in the vat within about two hours, so a dairy that makes it makes cheese twice a day. The valley's kitchen is built around this cheese in the way Emilia's is built around Parmigiano, and it is the kitchen of a place that used to be cut off for half the year. There is almost no olive oil and very little fish, and nothing that could be called light. There is butter, rye bread and cured pork fat, and above all there is cheese.

Fontina has been made in this valley since at least the twelfth century. In the castle of Issogne, a fresco painted around 1500 shows a cheese seller's shop, and the wheels in it are thought to be the oldest picture of Fontina there is. The milk must come from Valdostana cows, the local red-and-black pied breed, grazing in the valley. The wheels ripen for at least three months in cold, damp cellars. There they are turned, brushed and salted by hand, until the rind goes from tan to orange-brown. The summer cheese is made up at the alpage, the high pasture, while the herds are there, and it tastes of the flowers the cows have been eating.

What comes out is semi-soft and elastic. It is mild and nutty when young, and more savoury and complex with age. It also melts better than almost any other cheese, and the winter dishes of the valley depend on that.

One of them is fonduta alla valdostana. The cheese is soaked in milk, then whisked with egg yolks and butter until it turns into a warm cream. It is poured over polenta or toast, and in the grand version it is finished with white truffle from just over the border in Piedmont. It is not Swiss fondue. There is no wine or kirsch in it, and you eat it with a spoon.

The bread here is rye, and it used to be baked only a few times a year, in an oven the whole village shared. The loaves were baked hard and stored on wooden racks, and when bread was needed a piece was softened in soup or broth. Several villages still have the old oven and fire it up for a festival. In Valpelline the hard bread goes into seupa à la Vapelenentse, a baked soup of stale rye bread, cabbage and Fontina, soaked in broth and browned in the oven. It is what a peasant kitchen does with three things it always had.

Beef goes into carbonada, the valley's stew. The meat was traditionally salt-cured, and it is cooked very slowly with onions, red wine and spices until it is almost black, then served over polenta. The polenta has a dish of its own, polenta concia, cooked and then beaten with butter and cubes of Fontina until it is rich enough to stand a spoon in. Mocetta is meat salted and air-dried, then sliced thin and eaten with honey and rye bread. It was once made from chamois or ibex, and now it is usually beef or goat. Chestnuts, potatoes and game fill out the rest.

Put rye bread, some mocetta, a piece of Fontina and a few slices of Lard d'Arnad, the cured pork fat, on one board. Pour a glass of red, and that is the merenda, the afternoon plate. A couple of valleys to the east, South Tyrol has the same idea and calls it Marende.

Meals end with the coppa dell'amicizia, the friendship cup, and a glass of génépy comes alongside it. Génépy is a pale green liqueur made by steeping the alpine wormwood of the same name. It is bitter and herbal, and people here reckon it good for the altitude, the digestion and most other complaints. The cup is a low, round wooden bowl with a lid and several spouts. It is filled with hot coffee, grappa, sugar and lemon peel, and briefly set alight. Then it passes from hand to hand around the table, and each person drinks from the spout nearest them. The grolla, a taller carved wooden goblet, is the same idea with more ceremony. The friendship cup, by tradition, must not be put down until it is empty.
