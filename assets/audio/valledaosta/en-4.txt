Castles of the Aosta valley.

A corridor between Italy and France with a castle at every bend, a Roman town at its centre, a fort that held up Napoleon, and the park that saved the ibex. What to look at in the Aosta valley and why it is all there.

The Aosta valley is a corridor. For two thousand years everything crossing between the Italian plain and France or Switzerland has funnelled through it, over the Great and Little St Bernard passes, and whoever held the valley taxed whatever went past. That is why a region with fewer people than a mid-sized town has something like a hundred castles, towers and fortified houses, most of them within sight of the main road, and a Roman city at its centre.

Fénis and the Challant castles.

The best known is Fénis, and it is the one that looks like a child's drawing of a castle: a pentagonal keep inside a double ring of curtain walls, studded with round and square towers joined by a covered walkway. It belonged to the Challant family, viscounts of Aosta, who appear in the records here from 1242, and it took its present shape between about 1320 and 1420 under Aymon of Challant and his son Boniface. It was never a serious military strongpoint. It sits on a low knoll with higher ground behind it, and it was built as an administrative seat and a display of standing. Inside, the courtyard carries a fifteenth-century Saint George killing the dragon and a row of sages and prophets holding scrolls of proverbs in old French, painted by the Piedmontese artist Giacomo Jaquerio and his circle. By the nineteenth century it was a farm building; the architect Alfredo d'Andrade bought it in 1895 and began the restoration that a second campaign finished in 1935.

Other Challant castles line the same road: Issogne, a Renaissance courtyard house with a wrought-iron pomegranate tree in its yard and frescoes of fifteenth-century shops; Verrès, a single severe cube on a crag; Sarre, bought by King Victor Emmanuel II as a hunting lodge and decorated with the antlers of several thousand ibex and chamois, which tells you something about how the ibex nearly ended.

The fort that annoyed Napoleon.

Where the valley narrows to a gorge stands Forte di Bard. In May 1800 Napoleon brought the Army of the Reserve over the Great St Bernard pass to surprise the Austrians in the Po valley, and found the road blocked by this fort, held by a garrison of a few hundred Austro-Piedmontese. It held out for about two weeks against a force of tens of thousands. The French eventually smuggled guns past at night with the wheels muffled in straw and the road covered in dung, but the delay wrecked the timetable of the campaign, and Napoleon, unamused, called it the vilain castel de Bard and had it demolished. What stands today was rebuilt from 1830 to 1838 by Charles Albert of Savoy, who expected the French to come again. It reopened in 2006 as the Museum of the Alps, and in 2015 it played the fictional country of Sokovia in a Marvel film, which has brought it a different sort of visitor.

Key facts.

Aosta was founded in 25 BC as Augusta Praetoria Salassorum, settled with 3,000 veterans on the site of the defeated Salassi. Its walls ran 724 by 572 metres and its theatre held about 4,000.

Fénis: Challant family, documented 1242, built up 1320–1420, restored from 1895 by Alfredo d'Andrade and again in 1935.

Forte di Bard: besieged May 1800; razed on Napoleon's orders; rebuilt 1830–1838; Museum of the Alps since 2006.

Gran Paradiso became Italy's first national park in 1922, out of a royal hunting reserve of 1856. It covers about 703 km² across Valle d'Aosta and Piemonte, and Gran Paradiso itself, 4,061 m, is the only peak over 4,000 metres entirely inside Italy.

Rome in the mountains.

Aosta is a Roman town that never stopped being used. The grid of streets laid out in 25 BC is still the street plan; the Arch of Augustus stands at the eastern entrance, with a crucifix hung under its vault since 1449 as a votive offering against the flooding of the torrent beside it; and traffic still passes through the Porta Praetoria, a double gate with a courtyard between its two arches where the guard once checked who was coming in. Behind it the façade of the Roman theatre rises twenty-two metres, and a long stretch of the town wall survives. The town is bilingual: French and Italian are both official, street signs come in both, and the older generation still speaks Valdôtain, a Franco-Provençal patois that is neither.

The ice and the ibex.

The valley is closed at its head and its northern side by the highest ground in the Alps: Mont Blanc at 4,808 metres, and the Cervino, the Matterhorn, at 4,478, whose Italian face rises above Breuil-Cervinia. To the south is Gran Paradiso. In 1856 Victor Emmanuel II turned that massif into a royal hunting reserve, largely to stop other people shooting the last Alpine ibex in the Alps, of which perhaps sixty were left. Protected, and later handed to the state as Italy's first national park in 1922, the herd recovered to a few thousand, collapsed again to about four hundred during the Second World War, and has since climbed back. Every ibex now living in the Alps descends from the animals that survived here. The paths the royal gamekeepers cut are the walking trails today, and the ibex are used enough to people that they will stand on a rock and look at you.

Before you move on.

The valley is a pass corridor, which is why it has around a hundred castles; Fénis, built by the Challant from 1320, is the emblem.

Forte di Bard held up Napoleon for two weeks in 1800 and was rebuilt in the 1830s; Aosta keeps its Roman grid, arch and gate from 25 BC.

Gran Paradiso, Italy's first national park (1922), saved the Alpine ibex from extinction, and every ibex in the Alps descends from its herd.
