Castles of the Aosta valley.

In May 1800 Napoleon brought the Army of the Reserve over the Great St Bernard pass, meaning to surprise the Austrians in the Po valley. Near the foot of the Aosta valley he found the road blocked. Where the valley narrows to a gorge, a fort stood on a rock. A garrison of a few hundred Austrian and Piedmontese soldiers held it for about two weeks against a force of tens of thousands. In the end the French smuggled their guns past at night, with the wheels muffled in straw and the road covered in dung. But the delay had wrecked the timetable of the campaign. Napoleon called the place the vilain castel de Bard, and he had it demolished.

The fort stood there because the valley is a corridor. For two thousand years much of the traffic between the Italian plain and France or Switzerland has come through it, over the Great and Little St Bernard passes. Whoever held the valley taxed what went past. So a region with fewer people than a mid-sized town has something like a hundred castles, towers and fortified houses, most of them within sight of the main road. The fort at Bard that stands today is not the one Napoleon saw. Charles Albert of Savoy rebuilt it between 1830 and 1838, because he expected the French to come again. It reopened in 2006 as the Museum of the Alps, and in 2015 it played the fictional country of Sokovia in a Marvel film, which has brought it a different sort of visitor.

Before the castles there was Rome. In 25 BC the Romans defeated the Salassi, who held the valley, and founded Aosta on their land, at the centre of the valley. The town has never stopped being used. The grid of streets they laid out is still the street plan. The Arch of Augustus stands at the eastern entrance, and a crucifix has hung under its vault since 1449, as a votive offering against the flooding of the torrent beside it. Traffic still passes through the Porta Praetoria, a double gate with a courtyard between its two arches, where the guard once checked who was coming in. Behind it the façade of the Roman theatre rises twenty-two metres, and a long stretch of the town wall survives.

The Challant family, viscounts of Aosta, built three of the castles along the road. Their best-known castle is Fénis, and it looks like a child's drawing of a castle. A five-sided keep stands inside a double ring of walls, with round and square towers joined by a covered walkway. It took that shape between about 1320 and 1420, under Aymon of Challant and then his son Boniface. It was never a serious fortress. It sits on a low knoll with higher ground behind it, and it was built as a seat of administration and a display of standing. The Piedmontese painter Giacomo Jaquerio and his circle decorated the courtyard in the fifteenth century. Saint George kills the dragon there, and a row of sages and prophets hold scrolls of proverbs in old French. By the nineteenth century the castle was a farm building. The architect Alfredo d'Andrade bought it in 1895 and began restoring it.

Lower down the valley the Challant built Issogne as a Renaissance house around a courtyard. In the middle of the yard stands a pomegranate tree made entirely of wrought iron, a fountain that Giorgio di Challant had made around 1502 as a wedding present. Under the portico, around 1500, a painter known only as Magister Collinus filled the lunettes with the shops of a town, from the baker at his oven to the apothecary with his jars. One of them is a cheese seller's, with wheels that look very much like Fontina. Across the river, on a crag above Verrès, stands a Challant castle of the opposite kind, a single severe cube.

Mont Blanc and the Cervino, the Matterhorn, close the valley at its head and along its northern side, and to the south rises Gran Paradiso. Near Aosta, King Victor Emmanuel II bought the castle of Sarre as a hunting lodge and decorated it with the horns of several thousand ibex and chamois. In 1856 the same king turned the Gran Paradiso massif into a royal hunting reserve, largely to stop other people shooting the last Alpine ibex in the Alps, of which perhaps sixty were left.

Protected, the herd grew. The reserve was handed to the state as Italy's first national park in 1922. The ibex recovered to a few thousand, collapsed again to about four hundred during the Second World War, and have since climbed back. Every ibex now living in the Alps descends from the animals that survived here. The paths the royal gamekeepers cut are the walking trails today, and the ibex are used enough to people that they will stand on a rock and look at you.
