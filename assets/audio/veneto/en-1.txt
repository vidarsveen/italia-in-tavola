Valpolicella and the art of appassimento.

The palace cellars at Ravenna were running low on a wine called acinaticium, and the steward of the royal estates reported it. Cassiodorus, praetorian prefect to the Gothic kings of Italy, wrote to the tax official of Venetia in the 530s and told him to buy more from the landowners of Verona, at a fair price. His letter says how the wine was made. The grapes were picked in autumn and hung upside down on pergolas in the farmhouses, and they were not trodden. They wrinkled without rotting until December, when the winter made their juice run. So the wine was new just when every other cellar held last year's. It was sweet and so dark that it might have been pressed from purple dye, and he called it a purple you could drink. North of Verona, in the hills of Valpolicella, the grapes are still dried before they become wine.

Valpolicella is a fan of valleys that run up from the plain towards the Lessini mountains. The historic Classico zone is five communes in the western valleys. The slopes are limestone and volcanic basalt, and the valley floors are gravel. Cool air comes down from the mountains at night, and Lake Garda, to the west, warms the hills.

The three main grapes are rarely grown anywhere else. Corvina has thick skins and tastes of sour cherry. Corvinone is a relation with larger berries that dries well, and Rondinella gives colour and a reliable crop.

In September and October the best bunches are picked and laid in a single layer on bamboo racks called arele, or in shallow plastic crates, in an airy loft called the fruttaio. The air in the loft may be controlled, but the rules do not allow dehumidifiers that dry the grapes with heat. Nor may the grapes be made into wine before 1 December. By then, or later, they have lost 30 to 40 per cent of their weight as water, and what is left has more sugar, more acid, more tannin and more flavour. The grape's chemistry changes too. Glycerol rises, which gives the wine its slippery texture, and the flavours of dried fruit, fig and bitter chocolate appear. On some bunches a grey mould is welcome, because it adds spice and still more glycerol.

What Cassiodorus described was a sweet wine, and Recioto della Valpolicella is its descendant. The dried grapes were pressed in the cold, the cold stopped the yeast before it had turned all the sugar to alcohol, and the wine kept its sweetness. Amarone is what happens when the yeast is allowed to finish. The wine comes out dry, powerful and slightly bitter, which is what amaro means. Negrar tells a story about how that first happened. In 1936, it goes, Adelino Lucchese, cellar master of the cooperative there, tasted a cask of Recioto that had fermented dry by mistake. It was not an amaro, he said, but an amarone, a big bitter one. The cooperative itself calls the story a legend, and its own papers contradict it. In the summer of 1933 Count Pieralvise Serego Alighieri, one of its main shareholders, wrote that he had been pursuing this wine for forty years. By the middle of 1936, the year of the supposed accident, an inventory already listed three vintages of Amarone.

Amarone was first offered for sale in the 1950s, and Bolla first showed its 1950 at a restaurant in the Galleria in Milan. It stayed a small wine for a long time, and in the mid-1980s the zone made about two and a half million bottles a year. Then the rest of the world found it. In 2021 Valpolicella made nearly nineteen million bottles of Amarone, and the vineyard had grown by more than half to supply it.

Amarone leaves something behind. When the new wine is drawn off in late winter, the skins of the dried grapes still hold sugar and flavour. Young Valpolicella is poured over them and ferments again, and it takes on about a degree of alcohol, more colour and some of Amarone's dried fruit. That is Ripasso, re-passed. The rules allow only one such refermentation, and the Ripasso may be no more than twice the volume of the Amarone or Recioto whose skins it used.

Grapes that are not dried make young Valpolicella and Valpolicella Superiore, fresh wines with red fruit and moderate alcohol, served cool with pasta and cold cuts. Over the ridge to the west, on the eastern shore of Lake Garda, the same grapes make Bardolino, which is lighter still, and Chiaretto, a pale rosé for the lake. They show what the grapes taste like before the loft. The loft is also where a year can be lost. In 2014 rain and hail spoiled the harvest and the drying grapes. Romano Dal Forno and Bertani made no Amarone that year, and the Quintarelli estate sold its whole crop under the name of a lesser red.
