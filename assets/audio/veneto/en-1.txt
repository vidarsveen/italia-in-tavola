Valpolicella and the art of appassimento.

North of Verona, one set of vineyards makes four wines from the same grapes: light Valpolicella, sweet Recioto, dry and powerful Amarone, and Ripasso in between. The trick is drying the grapes.

Drive north from Verona and the plain breaks into a fan of valleys running up toward the Lessini mountains. This is Valpolicella, and it does something no other Italian region does on this scale: it picks its best grapes and then leaves them for months to dry before making wine. The method is called appassimento, and it turns a light, cherry-scented red into Amarone, one of the biggest and most expensive wines in Italy.

The valleys and the grapes.

The historic Classico zone is five communes in the western valleys: Fumane, Marano, Negrar, San Pietro in Cariano and Sant'Ambrogio. East of them lies the Valpantena, and beyond it the extended zone toward Soave, added when the DOC was drawn in 1968. The soils are limestone and volcanic basalt on the slopes, gravel on the valley floors, and the hills are cooled at night by air sliding down from the Lessini and warmed by Lake Garda to the west.

The grapes are local and hard to find anywhere else. Corvina is the backbone, thick-skinned and sour-cherry flavoured, and the rules require it to be 45–95% of the blend. Corvinone, a larger-berried relative that dries well, may replace up to half of it. Rondinella adds colour and reliability, and small amounts of Molinara, Oseleta or Croatina fill the rest. None of these grapes is heroic on its own; the region's genius is what it does to them.

Drying the harvest.

In September and October the best bunches are laid out in a single layer on bamboo racks called arele, or in shallow plastic crates, in a ventilated loft called the fruttaio. There they sit until at least the beginning of December, often longer, losing 30–40% of their weight as water evaporates. Sugar, acid, tannin and flavour concentrate, and the grape's own chemistry changes: glycerol rises, giving the wine its slippery texture, and the dried-fruit, fig and bitter-chocolate notes appear. A grey mould that is welcome on some bunches adds spice; a wet autumn can ruin the whole loft.

Key facts.

Valpolicella DOC dates from 1968; Amarone and Recioto became DOCG in 2010, Ripasso got its own DOC the same year.

Amarone must reach at least 14% alcohol and age at least two years, four for Riserva; most are 15–16.5%.

The Valpolicella vineyard has grown from about 5,000 hectares in 2000 to more than 8,000 today, driven by demand for Amarone.

The word Recioto comes from recie, Veronese for "ears": the ripest side lobes of the bunch, once picked for drying.

Recioto, Amarone, Ripasso.

The original dried-grape wine of Verona is Recioto della Valpolicella, sweet, dark and ancient; a sixth-century letter by Cassiodorus praised the acinaticum, the dried-grape wine of these hills. Fermentation was stopped by the cold of winter, leaving sugar in the wine. Amarone is what happens when the yeast is allowed to finish: the wine becomes dry, powerful and slightly bitter, which is what amaro means. The story told in Negrar is that in 1936 a cellarmaster found a cask of Recioto that had fermented to dryness and declared it "not an amaro but an amarone", a big bitter one. The first commercial bottles appeared in the 1950s, and Amarone stayed a curiosity until the 1990s, when it became one of Italy's export stars.

Ripasso, "re-passed", is the clever middle way. In late winter, when the Amarone has been drawn off, its warm, sugar-rich skins are still in the vat. Young Valpolicella is poured over them and referments for a couple of weeks, gaining a degree of alcohol, more colour and some of the dried-fruit flavour. The rule is that each lot of Amarone skins may produce at most twice its own volume of Ripasso. It costs a third of the price and is the wine most Veronese actually drink on a Sunday.

In the glass: Amarone della Valpolicella.

 Colour: Deep garnet, slow legs.

 Nose: Dried cherry, fig, raisin, black pepper, bitter chocolate, balsamic.

 Palate: Full body, velvety texture, ripe tannin, bitter-almond finish, often a few grams of sugar.

 Alcohol: 15–17%.

 Serve: 18 °C, decanted; from six years old, and after the main course rather than with it.

 At the table: Braised beef or horse, game, aged Monte Veronese and Asiago, or on its own.

Plain Valpolicella and Bardolino.

Do not overlook the undried wines. Young Valpolicella and Valpolicella Superiore are fresh, red-fruited and moderate in alcohol, meant for pasta and cold cuts and served cool. Across the ridge on the eastern shore of Lake Garda, the same grapes make Bardolino, lighter still, and Chiaretto, a pale rosé for the lake. The dried-grape wines get the headlines, but the plain ones show what the grapes taste like before the loft.

Before you move on.

Valpolicella's grapes are Corvina, Corvinone and Rondinella; appassimento dries them for three months or more in a fruttaio.

Recioto is the sweet original, Amarone the dry and powerful version, Ripasso young Valpolicella refermented on Amarone skins.

Amarone is 15% or more, velvety and bitter-edged: serve it with braised meat and old cheese, or after dinner.
