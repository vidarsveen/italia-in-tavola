Prosecco: from hills to glass.

"Here growes the wine Pucinum, now called Prosecho, much celebrated by Pliny." The Englishman Fynes Moryson wrote that about the coast near Trieste, in the account of his travels in the 1590s that he published in 1617. Prosecco is a village on the hills above Trieste. The vine is said to have spread from there westward into the Veneto, and the wine carried the village's name with it. Four centuries later a grape called Prosecco was growing in Brazil and in Australia, and anyone could print the word on a label.

A place name can be protected across the European Union, but a grape name cannot. So in 2009 Italy gave the grape a different name, Glera, and made Prosecco the name of a place again. The new Prosecco DOC takes in nine provinces, from Treviso and Venice east through Friuli to Trieste, where the village is. In Australia the name held. Otto Dal Zotto had planted the grape in the King Valley in 1999, and Australia turned down the European claim in 2013. Under a trade agreement of March 2026, Australian growers may go on calling their wine Prosecco at home, and they have ten years to stop using the word on exports.

Glera itself is a vigorous grape that gives large crops. It has a light scent of green apple, pear and white flowers and is not strongly aromatic, which suits a soft, fruity sparkling wine.

The wine began in the hills between Conegliano and Valdobbiadene, north of Treviso, in fifteen communes of steep, narrow ridges. The vines grow on grassy terraces called ciglioni, and on the steepest slopes every job is done by hand. A hectare there takes 800 to 900 hours of work a year, and a hectare on the plain about 150. UNESCO put these hills on its World Heritage list in 2019. The most famous slope, Cartizze, above Valdobbiadene, covers about 107 hectares, and some 140 owners share it.

The farms in these hills once made their wine sparkle in the bottle. It fermented a second time there and was poured cloudy off its yeast, a style called col fondo, with the bottom. Conegliano had had a wine school since 1876, the first in the Kingdom of Italy, and the chemist Antonio Carpenè, who had campaigned for it, made sparkling wine there by a method of his own. In 1895 Federico Martinotti, director of the wine institute at Asti, patented a way to run the second fermentation in a sealed tank instead. The Frenchman Eugène Charmat developed the idea from 1907, and abroad the method still carries his name. Over the first half of the twentieth century the farms in the hills moved their wine from the bottle to the tank.

Making the wine in a tank changes how it tastes as well as what it costs. Champagne ferments a second time in each bottle and then rests on its yeast for months or years, which gives it flavours of bread and biscuit. Prosecco referments in the tank for a few weeks and is bottled under pressure, so it keeps the fruit of the grape. It tastes of pear and apple rather than toast, and it is made to be drunk young. It is also quicker and cheaper to make. Until about 1980 Prosecco was known mostly in the places where it was made. It grew in the 1990s and fast after 2005, and in 2013 it overtook Champagne. In 2025 the Prosecco DOC filled 667 million bottles, while Champagne shipped 266 million.

On the label, Extra Dry, the traditional style and the most common, is sweeter than Brut, and Dry is sweeter still. The hills also still make the old farmhouse wine, now labelled Sui lieviti. It is refermented in the bottle and left cloudy on its lees, it is never disgorged, and it is always made in the driest style.

The Veneto's other whites come from the lake, the plain and the hills east of Verona. On the southern shore of Lake Garda, shared with Lombardy, Lugana makes a fuller white from the Turbiana grape, and the flat land south of the lake makes Custoza. Pinot Grigio grows across the whole plain. The Delle Venezie DOC, created for it in 2017, covers some 27,000 hectares and makes about 230 million bottles a year, which is more wine than the whole of Greece produces.

East of Verona, around the walled town of Soave and Monteforte d'Alpone, the grape is Garganega, and it must make up at least 70 per cent of the wine. In October 1931 Soave became the first Italian wine recognised by law as a vino tipico, a typical wine of quality. The Classico hills are black volcanic basalt on the eastern side and limestone on the west. The wine is dry and tastes of almond and lemon, with a mineral edge in the best sites. In 1971 the Pieropan family bottled the grapes of one of those sites, Calvarino, under its own name, and the family counts it as the first Italian white named after a single vineyard. When the Veneto got its first DOCG, in 1998, it went to a Soave, and to the sweet one. Recioto di Soave is made from Garganega dried after the harvest, the way Verona's grapes were dried when Cassiodorus wrote.
