Prosecco: from hills to glass.

The world's best-selling sparkling wine is made in a tank, not a bottle, from a grape that changed its name. How to tell the plain from the Superiore, what 'Extra Dry' really means, and Veneto's other whites.

More bottles of Prosecco are opened each year than of Champagne, which is a remarkable thing for a wine that thirty years ago was a local fizz drunk in the bars of Treviso. Veneto is Italy's largest wine region by volume, and Prosecco is the reason. It is also one of the easiest wines to buy badly, because the same word covers an industrial plain and a strip of near-vertical hills that UNESCO put on its World Heritage list.

A grape that changed its name.

Until 2009 both the wine and the grape were called Prosecco. That year the grape was officially renamed Glera, and "Prosecco" was tied to a place: a village near Trieste from which the vine is said to have spread. The point was legal. A place name can be protected across the European Union; a grape name cannot, and Prosecco had begun appearing on bottles from Brazil to Australia. Glera is a vigorous, high-yielding grape with a light flavour of green apple, pear and white flowers, and low aromatic intensity, which is exactly what a soft, fruity sparkling wine needs.

Three tiers.

Reading the label.

Prosecco DOC: the big zone, nine provinces across Veneto and Friuli, mostly flat, machine-harvested; over 600 million bottles a year. Treviso on the label means the grapes came from that province.

Conegliano Valdobbiadene Prosecco Superiore DOCG: the original hills, fifteen communes, hand-picked; around 100 million bottles. Rive plus a village name means a single steep site; Cartizze is 107 hectares of the steepest slopes at Valdobbiadene, the grand cru.

Asolo Prosecco DOCG: a smaller hill zone to the south-west, around the town of Asolo.

The Superiore hills are the point of the reading's title. Between Conegliano and Valdobbiadene the land rises in steep, narrow ridges, worked by hand on grassy terraces called ciglioni; some slopes take hundreds of hours a hectare each year, five times the work of the plain. The Conegliano wine school, founded in 1876, was the first in Italy, and it was here that Antonio Carpenè and his successors worked out how to make Glera sparkle cheaply and cleanly.

Tank, not bottle.

Champagne gets its bubbles from a second fermentation inside each bottle, followed by months or years on the yeast, which gives bread and biscuit flavours. Prosecco uses the Martinotti method, patented in Asti in 1895 and known abroad as Charmat: the base wine referments in a sealed steel tank for a few weeks and is bottled under pressure. It is cheaper, faster and, more importantly, it keeps the grape's fresh fruit instead of covering it with yeast. That is why Prosecco tastes of pear and apple rather than toast, and why it is meant to be drunk young.

The sweetness scale is the trap. Brut is dry (under 12 g of sugar per litre). Extra Dry, the traditional style and the most common, is sweeter than Brut at 12–17 g. Dry is sweeter still, 17–32 g, and Cartizze is usually made this way. Since 2019 the DOCG has also allowed Sui lieviti, the old farmhouse style refermented in the bottle and left cloudy on its lees, known locally as col fondo; and since 2020 there is a Prosecco DOC Rosé, Glera with 10–15% Pinot Noir.

In the glass: Conegliano Valdobbiadene Prosecco Superiore Brut.

 Colour: Pale straw, fine persistent bubbles.

 Nose: Green apple, pear, white peach, acacia flowers.

 Palate: Light body, crisp acidity, soft mousse, clean dry finish.

 Alcohol: 11–12%.

 Serve: 6–8 °C, in a white-wine glass rather than a flute; drink within two years.

 At the table: Cicchetti, prosciutto, fried seafood, risi e bisi; the base of a Bellini and a spritz.

Veneto's other whites.

West of Verona is Soave, Italy's most important dry white by history if not by fame. The grape is Garganega, at least 70%, grown around the walled town of Soave and Monteforte d'Alpone; the Classico hills are black volcanic basalt on the eastern side and limestone on the west, and the wine is dry, almondy and lemony, with a mineral edge in the best sites. Recioto di Soave, from dried grapes, was Veneto's first DOCG in 1998. On the southern shore of Lake Garda, shared with Lombardy, Lugana makes a fuller white from Turbiana, and the flat land south of the lake makes Custoza. And across the whole Veneto plain grows Italy's ocean of Pinot Grigio: the Delle Venezie DOC, created in 2017, is by itself larger than most countries' entire wine output.

Before you move on.

Glera is the grape, Prosecco the place; DOC is the plain, Conegliano Valdobbiadene DOCG the hills, Cartizze the top slope.

Tank method keeps fresh pear and apple fruit; Brut is drier than Extra Dry, which is drier than Dry.

Soave, from Garganega on volcanic hills, is Veneto's serious still white; Pinot Grigio is its volume white.
