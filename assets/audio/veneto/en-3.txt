Cicchetti and the Venetian bàcaro.

On Røst, at the outer end of Lofoten, the fishermen dried their cod in the wind and the sun without salt until it was as hard as wood. Before they ate it, they beat it with the back of an axe. The Venetian merchant Pietro Querini wrote that down in 1432, and he had never meant to be there. The Querina, the ship he sailed in, had left Candia, on Crete, the year before with Malvasia wine and spices. In November a storm tore away her rudder, and in December the crew abandoned her in two boats. The smaller boat was never seen again. On 6 January 1432 sixteen men from the larger one came ashore on Sandøya, an empty island off Røst, and lived on shellfish until fishermen found them. They stayed among the people of Røst for more than three months.

Eleven of the survivors reached home, Querini himself in January 1433. The islanders had given them sixty stockfish to trade, and those went for herring, horses and bread on the way south, so none of the fish came to Venice with him. The fish was already traded without him. Querini recorded that about fifty boats carried the islanders' stockfish to Bergen, where German, English and Scottish merchants bought it. Venice needed fish that kept through Lent and on its ships, and it came to buy stockfish too. In the Veneto the word baccalà still means stockfish, not salt cod.

On the counter of a Venetian wine bar that fish becomes baccalà mantecato. It is soaked for days, poached, and beaten with olive oil until it turns into a pale, airy cream, which is spread on bread or on a slice of grilled white polenta.

From late morning the counters of the bàcari, the small wine bars of Venice, fill with saucers of cicchetti. There is whipped stockfish on bread, a fried meatball, a sardine under a heap of onions, and half a boiled egg with an anchovy. Each costs a euro or two and goes down in two bites, standing, with an ombra, a small glass of wine. Then people walk on to the next bar. Most bàcari are near the Rialto market or in Cannaregio. They open early and close early, and the wine, a Prosecco, a Soave or a red Raboso, is poured from a jug rather than from a bottle with a label.

Nobody can prove where the names come from. The usual story is that the wine sellers in Piazza San Marco moved their stalls through the day to stay in the shade of the Campanile. A glass in the shade became an ombra, a shadow. Nothing records it, and the Venetian etymological dictionary takes the word to mean a small amount, as in a shadow of salt. Bàcaro is often traced to Bacchus, and the writer Elio Zorzi told a more particular story. Some time after 1869 Pantaleo Fabiano, from Trani in Puglia, began selling Puglian wine in Venice. An old gondolier from the ferry at San Silvestro tasted it and called it un vin da bàcaro, a wine for making merry, and the wine shops took the name. Cicchetto probably comes from the French chiquet, a small piece.

The polenta under the stockfish is white. On the plain and in the hills around Venice, Padua and Treviso, it was made from white maize, and an old variety called biancoperla is still grown for it. In the mountains polenta was yellow. White polenta goes with what comes out of the lagoon, the tiny grey shrimp called schie and the soft-shelled crabs called moeche. Venice is also a city of rice. Risi e bisi, rice with the first peas from the lagoon islands, was by tradition served to the doge on St Mark's day, 25 April. It is thicker than a soup and looser than a risotto, and it is eaten with a spoon. Cuttlefish ink turns risotto al nero di seppia black, and on Burano the rice is cooked in the broth of the lagoon's goby, as risotto di gò.

Vicenza cooks the stockfish another way. The confraternity founded at Sandrigo in 1987 sets down the recipe for baccalà alla vicentina. The fish is cooked for hours at the barest simmer with onions, salted sardines, milk, grated grana and oil, and the pot is shaken rather than stirred. It is eaten with polenta as a main course, and since 2002 Sandrigo has been twinned with Røst. Elsewhere inland, a town is often known for one thing it grows. Treviso has its red radicchio, Bassano its white asparagus, Marostica its cherries, and the plateau above Vicenza its Asiago cheese. Verona still puts horse on the menu, in a stew called pastissada de caval. Its legend goes back to 489, when Theodoric defeated Odoacer outside the city walls and the hungry townspeople are said to have stewed the dead horses in wine and spices. The battle happened, but no document links it to the stew. With boiled meats Verona serves pearà, stale bread cooked with beef marrow and broth and a great deal of pepper, which is what its name means.

Two things from the Veneto are now made all over the world. The usual story of the spritz, which no document supports, is that Austrian soldiers in the nineteenth century asked for the local wine to be sprayed, gespritzt, with water. The bitter came later. The Barbieri brothers launched Aperol at the Padua fair in 1919, and the Pilla brothers registered Select in Venice in 1920. The drink became bitter, Prosecco and a splash of soda, with an olive in Venice and a slice of orange elsewhere. Select is still the one Venice pours, and Aperol the one the rest of the world knows, promoted abroad by Campari, which bought its maker in 2003.

The dessert called tiramisù is only about fifty years old. Le Beccherie, a restaurant in Treviso, says that Alba Campeol and Roberto Linguanotto finished working it out in 1972, when tiramesù, pick me up, went on the menu. It was savoiardi biscuits soaked in coffee and layered with mascarpone cream, under cocoa. Giuseppe Maffioli wrote about it in the magazine Vin Veneto in 1981. Friuli claims it as well, for Norma Pielli, who cooked at a hotel in Tolmezzo in the 1950s, and for a cup dessert that Mario Cosolo served at Pieris in 1938.

The saucer of sardines under onions on the counter is a way of keeping fish in vinegar, and it is called sarde in saor. Sardines are floured and fried, then layered with onions stewed soft in vinegar, with raisins and pine nuts, and left for a day or two, so the fish tastes sweet and sour. The vinegar kept the fish on long voyages. A cookbook written in Venice in the fourteenth century already makes fish sour and spices it, in a dish it calls fish a savore, and saor is the Venetian form of the same word. In 1576, during a plague, the Senate vowed to build a church to the Redeemer. The plague was declared over on 13 July 1577, and Palladio's church of the Redentore stands on the Giudecca. Every July, on the Saturday night before the third Sunday, a bridge is laid on boats across the canal to the church. Venetians eat sarde in saor cold in their own boats on the water and wait for the fireworks.
