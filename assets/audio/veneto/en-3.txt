Cicchetti and the Venetian bàcaro.

Venice eats standing at the bar: sardines in sweet-and-sour onions, whipped stockfish from Norway, baby octopus and polenta. The bàcaro, the ombra, the spritz, and the dishes of the lagoon and the mainland.

Venetians do not sit down to lunch so much as graze through it. From late morning, in the small bars called bàcari, the counter fills with saucers of cicchetti: a slice of bread with a spoon of whipped cod, a fried meatball, a sardine under a heap of onions, half a boiled egg with an anchovy. Each costs a euro or two and is eaten in two bites with an ombra, a small glass of wine. Then you walk to the next one. It is the oldest fast food in Italy, and the best introduction to how the city cooks.

Ombra and bàcaro.

The names are the city's history in miniature. An ombra is a shadow: the wine sellers of Piazza San Marco once moved their stalls around the square through the day to stay in the shade of the Campanile, and a glass in the shade became simply un'ombra. Bàcaro is usually traced to Bacchus by way of far bàcara, to make merry. Cicchetto comes from the Latin ciccus, a trifle. The bàcari cluster around the Rialto market and in Cannaregio, they open early and close early, and the wine is a jug of Prosecco, Soave or Raboso rather than anything with a label.

Two dishes that explain Venice.

Sarde in saor is the sailors' dish. Sardines are floured and fried, then layered with onions stewed soft in vinegar, and left for a day or two; raisins and pine nuts go in as well, a sweet-and-sour taste that came through Byzantium and the Arab world along the spice routes. The vinegar preserved the fish on long voyages, and the onions kept scurvy away. It is still eaten cold, on the night of the Festa del Redentore in July, from boats on the lagoon.

Baccalà mantecato is the Norwegian dish. In 1432 the Venetian merchant Pietro Querini was shipwrecked on Røst in the Lofoten islands, wintered there, and came home with a description of the islanders' dried cod, stoccafisso. Venice, which needed fish that kept for Lent and for ships, adopted it, and to this day in Veneto the word baccalà means stockfish, not salt cod. For mantecato the fish is soaked for days, poached, and beaten with olive oil until it becomes a pale, airy mousse, spread on bread or grilled white polenta. Vicenza has its own version, baccalà alla vicentina, cooked slowly with onion, anchovy, milk and Parmesan and served with polenta as a main course.

Key facts.

Polenta in Venice is white, from the biancoperla corn of the eastern plain; the yellow kind belongs to the mountains and Verona.

Moeche are the lagoon's green crabs caught in the hours after moulting, in spring and autumn, and fried whole; schie are tiny grey shrimp served on soft polenta.

Bigoli in salsa, thick whole-wheat spaghetti with a sauce of melted onion and salted sardine or anchovy, is the dish of fasting days: Ash Wednesday, Good Friday, Christmas Eve.

Fegato alla veneziana is calf's liver sliced thin and cooked fast with an equal weight of onions.

Rice, the lagoon and the mainland.

Venice is a rice city, not a pasta city. Risi e bisi, rice with fresh spring peas from the lagoon islands, was served to the Doge on the feast of St Mark, 25 April; it is thicker than a soup and looser than a risotto, and should be eaten with a spoon. Cuttlefish ink turns risotto al nero di seppia black; in winter it is risotto di gò, made from the goby of the lagoon. Inland the produce is famous by town: red radicchio of Treviso, white asparagus of Bassano, the cherries of Marostica and the Asiago cheese of the plateau above Vicenza. Verona keeps horse on the menu, in pastissada de caval, a stew said to descend from the battle of 489 outside the city walls, and boils its meats to eat with pearà, a peppery sauce of breadcrumbs and bone marrow.

The spritz and the tiramisù.

Two Veneto inventions have conquered the world's bars and menus. The spritz began under Austrian rule in the nineteenth century, when soldiers asked for their Venetian wine to be sprayed, gespritzt, with water. The bitter came later: Select, made in Venice from 1920, is the local choice, Aperol from Padua the global one, and the modern drink is bitter, Prosecco and a splash of soda, with an olive in Venice and an orange slice elsewhere. Tiramisù, "pick me up", is a layer cake of coffee-soaked savoiardi biscuits and mascarpone cream that appeared on the menu of the restaurant Le Beccherie in Treviso in the early 1970s; other towns claim it, but Treviso's is the version the world eats.

Before you move on.

A bàcaro serves cicchetti, small bites, with an ombra, a small glass of wine; eat standing, then move on.

Sarde in saor is sweet-and-sour preserved fish from the spice trade; baccalà mantecato is Norwegian stockfish whipped with oil.

Venice cooks with rice and white polenta; the spritz and tiramisù both come from Veneto.
