Venice: a republic built on trade.

For eleven hundred years Venice was a state without land that fed Europe its pepper, sugar and coffee. The Rialto, the Arsenale and the Campanile, and how a trading empire ended up on the plate.

Venice had no fields, no forests, no mines and no fresh water. What it had was salt, boats and a position at the top of the Adriatic where the sea roads from the East met the river roads to Germany. On that it built a republic that lasted from the election of its first doge, traditionally dated to 697, until Napoleon dissolved it in 1797: eleven centuries, the longest-lived republic in history. Everything the city eats, and most of what it looks like, follows from that.

Salt, spices and the Rialto.

The first trade was salt from the lagoon pans at Chioggia, sold up the rivers of the Po valley in exchange for grain. Then came the East. After the Fourth Crusade in 1204 Venice held ports and islands from the Adriatic to the Black Sea, and its galleys brought back pepper, cinnamon, cloves, ginger and sugar from Alexandria and Beirut, and silk and cotton from Constantinople. The Rialto, the market on the highest ground in the city, was the place where all of it was sold; the Banco di Rialto of 1587 was one of Europe's first public banks. Fish is still sold there every morning under the same arcades.

The spice trade is why Venetian cooking tastes as it does. Raisins and pine nuts in the sardines, cinnamon in the pastries, sugar in everything: these were luxuries elsewhere and stock-in-trade here. The city is credited with introducing the fork to Western Europe, brought by a Byzantine princess who married a doge's son in the eleventh century, and with the first coffee in Italy, sold from 1615 and served since 1720 at Caffè Florian on the Piazza, one of the oldest cafés in the world still open.

The Arsenale.

A trading empire needs ships, and the republic built them in the Arsenale, a walled state shipyard founded in 1104 that grew to cover a sixth of the city. At its peak in the sixteenth century it employed sixteen thousand arsenalotti and could turn out a galley a day from prefabricated parts moved along a canal, an assembly line three centuries before Ford. Dante used its boiling pitch as an image of hell. It is still a naval base, and its vast halls now hold the Biennale.

Key facts.

The relics of St Mark were smuggled out of Alexandria in 828 hidden under pork, which Muslim customs officers would not touch; the basilica was built to hold them.

Glassmaking was moved to Murano in 1291 to keep furnaces away from the wooden city; Murano glass became a monopoly export.

Gondolas have been black since a sumptuary law of 1562; there were some ten thousand, and there are about four hundred today.

Marco Polo left Venice for China in 1271 and returned in 1295; his book made the East a European obsession.

The Campanile.

The Campanile di San Marco is the landmark of this module and the tallest thing in the city at 98.6 metres. It began as a watchtower and lighthouse in the ninth century and took its present form, the brick shaft and the green pyramid roof, in 1514. On 14 July 1902 it cracked, and at ten in the morning it folded quietly into the square, killing no one but the caretaker's cat. The city council met that evening and voted to rebuild it com'era, dov'era, as it was, where it was, and the new tower opened on St Mark's day, 25 April 1912. Galileo demonstrated his telescope to the doge from the old tower in 1609; the lift in the new one takes you to the same view over the lagoon.

After the republic.

Napoleon took the city in 1797, Austria held it for most of the next seventy years, and Italy annexed it in 1866. The trade went to Trieste and Genoa, and Venice became what it is now, a city living from visitors. The mainland took over the wealth: Verona, with its Roman Arena of the first century, the third largest amphitheatre in Italy and an opera house every summer since 1913; Padua with its university of 1222; Vicenza with the villas of Palladio. The historic city has fewer than fifty thousand residents and receives some thirty million visitors a year, and in 2024 it became the first city to charge day-trippers to enter. The MOSE barriers, raised for the first time in 2020, now hold back the highest tides. The bàcari still open at eleven.

Before you move on.

Venice was a republic of traders from 697 to 1797: salt first, then pepper, sugar, silk and coffee, sold at the Rialto.

The Arsenale was Europe's largest industrial site, building galleys on an assembly line; the Campanile fell in 1902 and was rebuilt as it was by 1912.

The spice trade is on the plate: raisins and pine nuts in sarde in saor, sugar and coffee in the cafés, the fork on the table.
