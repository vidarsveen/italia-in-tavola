Venice: a republic built on trade.

The napkins, the bread, the plates and even the knives and forks on the table were made of sugar. Venice laid that table for Henry III of France in July 1574, and an apothecary, Nicolò della Cavaliera, modelled about three hundred sugar figures to go on it, after designs by the architect Jacopo Sansovino. Venetian galleys had been bringing sugar and spices into the city for centuries, and the Cornaro family had grown sugar cane on Cyprus since the fourteenth century.

The city had almost nothing of its own except its position, at the top of the Adriatic, where the sea roads from the East met the river roads to Germany. It had no fields, no forests and no mines, and although it stood in water, as Marin Sanudo wrote in 1490, it had no water. Rain was filtered through sand into cisterns under the wellheads in its squares, and boatmen brought river water from the Brenta in barges. What Venice did have was salt, from the pans at Chioggia at the southern end of the lagoon. The republic kept a monopoly on it and sold it up the rivers of the Po valley as far as Cremona, Pavia and Milan, and it took grain, timber and iron in exchange. On that trade it built a republic that lasted about eleven centuries.

The spices came after the Fourth Crusade, in 1204, which left Venice with ports and islands from the Adriatic to the Black Sea. From the 1330s the state sent convoys of galleys, the mude, to Alexandria and Beirut, and they came back with pepper, cinnamon, cloves, ginger and sugar. The galleys also brought silk and cotton from Constantinople. Pepper made up more of the cargo than all the other spices together, and towards the end of the fifteenth century about 1,600 tonnes of spices a year came into the city. They were sold at the Rialto. The market has stood there since 1097, beside the rivus altus, the deep channel that gave it its name, and fish is still sold there every morning in the arcaded Pescheria.

Then Portugal found the sea route to India. In July 1501 the Venetian banker Girolamo Priuli wrote in his diary that spices of every kind would now fall enormously in Venice, because the buyers would go to Lisbon. Within fifteen years the spice reaching Venice had fallen below 500 tonnes a year.

Venetian cooking is usually explained by that trade. A cookbook written in the city in the fourteenth century, the Libro per cuoco, has 135 recipes and two mixtures of spice, a sweet one of cinnamon, nutmeg and ginger and a strong one. The raisins and pine nuts in sarde in saor are often called a memory of the galleys. The food historian Michael Krondl warns that the idea of a spiced Venetian cuisine was largely made up in the nineteenth century. The records do show a state that fed its nobles in public. The doge gave four banquets a year for about a hundred patricians, and one eighteenth-century menu ran from capons and roast pheasant to frog soup, rice with mussels, sole in saor and coffee. Every December he also gave each member of the Great Council five wild ducks, until in 1521 a silver coin called the osella, the bird, took their place.

Wine came by sea as well. The wines of Greece and Cyprus were sold in shops called malvasie, after Monemvasia in the Peloponnese, and the men who kept them had a guild of their own. In the early 1500s the city allowed only about twenty of these shops, and streets called della Malvasia still run between the canals. Coffee came from Istanbul. Venetian ships were bringing the beans by 1615, and the first coffee house opened on Piazza San Marco in 1683. Caffè Florian opened on the same piazza on 29 December 1720, under the name Alla Venezia Trionfante. By 1759 the city had well over two hundred coffee houses, and that year the government stopped new ones from opening.

The galleys were built in the Arsenale, the state shipyard, where up to two thousand men worked on an ordinary day. The state gave its shipwrights wine as well as wages. By the 1630s the wine ran from a fountain in the middle of the yard, and it cost the republic about two per cent of its yearly budget. Along the waterfront at San Biagio, the state ovens baked the biscuit that fed the galleys. Thirty-two new ovens were built there in 1473, and they took about a fifth of the flour the city used. The biscuit kept for a very long time. A store of it found on Crete in 1821 was more than a hundred and fifty years old.

The wine sellers of the piazza are said to have followed the shade of the Campanile di San Marco around the square. The tower is the tallest structure in the city, and it took its present shape, the brick shaft and the green pyramid roof, in 1514. On 14 July 1902, at 9.47 in the morning, it cracked and collapsed into the square, and it killed nobody but the caretaker's cat. The city council met that evening and voted to rebuild it com'era, dov'era, as it was and where it was. The new tower opened on St Mark's day, 25 April 1912.

The republic ended on 12 May 1797, when the Great Council abdicated, and Napoleon took the city. The doge's banquets ended with it. Caffè Florian, which had opened seventy-seven years earlier, stayed open, and it still serves coffee on the piazza.
