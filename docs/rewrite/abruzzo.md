## Openings already heard, and the one trap this region walks straight into

The listener reaches Abruzzo 1 having just heard:

- Marche 3: a technique as a second-person instruction (stoning an olive).
- Marche 4: a scene at a meal reported by a witness (Federico at table).
- **Lazio 1: a cargo of wine leaving the hills at night in barrels, bound for the city.**
- **Lazio 2: a hectare planted by a grandfather in 1953 whose wine was sold in bulk into Rome, bottled by the
  grandson in 2001.**

**So the tanker must not open reading 1.** The best-sourced material in the whole Abruzzo wine file is the bulk
economy — the *cariarelli* who bought the harvest and carried it north as a *taglio nobilitante* to lend colour
and body to thinner wines from Piedmont and the Veneto (W27), and the co-operatives selling 86% of their wine
unbottled (W18). It is excellent, and as an opening it would be the third consecutive chapter about wine
leaving a region in bulk. That is precisely the failure the Trentino review caught, when four chapters in a row
opened on a date. The bulk economy belongs in the body of reading 1, as the thing the opening pushes against.

Shapes spent across the twelve regions rewritten so far, not to be reused: a sales figure with a year; a
person's age and trade with a year; a person at work in the early morning; two things poured side by side; a
queue at a cart; a vehicle climbing; an army crossing a pass; "Until the 1830s X was…"; a man going out at
night with a dog; a named grower who says something; a competition with a date; a market word explained;
"Every spring/autumn, until…"; a grant or ruling with a date; goods leaving by rail; people given a deadline to
leave; a figure in a wall painting; a traveller noticing a rock; a cellar running low; a quotation; fishermen
drying fish; objects on a table made of sugar; hail destroying a crop; a dated document; a habitual work crew
going out; a shop sign; a cargo moving at night; one plot across two generations.

**Chosen shapes: 1 a refusal** (a man who sells most of his harvest and bottles almost none of it, and in three
years out of ten makes no white at all); **2 a decision under a clock** (the hours a winemaker has before
Montepulciano's skins have already made the colour). Story arcs: 1 a grape that was bought for what it could
do to other people's wine, and two men who would not sell it that way; 2 a wine that is not a by-product,
told through the mechanism that makes it impossible to make pale.

## What the readings must keep answering

Quiz (`content/quiz.js` / `.no.js`). Several answers are built on claims the research has now overturned:

- 1.1 the Tuscan town makes Sangiovese — **stands**.
- 1.2 sea air and cold mountain nights — stands; the mechanism is in the disciplinare (W21).
- 1.3 "The pergola was built for volume and the wine was sold in bulk" — **must be rewritten.** W20 says no
  source supports "designed for volume", and the pergola is also a heat and hail response. The bulk half is
  documented (W18); the pergola half is not.
- 2.1 "the skins are pressed off after six to twenty-four hours" — **must be rewritten.** The rule says white
  vinification *or* a short cold maceration of **8–12 hours** (W8); six to twenty-four is trade description of
  practice, not the rule.
- 2.2 "picked with the rosé in mind, often earlier" — producer practice, unsourced (Part C item 16). Rewrite or
  re-found on the mechanism.
- 2.3 Cerasuolo di Vittoria — stands.
- 3.1 the fornacella barely wider than the meat — stands, pending the food file.
- 3.2 pressed through steel strings — stands.
- 3.3 "set with pig's rennet rather than lamb's" — the rennet stands, but **F21/F23 say Slow Food makes no
  uniqueness claim and "the only cheese in Europe" is unsupported**, so the prose and the `why` must drop the
  superlative.
- 4.1 the mountains too poor to farm — stands, but note **F35: 36% of the territory is protected, not "roughly
  half"**, which the prose, the recap and the facts box all get wrong.
- 4.2 the Calderone split in 2000 — stands pending the food file.
- 4.3 "An earthquake in 1461 wrecked it" — **wrong, and the facts box repeats it.** F41: the 1461 quake damaged
  Rocca Calascio and **the Piccolomini rebuilt it, adding the four round towers in the 1460s**; the earthquake
  that ruined it and emptied the village was **1703**. The answer, the `why`, the prose and the box all change.

Tasting cards (`content/tasting.js` / `.no.js`), which must match the readings' tables character for character:
`IT-65|Montepulciano d'Abruzzo`, `IT-65|Cerasuolo d'Abruzzo`, `IT-65|Trebbiano d'Abruzzo`, `IT-65|Pecorino`.

house style against a legal minimum of 12% (12.5% Riserva) and are unchecked.

## Checked before drafting — readings 1 and 2

From `abruzzo-research-wine.md`. **Errors, gaps and overclaims in the old text:**

- **W6, the biggest hole.** Readings 1 and 2 predate the **2021–23 reorganisation of Abruzzo's appellations
  entirely**: the eight IGTs merged into a single **Terre d'Abruzzo IGT**, a **Superiore** mention introduced
  across the DOPs, and four provincial appellations (Colline Teramane, Colline Pescaresi, Terre de L'Aquila,
  Terre di Chieti) able to carry Superiore and Riserva. The whites were tightened too: 85% minimum of the
  Trebbiano varieties, yields cut about 10%, blending capped at 15%.
- **W5.** "Colline Teramane … the region's first DOCG" is true but stale: **Abruzzo now has three.** Tullum /
  Terre Tollesi DOCG on 4 July 2019 (Italy's 75th, the single comune of Tollo), and **Casauria DOCG in 2025**.
  The Gazzetta citation completing Casauria could not be found, only the consortium's listing, so write Colline
  Teramane (2003) and Tullum (2019) and call Casauria the newest, recognised in 2025.
- **W8.** Cerasuolo's maceration and colour. The rule is white vinification **or 8–12 hours** of cold
  maceration, not "six to twenty-four hours" (that is practice, and must be attributed as such), and the legal
  colour is **rosa ciliegia**, cherry pink, not "cherry red to deep coral".
- **W11.** Trebbiano. "Thought not to be a Trebbiano at all but Bombino Bianco" oversimplifies badly. The DOC
  permits **Trebbiano Abruzzese and/or Bombino Bianco and/or Trebbiano Toscano**; the two were separated in the
  national register in **1994**; no conclusive DNA study has settled it; and the SSR paper usually cited for it
  **does not mention Trebbiano Abruzzese at all**. Do not write "it is really Bombino Bianco".
- **W14.** Emidio Pepe **died on 28 July 2026, aged 94**, the day after his birthday, so the reading's present
  tense is now wrong. Also corrected: the **reds** were de-stemmed by hand and the **whites** crushed by foot;
  fermentation was in **glass-lined** concrete, the lining being the point because it is inert; and the estate
  **decants** old bottles after ten to twenty years rather than "re-corking" them.
- **W22, W23, W21.** Three specifics in the old text are unsupported and must go or soften: "permitted in
  twenty provinces" (the ministry register was unreachable), "buds late" (no source, and Serafini's 1854 line
  distinguishes an early *primaticcio* from a late *cordisco*), and the Gran Sasso "about forty kilometres
  inland". **"Rain shadow" is also undocumented** — what is sourced is thermal swing. "Two thirds mountain"
  (65%) and reading 2's "about 130 kilometres" of coast both check out.
- **W24.** The Costa dei Trabocchi is about **60 km across nine comuni including Ortona**, not "south of
  Ortona". D'Annunzio was born in Pescara and wrote about the **trabocco Turchino** at San Vito Chietino in
  *Il trionfo della morte* (1894), but the "skeleton of a great beast" wording could not be reached in the text
  — attribute the novel, drop the quotation.
- **W9.** The old text says "Italy's darkest rosé", which is **unverifiable**. What is defensible, with care, is
  that the 2010 DOC was the first Italian denomination given over entirely to a rosé — and even that is
  trade-blog sourcing. **Castel del Monte Bombino Nero DOCG** (Puglia, 2011) is exclusively rosé and outranks
  it. Do not upgrade the claim while rewriting.
- **W3.** "One of Italy's most planted" is right in substance but the figures disagree (34,800 ha on the 2010
  census, second among reds; 30,700 ha undated; fourth among all varieties on 2015 data). Safe: "second only to
  Sangiovese among Italy's red grapes", or "more than half of Abruzzo's vineyard". Do not print one national
  hectare figure as current.
- **W16 note.** Berry Bros places Valentini's Loreto Aprutino inside the Colline Teramane DOCG. That is
  **wrong** — that DOCG is the province of Teramo only, and Loreto Aprutino is in Pescara. Do not repeat the
  merchant's error. Pepe's Torano Nuovo *is* in Teramo, so the old text's placement of Pepe is right.

## 1. Montepulciano: the grape, not the town

- Summary → a grape bought for what it could do to other people's wine, and the growers who would not sell it
  that way.
- Lead (a refusal): Valentini at Loreto Aprutino selling about ninety-five per cent of his harvest and bottling
  the rest; no white at all in three years out of ten, no red in 2007 or 2008. Gabriele's line about taking the
  same care over everything, because at harvest you do not yet know what will be bottled.
- What the rest of the region was doing: the *cariarelli* at harvest, the wine going north as a *taglio
  nobilitante*; 13,000 growers in Chieti on under two hectares each; 86% of co-operative wine sold unbottled
  against 85% of estate wine sold in bottle. Two Abruzzos in one table, with a date.
- The name (quiz 1.1): Vino Nobile is Sangiovese from a Tuscan town; this grape is not grown there; the
  confusion is ampelographic and the name has never been explained. Torcia 1792 and Serafini's two
  Montepulcianos in 1854.
- The place (quiz 1.2): 65% mountain, a 130 km coastal strip, sea to the east and the Gran Sasso and Maiella
  to the west, vineyards to 700 m, strong day–night swings — in the appellation's own words. Tasting table.
- What changed it (quiz 1.3, rewritten): Masciarelli's 1981 first vintage off two and a half hectares, the
  density and the training, and what the estate was when he died in 2008; Pepe at Torano Nuovo from 1964, the
  hands, the feet, the glass-lined concrete, half of every vintage held back, and the 1975 that proves it. Box.
- The whites: the Trebbiano tangle, three permitted grapes, separated in the register in 1994, DNA still open;
  then Pecorino, and Cataldi Madonna putting the name on the front of the label in 1996.
- Ending: Pepe's held-back half, and a wine the region spent fifty years selling by the tanker now being opened
  at fifty years old.

## 2. Cerasuolo and the Adriatic rosé

- Summary → the one wine whose colour is decided by the clock rather than by the grower's taste.
- Lead (a decision under a clock): Montepulciano in the vat, the skins already giving up colour, and eight to
  twelve hours before the choice is gone.
- Why it cannot be pale: the pigment in the skins; the rules offering white vinification or a short cold
  maceration; so the region legislated for what the grape does rather than for what Provence does.
- What the trade says against what the rules say (quiz 2.1, rewritten): six to twenty-four hours is practice,
  eight to twelve is the text; «vino di una notte» is trade usage and appears in no disciplinare.
- Not a by-product (quiz 2.2, re-founded): the 1903 record of *vini cerasuoli* long made in Chieti, attributed
  to its single source; the everyday domestic wine of the house and the field; and the honest note that nothing
  documents it as the shepherds' wine.
- The DOC of 2010 (quiz 2.3 stands): 42 years inside the red's appellation; 85% and 12%; the legal colour
  *rosa ciliegia* and the almondy finish written into the rules. Tasting table and box.
- The coast: 60 km of trabocchi across nine comuni including Ortona, eighteenth-century structures, d'Annunzio
  and the Turchino in a novel of 1894; brodetto pointing forward to reading 3 in one clause only.
- Ending: the colour that tells you nothing about the wine, because the law lets two different methods reach it.
