## Section 1 — Food claim audit

### Peperone / Peperoni di Senise

**F1. "They have been protected as an IGP since 1996."**
- Verdict: **CONFIRMED**
- What is true: registered by **Regolamento (CE) n. 1263/96 of 1 July 1996**, published in OJ L 163 of
  2 July 1996, under the Art. 17 procedure of Reg. (CEE) 2081/92. The disciplinare's own header names
  that regulation. Note that a great many Italian food pages (Il Giornale del Cibo, Cookist,
  FruitJournal, several producers) say the IGP dates from **2016** — that is wrong, and probably a
  garbled reference to a later amendment. Do not repeat it.
- Source: disciplinare header, "Iscrizione nel Registro … ai sensi del Reg. CE n. 1263/96",
  <https://www.basilicatanet.com/public/web/documenti/disciplinare_peperonidisenise_IGP.pdf>; Qualivita,
  <https://www.qualivita.it/normativa/peperoni-di-senise-igp-modifica-disciplinare-guri-n-296/>

**F2. The disciplinare in force is the 1996 one.**
- Verdict: **WRONG — superseded**
- What is true: a *non-minor* amendment was approved in **November 2020** (Reg. UE 2020/1718, of 11
  November 2020, GUUE L 386 of 18 November 2020; Italian publication GURI n. 296 of 28 November 2020;
  the amendment application had been published in GUUE C 170 of 18 May 2020). Among the changes: the
  protected name became **plural, "Peperoni di Senise"**, because there are three ecotypes; sowing and
  transplanting dates and plant spacing were changed; mechanical equipment previously forbidden was
  allowed; the water content permitted in the dried product was **raised from 10–12% to ≤18%**; and the
  drying was rewritten as **indirect** sun or aerated rooms. A writer describing "the rules" should
  describe the 2020 rules, or say which text is being quoted.
- Source: Disciplinare.it summary of the amended text,
  <https://www.disciplinare.it/modifica-del-disciplinare-di-produzione-peperoni-di-senise-igp.html>;
  Qualivita, <https://www.qualivita.it/normativa/peperone-di-senise-igp-approvazione-modifica-disciplinare-guue-l-386/>

**F3. "A sweet pepper with unusually thin flesh, one and a half to two millimetres."**
- Verdict: **PARTLY RIGHT**
- What is true: the disciplinare sets flesh thickness **1.5–2.2 mm** for the *Appuntito* and *Uncino*
  types and **1.5–2.0 mm** for the *Tronco*. The "Key facts" box in the old text gives 1.5–2.2 mm, which
  is right as a range; the body text's "one and a half to two millimetres" is the Tronco figure applied
  to all three. Also specified: dry matter of the pericarp **13–15%**.
- Source: disciplinare art. 6 and art. 5, verbatim: "spessore della polpa: da 1,5 a 2,2 mm" (Appuntito,
  Uncino), "da 1,5 a 2,0 mm" (Tronco); "contenuto in sostanza secca del pericarpo pari a 13-15%".

**F4. "Three shapes: pointed, hooked and blunt."**
- Verdict: **PARTLY RIGHT — the third name is mistranslated**
- What is true: the three morphological types are **Appuntito** (pointed; the prevalent type, says the
  disciplinare), **Uncino** (apex curved into a hook) and **Tronco** — not "blunt" but *truncated*, and
  the disciplinare's own gloss is **"a naso di cane"**, dog's-nose. The Tronco also has three pronounced
  ribs, one more developed and curved at the tip. Dimensions: Appuntito 10–17 cm long, basal diameter
  3.5–5 cm; Uncino 11–16 cm, 3.5–5.2 cm; Tronco 9–14 cm, 3.0–5.1 cm. All three: stalk so firmly attached
  it does not come off even after drying; colour at commercial maturity green or **rosso porpora**;
  flavour sweet.
- Source: disciplinare artt. 2 and 6, verbatim.

**F5. The peppers come from Senise.**
- Verdict: **PARTLY RIGHT — the area is twelve communes, not one**
- What is true: the production zone is the land around Senise facing the **Sinni**, **Agri** and
  **Sarmento** valleys, in named river-flat (*golenali*) and flat parcels of: Francavilla sul Sinni,
  Chiaromonte, Senise, Noepoli, San Giorgio Lucano, Valsinni, Colobraro, Tursi, Montalbano Jonico,
  Craco, Roccanova, Sant'Arcangelo. Soils alluvial silty-sandy or medium-textured hill soils at
  **250–340 m**; Mediterranean climate, July–August means 25.5 °C, January–February means 7.10 °C. Note
  that **Craco** — the ghost town in reading 4 — is inside the pepper zone, which is a genuine link
  between the two readings.
- Source: disciplinare art. 3 and art. 4, verbatim.

**F6. "Picked in August, the peppers are threaded on long strings called serte … and hung outside in
the shade to dry for weeks."**
- Verdict: **WRONG in sequence — shade first, then sun**
- What is true: the 1996 disciplinare's *pratica di trasformazione* is explicit and in this order:
  (1) harvest at full maturity, from the **first ten days of August**, by hand, repeatedly, because the
  crop ripens in stages; (2) the pods are laid on cloths or nets **in dry, well-aired rooms for at least
  2–3 days, away from the light** ("lontano dalla luce"); (3) the stalks are threaded on fine twine so
  that the pods lie in a spiral offset by about 120°, making the *collane* or *serte*; (4) **"Le serte
  devono essere esposte al sole"** — the strings are then put in the sun and stay there until water
  content reaches 10–12%; (5) afterwards they are stored in airy rooms. The 2020 text replaces this with
  drying *indirectly* in sun or in aerated rooms and a ceiling of ≤18% water. Either way, "hung outside
  in the shade" is the opposite of the 1996 rule for the serte themselves.
- Source: disciplinare art. 4, "Pratica di trasformazione", points 1–4, verbatim; 2020 amendment via
  Disciplinare.it (F2).

**F7. "Serte, one and a half to two metres of them."**
- Verdict: **CONFIRMED (1996 text)**
- What is true: "In 'Serte' o 'Collane' di lunghezza variabile da 1,5 a 2,0 m", water ≤10–12%, colour
  *rosso vinaccia*. The 2020 text gives a maximum of 2.0 m and water ≤18%. Fresh peppers are marketed in
  12–15 kg wooden crates; dried in serte; transformed product in opaque glass of 500 g and 1000 g or
  plasticised paper sachets with a window, of 50 g and 100 g.
- Source: disciplinare artt. 6 and 7, verbatim.

**F8. "Dried, they can be ground into a sweet red powder the locals call zafarana."**
- Verdict: **PARTLY RIGHT on two counts**
- What is true: (a) the powder is **not a local habit outside the rules — it is in the disciplinare**,
  as a protected form of the product: "In 'Polvere' a grana finissima ottenuta dalla macina dei peperoni
  secchi previo trattamento in forno per eliminare il residuo di umidità", and the transformation
  section actually *prescribes* the oven treatment and the milling (art. 4, points 5–6). So the powder
  is an official, traditional form, not a by-product. (b) **zafarana / zafaran is the dialect name of
  the pepper itself**, not only of the powder — the Senise festival is *U strittul ru zafaran*, "the
  pepper alley". The word is a real Arabic loan, from **zaʿfarān** (saffron), the same root that gives
  Italian *zafferano*, Spanish *azafrán*; so the saffron link is etymological fact, not folk resemblance
  — though what the name transferred was the colour, not a comparison of flavour.
- Source: disciplinare artt. 4 and 6; Treccani, *zafferano*,
  <https://www.treccani.it/vocabolario/zafferano/>; festival name, ASSA Senise via
  <https://sagreautentiche.it/sagre/u-strittul-ru-zafaran-il-vicolo-del-peperone/>

**F9. "Crusco."**
- Verdict: **CONFIRMED, and worth a line**
- What is true: *crusco* is not a variety and not in the disciplinare; it is Lucanian dialect for
  crisp/crunchy — effectively onomatopoeic, the noise the pod makes between the teeth. The IGP protects
  the pepper; "peperone crusco" describes what frying does to it. A separate collective mark for
  "Crusco" has been discussed by the consortium precisely because the dialect word is unprotected.
- Source: Tenuta Padì (producer), <https://tenutapadi.it/2026/02/13/perche-si-chiama-peperone-crusco/>;
  Gazzetta del Mezzogiorno, "Peperone di Senise da proteggere: si va verso il marchio «Crusco»",
  <https://www.lagazzettadelmezzogiorno.it/news/potenza/1459183/>. **Both secondary/press.**

**F10. "Drop a whole dried pepper into hot olive oil for a few seconds … a few seconds too long and it
turns bitter."**
- Verdict: **CONFIRMED as practice; the chemistry is nowhere stated in a primary source**
- What is true: the practice everyone describes is **170–180 °C for 2–3 seconds**, lifted out while
  still bright red. The explanation offered in Italian sources is that drying concentrates the sugars
  and aromatics, and that concentrated sugar burns almost instantly at that temperature, leaving a
  bitterness that runs right through the flesh; seeds are removed because they scorch first. **No food-
  science paper was found** measuring this. The recipe's "at 170 °C caramel and carbon are a second
  apart" is a reasonable statement of caramelisation/pyrolysis but is the writer's own; it should not be
  attributed to anyone. The mechanism that *is* solidly stated in sources is the physical one: the
  residual water flashes to steam and sets the thin pericarp into a brittle sheet, and the pod finishes
  crisping as it cools, not in the oil.
- Source: Cibi Lucani, <https://www.cibilucani.it/blog/2025/06/23/peperoni-cruschi-cosa-sono-e-come-friggerli-alla-perfezione/>;
  Sapori dei Sassi, <https://www.saporideisassi.it/blog/come-si-friggono-e-si-preparano-i-peperoni-cruschi/>.
  **Producer/blog sources only.**

**F11. "Very little water in it … that is what makes the whole thing possible."**
- Verdict: **CONFIRMED**
- What is true: the variety is selected for a thin pericarp and low water; drying brings the pod to
  10–12% water (1996) and that degree of dehydration is what keeps mould off through a winter hanging on
  a wall. Dry matter of the fresh pericarp is fixed at 13–15% by the disciplinare, which is the
  measurable form of the same claim.
- Source: disciplinare art. 5; Casata del Lago, <https://casatadellago.com/peperoni-cruschi-come-si-essiccano/>

**F12. Where the pepper came from and when.**
- Verdict: **MISSING from the old text; solid enough to use with a hedge**
- What is true: Italian sources agree the pepper reached the Senise area with the Spanish, from the
  Antilles, between the **16th and 17th centuries**, and that cultivation is documented in the Sinni
  valley from then. This is repeated consistently but I found no archival citation; treat as "is said to
  have arrived" or attribute to the consortium/ALSIA.
- Source: CiboToday, <https://www.cibotoday.it/storie/peperone-senise-basilicata-produzione.html>;
  Slow Food Ark of Taste, <https://www.fondazioneslowfood.com/it/arca-del-gusto-slow-food/peperone-di-senise/>

**F13. Production volumes, and how much of what is sold as *cruschi* is actually Senise IGP.**
- Verdict: **The substitution problem is real and much bigger than the old text implies**
- What is true, with the years attached:
  - **c. 1990**: up to **200 ha** under peppers; the building of the **Monte Cotugno dam** flooded much
    of the traditional growing land and cut the area sharply. (The dam is on the Sinni at Senise.)
  - **2009**: 14 certified firms, **11 ha** in the control system.
  - **2020**: certified IGP area **no more than 8 ha**.
  - **2021 campaign**: area rose from seven to **36 ha**.
  - Consortium figures reported alongside: about **300 quintals of fresh peppers → about 30 quintals of
    finished cruschi**; another source gives 20–25 ha and about 300 tonnes fresh.
  - **2020**: the Senise IGP pepper is named by ICQRF (Italy's central food-fraud repression body) among
    the **50 most counterfeited Italian food products**, alongside Basilicata's Melanzana Rossa di
    Rotonda.
  - **2021–22**: the consortium adopted the **State anti-counterfeiting seal from the Istituto
    Poligrafico e Zecca dello Stato (IPZS)**, distributed to its **40 registered firms**.
- What could NOT be established: any published percentage of the market held by non-IGP or imported
  dried peppers. Several fraud investigations into Italian food (tomato, seeds) involve Chinese and
  Hungarian imports, but **none found names Senise peppers specifically**. The honest formulation is
  arithmetic: a few dozen hectares cannot supply a national snack, so most packets labelled *peperoni
  cruschi* are not Senise IGP; say that as arithmetic, not as an accusation.
- Source: Colture Protette/Edagricole, "Qualificare una filiera: i Peperoni di Senise Igp",
  <https://coltureprotette.edagricole.it/orticoltura/qualificare-una-filiera-i-peperoni-di-senise-igp/>;
  ALSIA, <https://www.alsia.it/opencms/opencms/Temi/attivita/Produzioni/Peperone-di-Senise-IGP/>;
  CIA via Gazzetta della Val d'Agri, <https://www.gazzettadellavaldagri.it/cia-il-peperone-igp-di-senise-e-la-melanzana-rossa-di-rotonda-tra-i-50-prodotti-alimentari-italiani-piu-contraffatti/>;
  FreshPlaza, <https://www.freshplaza.it/article/9692851/il-peperone-di-senise-igp-sceglie-il-contrassegno-di-stato-da-apporre-sulle-confezioni/>

### Pane di Matera

**F14. "Pane di Matera IGP (2008)."**
- Verdict: **CONFIRMED**
- What is true: registered by **Reg. (CE) n. 160/2008**, dated **21 February 2008**; Italian publication
  in GU 18 March 2008. The disciplinare in force is the 2018 consolidated MASAF text, which refers to
  Reg. (UE) 1151/2012. Production and packaging zone: the **whole province of Matera**.
- Source: disciplinare artt. 1 and 3; Qualigeo, <https://www.qualigeo.eu/prodotto-qualigeo/pane-di-matera-igp/>;
  Agraria.org, <https://www.agraria.org/prodottitipici/pane-di-matera.htm>

**F15. "Made from durum semolina, a sourdough mother, salt and water, and nothing else."**
- Verdict: **WRONG** (reading 3, and repeated in the `pane-di-matera` recipe headnote in both languages)
- What is true: the disciplinare's recipe, per 100 kg of semolina, is:
  semola rimacinata and/or semolato di grano duro **100 kg**; *lievito madre di produzione* **20–30 kg**;
  salt **2–3 kg**; water **75–85 l**; and **lievito compresso (Saccharomyces cerevisiae) 0.5–1 kg**.
  Baker's yeast is an ingredient of the protected bread, listed in the rules. "Nothing else" is not true,
  and the recipe's note "Sourdough, not yeast — the mother is in the rules" is the wrong way round: both
  are in the rules. The mother may be refreshed at most three times before it goes into the dough, and a
  portion is kept in the fridge for the next day's baking.
- Source: disciplinare art. 6, "Processo di produzione", verbatim table.

**F16. "Durum semolina from local wheats."**
- Verdict: **PARTLY RIGHT — there is a number and four named varieties**
- What is true: **"Almeno il 20% della semola rimacinata e/o semolato da utilizzare … deve provenire da
  ecotipi locali e vecchie varietà quali Cappelli, Duro Lucano, Capeiti, Appulo coltivate nel territorio
  della provincia di Matera."** No GMO semolina. The semolina itself must meet: protein ≥11%, yellow
  index ≥20, moisture ≤14.5%, ash ≤1.35% on dry matter. (Several secondary sources render the third
  variety as "Duilio" — the disciplinare says **Duro Lucano**.)
- Source: disciplinare art. 2, verbatim.

**F17. The sourdough mother.**
- Verdict: **MISSING from the old text, and it is the best fact in the document**
- What is true: the disciplinare specifies how the mother is made: **1 kg of durum semolina + 250 g of
  the pulp of fresh ripe fruit, previously macerated in water (250–300 cl)**; knead elastic, hold in a
  graduated food container at 26–30 °C until it doubles (10–12 hours), then refresh with semolina equal
  to the weight obtained plus 50% water, repeating until it rises in 3–4 hours. The IGP's own
  explanation of quality (art. 5a) puts *the method of preparing the mother* first, because it captures
  yeast strains that develop in the production area. A bread whose starter is legally required to begin
  with macerated fruit is a stronger fact than "a sourdough mother".
- Source: disciplinare art. 6, "Preparazione del lievito madre", verbatim; art. 5a.

**F18. "Shaped either as a high domed loaf or as a cornetto, a horn, weighs one to two kilos."**
- Verdict: **WRONG on the weights**
- What is true: art. 8 requires "**Forma a cornetto oppure a pane alto**; **pezzatura da 500 g a 10 kg**".
  Preforms are weighed from 0.6 kg to a maximum of 12 kg to give a finished loaf of 0.5 to 10 kg, ±10%.
  The 1–2 kg range in the old text and in the recipe headnote is the everyday bakery format, not the rule.
- Source: disciplinare artt. 6 and 8, verbatim.

**F19. "Crust at least 3 mm … straw yellow and full of irregular holes."**
- Verdict: **CONFIRMED**
- What is true: art. 8 lists "spessore della crosta di almeno 3 mm" and "mollica di colore giallo
  paglierino con caratteristica alveolazione". The IGP also fixes a **sensory profile** with scored
  descriptors — acid odour 1.0–2.0, **burnt odour 3.0–4.5**, acid taste 1.0–2.5, crust crispness 4.5–6.0,
  assessed to ISO 13299:2016 — which is the technical version of the old text's "almost black in places".
  Composition per 100 g: protein ≥8.1, carbohydrate ≥51.3 (fibre ≥2.9), fat ≥1.0, ash ≥2.24.
- Source: disciplinare artt. 2 and 8, verbatim.

**F20. "A one-kilo loaf keeps a week; two kilos keeps nine days."**
- Verdict: **WRONG on the second figure** (reading 3 key-facts box, reading 3 body, and the
  `pane-di-matera` recipe headnote in both languages)
- What is true: "La conservabilità … può raggiungere i **7 giorni** per la pezzatura da **1 Kg** ed i
  **14 giorni** per la pezzatura da **10 kg**. Per le pezzature intermedie la conservabilità è definita
  dal panificatore nel rispetto dei giorni sopra indicati." There is no nine-day figure and no two-kilo
  figure in the rules; the keeping time scales with the loaf up to a fortnight at ten kilos. (The Qualigeo
  summary also gives 7 and 14 days; the "9 days at 2 kg" appears to have entered the old text from a
  food blog.)
- Source: disciplinare art. 6, verbatim.

**F21. "Baked in a wood oven."**
- Verdict: **WRONG as a requirement**
- What is true: the rules allow **"forni a legna oppure a riscaldamento indiretto"** — wood or
  indirectly heated ovens. Wood firing is optional and is rewarded on the label: only if the loaf was
  baked in a wood oven may the packaging add **"pane cotto in forno a legna"**, and in that case
  **autochthonous woods** must be used. Baking runs from **1 hour for a 500 g loaf to 4 hours 30 minutes
  for a 10 kg loaf**, and the flue or oven door is opened for the last 15–30 minutes to let the steam out.
  Packaging must be done inside the province, in microperforated food film or a paper bag.
- Source: disciplinare art. 6, verbatim.

**F22. The loaf is stamped.**
- Verdict: **CONFIRMED, and the modern rule is different from the historic one — worth both**
- What is true: the disciplinare *requires* that every loaf be "marchiate sulla sommità con timbro a
  pressione riportante le lettere in stampato maiuscolo **'MT'**" before it goes into the oven. So the
  stamping survives, but what is pressed in now is the province's two letters, not a family's.
- Source: disciplinare art. 6, verbatim.

**F23. "Families baked once a week in the communal oven and stamped each loaf with a carved wooden or
terracotta seal bearing the head of the household's initials … The museum in Matera has a collection."**
- Verdict: **CONFIRMED — and the primary-ish source is the disciplinare itself**
- What is true: art. 7 of the MASAF disciplinare tells the story as part of the IGP's justification:
  every family had a mortar cut in stone for milling at home; private ovens gave way to public ones to
  which each family brought its own dough; each oven was cut in the rock and sealed shut; after about
  three hours the door was removed and the women collected their bread, **"riconoscendole dal marchio con
  le iniziali del proprio capofamiglia posto sul pane prima dell'infornata."** The document also dates
  the trade: four *maestri di centimoli* (millers) in Matera in **1857**; the first industrial mill in
  **1884**, about 50 workers and a siren; Pietro Antonio Ridola counted **eleven ovens in 1857** and
  about **fifteen in the mid-1950s**; over **130 bakers** in the Materano today.
  The stamps themselves: carved by **shepherds**, during transhumance, when they had time and wood; an
  upper figurative part (sacred scenes, human figures, animals, symbols of virility and prosperity) and a
  handle whose end carried the initials or effigy of the head of the household; **replaced with a new one
  when the eldest man died**; also given by a suitor to a woman, kept if she accepted him and returned if
  she did not. The collection is at the **Museo Archeologico Nazionale Domenico Ridola** in Matera.
- Source: disciplinare art. 7, verbatim; Turismo.it, "4 cose da sapere sui timbri da pane di Matera",
  <https://www.turismo.it/tradizioni/articolo/art/divbasilicata-4-cose-da-sapere-sui-timbri-da-pane-di-materadiv-id-11303/>

**F24. The cornetto shape and what its peaks mean.**
- Verdict: **UNVERIFIABLE as symbolism; there is a competing practical explanation**
- What is true: two accounts circulate in Italian local sources, neither with a primary citation.
  (a) **Practical**: the tall *cornetto* form arose from the need to get more bread into the same oven
  as the population of the Sassi grew through the 18th and 19th centuries — a height-for-floorspace
  trade. (b) **Symbolic**: three cuts made with a knife, or three peaks, for the Trinity, as a household
  thanksgiving. The disciplinare says nothing about either. If the reading wants the Trinity, it must be
  attributed ("Matera bakers will tell you…") or left out.
- Source: WikiMatera, <https://www.wikimatera.it/cosa-mangiare-a-matera/il-pane-di-matera-igp-e-le-sue-ricette/il-pane-di-matera-igp/>;
  it.wikipedia, *Pane di Matera*. **Both weak.**

**F25. "Max 33% moisture."** (in the recipe's orbit; the old reading does not say it, some sources do)
- Verdict: **NOT IN THE DISCIPLINARE**
- What is true: art. 8 says only "I parametri di umidità sono quelli previsti dalla normativa cogente" —
  the moisture limits are whatever Italian bread law requires (which is where the 33% figure for large
  loaves comes from). Do not present 33% as an IGP rule.
- Source: disciplinare art. 8, verbatim.

### Lucanica, and the classical citations

**F26. "Cicero and Martial both mention Roman soldiers bringing it home from Lucania."**
- Verdict: **WRONG on both names**
- What is true: the soldiers are **Varro's**, not Cicero's or Martial's. *De lingua Latina* V.111:
  **"quod fartum intestinum crassum, Lucanicam dicunt, quod milites a Lucanis didicerunt"** — "the thing
  stuffed into the large intestine they call *lucanica*, because the soldiers learned it from the
  Lucanians." That single sentence, written c. 47–44 BC, is the whole basis of the story. **Martial does
  not mention soldiers at all**: *Epigrams* XIII.35, titled *Lucanicae*, reads in full
  **"Filia Picenae venio Lucanica porcae: / pultibus hinc niveis grata corona datur."** — "I come, a
  Lucanian sausage, daughter of a Picene sow; from me a welcome garland is given to snowy porridge."
  That is a two-line gift-tag about serving sausage with *puls*, and it names Picenum as the source of
  the pig. **Cicero: no passage was located.** The attribution is repeated by English Wikipedia
  (*Lucanica*), by the Picerno IGP literature and by food writing generally, always without a citation.
  Treat as unverified and drop it.
- Source: Varro via Loeb/LacusCurtius and topostext, <https://topostext.org/work/728>; Martial from The
  Latin Library, <https://thelatinlibrary.com/martial/mart13.shtml> (checked verbatim); the unsourced
  Cicero claim at <https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucanica>.

**F27. A Roman recipe exists.**
- Verdict: **CONFIRMED — and the old text misses it**
- What is true: **Apicius, *De re coquinaria* II.4**, gives *Lucanicae*: pepper, cumin, savory, rue,
  parsley, *condimentum*, bay berries and *liquamen* ground together, well-pounded meat worked in, then
  peppercorns, plenty of fat and pine nuts, filled into a very thinly drawn casing and **hung in the
  smoke**. A reading that wants the Roman sausage should quote the recipe rather than the etymology.
- Source: Apicius II.4 via Coquinaria, <https://coquinaria.nl/en/lucanian-sausages/>

**F28. "Its descendants are eaten from Greece to Brazil under names that are all the same word worn
down" (luganega, loukaniko, longaniza, linguiça).**
- Verdict: **CONFIRMED — these are genuine cognates, not folk etymology**
- What is true: Latin *lūcānica* → Byzantine Greek **λουκάνικον** → modern Greek *loukaniko*; a Vulgar
  Latin *\*lucanicia* gives Old Galician-Portuguese *linguainça* → Portuguese **linguiça**, and Spanish
  **longaniza** (with the form influenced by *longus*, "long"). Also Italian *luganega*, Bulgarian
  *lukanka*, Albanian *likëngë*, Basque *lukainka*, Arabic *laqāniq / naqāniq / maqāniq*, Hebrew
  *naqniq*. These are documented in the etymological entries, not only in food writing.
- Source: Wiktionary, *lucanica*, *longaniza*, *linguiça*, *longganisa*; en.wikipedia *Loukaniko*,
  *Luganega*, *Longaniza*.

**F29. "The version from Picerno is the one with a protected name today."**
- Verdict: **CONFIRMED, with a correction of implication**
- What is true: **Lucanica di Picerno IGP**, registered by **Reg. (UE) 2018/1615 of 22 October 2018**,
  OJ L 270 of 29 October 2018 (announced in Italy 29 October 2018). The protected product: boned and
  de-nerved shoulder, neck, *sottospalla*, belly, fillet tip and ham trimmings, with salt, sweet or hot
  pepper, **wild fennel seed**, black pepper, dextrose and sucrose; hung on racks, which gives its
  distinctive **"U" shape**; 250–350 g, 3.0–3.6 cm diameter, 20–35 cm long; **final ageing at least 18
  days at 13–18 °C and 75–85% RH**; fourteen communes of the province of Potenza (Picerno, Tito,
  Satriano di Lucania, Savoia di Lucania, Vietri di Potenza, Sant'Angelo Le Fratte, Brienza, Balvano,
  Ruoti, Baragiano, Bella, Muro Lucano, Castelgrande, Sasso di Castalda). **The correction**: the name
  was *revived* — Italian sources state that from the 1970s, as family production in and around Picerno
  became commercial, producers took the historical name for their product. The IGP is therefore a
  20th-century commercial adoption of an ancient word, not an unbroken 2,000-year line. Saying so is
  more interesting and more honest.
- Source: it.wikipedia *Salsiccia Lucanica di Picerno*; Qualivita,
  <https://www.qualivita.it/news/lucanica-di-picerno-igp-italia/>; product spec summary via
  <https://www.informacibo.it/salsiccia-lucanica-di-picerno-igp/>; Camera di commercio della Basilicata
  control plan, <https://www.basilicata.camcom.it/sites/default/files/contenuto_redazione_isin/pagina_base/allegati/dc_lucanica_di_picerno_rev.0_4-8-22.pdf>

### Cheese

**F30. "Caciocavallo podolico … can be aged five or six years."**
- Verdict: **PARTLY RIGHT — at the far end of a wide range**
- What is true: Slow Food's own Italian presidium page gives ageing **from three months to three
  years**, "and in some cases eight or ten years"; the Gargano presidium sets a **minimum of three
  months**. Commercial cave-aged wheels are typically 6–24 months. Five or six years exists but is
  exceptional; write it as "some wheels are held for years" or give the range.
- Source: Fondazione Slow Food, presidio Caciocavallo podolico del Gargano (IT),
  <https://www.fondazioneslowfood.com/it/presidi-slow-food/caciocavallo-podolico-del-gargano/>;
  presidio Basilicata (Abriola, PZ),
  <https://www.fondazioneslowfood.com/en/slow-food-presidia/basilicata-podolico-caciocavallo/>

**F31. "Made only from the milk of the Podolica cattle that graze the scrub and give very little of it."**
- Verdict: **CONFIRMED, and there are figures**
- What is true: the Podolica is a hardy semi-wild southern-Apennine breed, kept in wild or semi-wild
  conditions, about **25,000 head** remaining; a cow gives roughly **1,100–1,900 litres in a whole
  lactation** (one Slow Food figure: ~1,500 l a year), averaging about **5 litres a day**; the cows will
  not take to machine milking because they suckle the calf, so they are **milked by hand alongside the
  calf**. Milk is produced year-round with a clear **peak from March to May**, when the pasture is up —
  which is why the cheese is seasonal in practice. They were still draught animals on Basilicata farms
  into the 1950s. The cheese is *pasta filata*: curd cooked, matured in whey, sliced and spun with
  boiling water, then shaped by hand and tied in pairs.
- Source: SOZOOALP quaderno 7 (2012), "La razza bovina podolica",
  <https://www.sozooalp.it/fileadmin/superuser/Quaderni/quaderno_7/8_Procopio_SZA7.pdf>; Slow Food
  presidia pages as above; Ruminantia, <https://archivio2023-2024.ruminantia.it/vi-raccontiamo-le-razze-la-podolica/>

**F32. What "podolico" means legally.**
- Verdict: **MISSING and important**
- What is true: **there is no DOP or IGP for caciocavallo podolico.** Protection is a Slow Food
  Presidium — two of them relevant here, Basilicata (Abriola) and Gargano — which is a private quality
  project, not a legal designation. The DOP that exists is **Caciocavallo Silano**, which is a different
  product and is not restricted to Podolica milk. So "podolico" on a label is a breed claim with no legal
  backing; a reader in a shop should know that.
- Source: Slow Food presidia pages; en.wikipedia *Podolica*; List of Italian DOP cheeses.

**F33. "Pecorino di Filiano from the Vulture."**
- Verdict: **CONFIRMED**
- What is true: **Pecorino di Filiano DOP**, registered by **Reg. (CE) n. 1485/2007 of 14 December
  2007** (GUCE L 330 of 15 December 2007); whole raw sheep's milk from **Gentile di Puglia e di Lucania,
  Leccese, Comisana and Sarda** ewes; minimum ageing **180 days**, after which conforming wheels are
  fire-branded; about **thirty communes in the province of Potenza**, Filiano sitting in the
  Vulture–Melfese.
- Source: Agraria.org, <https://www.agraria.org/prodottitipici/pecorinofiliano.htm>; ONAF technical
  sheet, <https://www.onaf.it/uploads/public/2423_pecorino-di-filiano.pdf>

### Mollica, pasta, the pig, and "cucina povera"

**F34. "Stale bread … becomes the breadcrumb topping, mollica, that goes on almost every pasta dish
here in place of cheese, which cost money."**
- Verdict: **PARTLY RIGHT — the practice and the nickname are attested; the economic explanation is lore**
- What is true: fried or toasted breadcrumb as a topping is documented right across the Mezzogiorno and
  is popularly called **"il formaggio dei poveri"**, the poor man's cheese — *pasta mollicata* in
  Basilicata and Calabria, *muddica atturrata* in Sicily, *spaghetti con la mollica* in Puglia. The
  explanation that cheese was too dear for labouring households is repeated by every Italian food source
  and is consistent with the food-consumption evidence (F37), **but no primary source states it as
  documented economic history**. The often-repeated origin story — Armento, province of Potenza, AD 976,
  a woman cooking pasta with breadcrumbs to celebrate a victory over Saracen raiders led by Byzantine
  monks — is a legend with no source. Attribute or drop.
- Source: Cookist, <https://www.cookist.it/alla-scoperta-della-muddica-atturrata-siciliana-il-famoso-formaggio-dei-poveri/>;
  That's Apulia, <https://thatsapulia.it/mollica-di-pane-fritta/>. **All secondary.**

**F35. "Pasta is made without egg, dragged or rolled by hand: strascinati, cavatelli, and ferricelli
rolled around an iron rod."**
- Verdict: **CONFIRMED in substance**
- What is true: *strascinati* are made by dragging a piece of durum-and-water dough down the board with
  two or three fingers, which thins one wall and roughens the outside; *cavatelli* are hollowed with the
  thumb instead; **ferricelli** (also *fusilli al ferretto*, *maccaruni*) are wound around a **ferretto**,
  a long thin iron rod of square section — originally, sources say, a stiff broom straw. The frequent
  local claim that the ferretto dates from Greek colonisation is a local claim with no evidence; don't
  repeat it.
- Source: Taccuini Gastrosofici, <https://www.taccuinigastrosofici.it/ita/news/contemporanea/pasta-cereali/ferretti-e-strascinari-lucani.html>;
  Il Borghista, <https://www.ilborghista.it/pagina-ferricelli--patrimonio-gastronomico-lucano-82>

**F36. "Pig-killing in January supplied the year."**
- Verdict: **CONFIRMED, with a wider window**
- What is true: the rite is documented across rural Basilicata and the south, held **in the cold months,
  usually December to January** (some accounts extend to February), precisely so the meat could be worked
  and cured safely; it was a family-and-*vicinato* occasion with assigned roles, and everything was kept —
  fat, *cicoli*, lard, pancetta, rind, sausages, soppressate, capicolli, ham, bones in brine for cooking
  with vegetables through the year, head, ears, trotters, tripe.
- Source: Luciano Pignataro, <https://www.lucianopignataro.it/a/i-giorni-delluccisione-del-maiale/210635/>;
  Ricocrea, <https://www.ricocrea.it/2020/01/07/rititradizioni-la-festa-del-maiale/>

**F37. "This was the poorest region in Italy for most of the twentieth century."**
- Verdict: **WRONG as stated**
- What is true, with years attached:
  - **1941**: Abruzzo, Basilicata and Calabria all below 57% of national per-capita GDP, **Calabria
    lowest at 49%**.
  - **1951**: the year Italian regional inequality peaked; the South as a whole at about 53% of the
    national average against 118% for the Centre-North. Tagliacarne's 1954 study put Lombardy's or Valle
    d'Aosta's per-capita income at roughly **three times** that of Calabria or Lucania.
  - **1951–1971**: convergence; the Mezzogiorno rises from about 61% to 73% of national income, and
    Basilicata is named as **one of only three southern regions (with Abruzzo and Molise) to register
    significant progress**.
  - **1981 / 1991 / 2001 (Istat)**: Basilicata €8,917 / €11,971 / €15,514 per head against Calabria
    €8,131 / €11,437 / €13,955. **Basilicata above Calabria in every one.**
  - **2015–2024**: Calabria is consistently Italy's poorest region on GDP per head and on declared
    income; Basilicata sits at about 79.7% of the national average against Calabria's 57.0%.
  - The **1951–54 parliamentary inquiry into poverty** found poverty rates of **33.2% in Basilicata**
    against **37.7% in Calabria** — again Calabria worse.
  - Add the complication the readings never mention: since 1996 Basilicata has hosted **Europe's largest
    onshore oilfield** (F47/D30), and region and municipalities have taken over **€2 billion in
    royalties** in twenty years.
  - **The defensible sentence** is "one of the poorest regions in Italy", or "among the poorest, though
    Calabria was usually poorer". Not "the poorest".
- Source: Daniele & Malanima and Felice via Treccani, "Lo sviluppo economico delle regioni",
  <https://www.treccani.it/enciclopedia/lo-sviluppo-economico-delle-regioni-dalle-tre-italie-alle-due-italie_(L'Italia-e-le-sue-Regioni)/>;
  Istat regional accounts via CEIC series for Basilicata and Calabria; Camera dei deputati archive of the
  inquiry, <https://archivio.camera.it/inventari/profilo/commissione-sulla-miseria-italia-e-sui-mezzi-combatterla-1951-1954>

**F38. "Every Italian region claims cucina povera. Basilicata has the strongest claim of any of them."**
- Verdict: **UNSUPPORTABLE as a ranking; the underlying food history is documented and better**
- What is true: no scholarly source ranks regions by claim to *cucina povera*, and the concept itself is
  contested (as this project has already ruled, following Montanari and Helstosky — and note that
  Montanari's own line is the opposite of a poverty-as-virtue reading: "poverty generates culture —
  perhaps more than wealth does"). What can be said from sources:
  - Lucanian food consumption recorded in inquiries between the 19th and 20th centuries was
    **almost entirely vegetable**; meat, predominantly sheep, appeared **only at feasts**; only nobles
    and landowners ate meat, fish and cheese regularly.
  - The defining staples are cereals (durum wheat), legumes, vegetables, pork and sheep, with chilli,
    dried sweet pepper and horseradish as the seasonings; the culture is described in Italian sources as
    one of **reuse**, of meat and above all of bread.
  - **The primary source to point at is the 1951–54 parliamentary inquiry** (*Commissione parlamentare
    d'inchiesta sulla miseria in Italia e sui mezzi per combatterla*), which surveyed **58,000 families**
    on food consumption, clothing, housing type and overcrowding, treated **Basilicata as one of its nine
    study areas**, and devoted **two dedicated volumes to a single sample commune, Grassano (MT)** — the
    town Carlo Levi was first confined to. Published as *Atti della Commissione…*, Camera dei deputati,
    **14 volumes, 1953–54**, index volume 1958. Its finding: **11.8% of Italians, over six million
    people, in poverty**; 7.5% of families registering zero expenditure on the food categories measured.
  - A rewrite that wants the idea without the ranking should route it through this inquiry — named,
    dated, parliamentary — rather than through a superlative.
- Source: Camera dei deputati historical archive (above); inchieste portal,
  <https://inchieste.camera.it/miseria/home.html?leg=01>; Storia e Futuro on Gaetano Ambrico and the
  inquiry, <https://storiaefuturo.eu/gaetano-ambrico-linchiesta-parlamentare-miseria/>; it.wikipedia
  *Cucina lucana* for the consumption summary.

**F39. Recipe cross-check: *lagane e ceci* and Horace.**
- Verdict: **CONFIRMED**
- What is true: Horace, *Satires* I.6.115: **"inde domum me ad porri et ciceris refero laganique
  catinum"** — "then home to my bowl of leeks, chickpeas and *lagana*." Horace was born at **Venosa in
  65 BC**, which the recipe already says. Whether *laganum* was boiled or baked is a genuine scholarly
  question — B. L. Ullman devoted an article to it (*Classical Philology* 7:4, October 1912, "Horace
  Serm. I.6.115 and the History of the Word Laganum") — so the recipe's hedge is correct and can be
  attributed to Ullman.
- Source: The Latin Library, *Sermonum liber I*; Ullman via JSTOR, <https://www.jstor.org/stable/261474>

**F40. Recipe cross-check: the wines.**
- Verdict: **CONFIRMED**
- What is true: **Matera DOC** recognised in **2005** (types include Primitivo, Rosso, Greco, Moro,
  Bianco, Spumante metodo classico); **Grottino di Roccanova DOC** recognised by ministerial decree of
  **24 July 2009**. Both pairings in the recipes are real appellations.
- Source: Quattrocalici, Basilicata denominations,
  <https://www.quattrocalici.it/regione/basilicata/denominazioni/>; Lavinium,
  <https://www.lavinium.it/le-doc-della-basilicata-grottino-di-roccanova/>

**F41. Recipe cross-check: *baccalà* in a mountain region.**
- Verdict: **CONFIRMED in substance**
- What is true: salt cod and stockfish spread through inland Italy precisely because they kept, giving
  people far from the sea a fish for the Church's days of abstinence; **"baccalà alla lucana"**, salt cod
  with fried dried sweet peppers, is a documented Basilicata dish with its own entry. The recipe's
  framing is sound. Its claim that "a large share of the cod on Italian plates left Norway as klippfisk"
  is true of the modern trade and is the kind of sourcing note this course wants.
- Source: InformaCibo, <https://www.informacibo.it/baccala-stoccafisso-storia-ricette-regionali-italia/>;
  en.wikipedia *Baccalà alla lucana*.

**F42. "Strascinati con peperoni cruschi e mollica … the region's signature plate."**
- Verdict: **DEFENSIBLE, and it can be sourced**
- What is true: Italian food writing repeatedly calls the dish a declaration of Lucanian identity, and
  the pairing of strascinati with cruschi is described as the iconic one; the cruschi themselves are
  called by cooks **"il parmigiano dei poveri"**, crumbled over dishes for savour and colour — a second,
  independent version of the mollica idea worth noting. Common variants add grated pecorino or
  *cacioricotta*, which is what restaurants serve.
- Source: en.wikipedia *Pasta con i peperoni cruschi*; Gran Consiglio della Forchetta,
  <https://www.granconsigliodellaforchetta.it/cucina-tipica/strascinati-con-i-peperoni-cruschi/>;
  Viaggiando Italia, <https://www.viaggiando-italia.it/strascinati-in-puglia-e-basilicata-la-pasta-ruvida-che-racconta-il-sud/>

## Section 2 — Matera and the landmark reading

**D1. "The rock is calcarenite."**
- Verdict: **CONFIRMED — and the local misnomer is worth a sentence**
- What is true: the formation is the Pleistocene **Calcarenite di Gravina**, a poorly cemented,
  well-bedded, easily cut bioclastic limestone resting on the compact Cretaceous **Calcare di Altamura**;
  the Gravina torrent cut down first through the calcarenite and then into the limestone beneath. It is
  called **"tufo"** locally and commercially, which is a misnomer: it is neither volcanic tuff nor
  calcareous tufa. There is an entire article on the confusion in the local review *Mathera*.
- Source: Parco della Murgia Materana, geology, <https://www.parcomurgia.it/pagina.php?id=14>;
  Società Geologica Italiana, "Matera, (Geo)Capitale Europea della Cultura 2019",
  <https://www.socgeol.it/N1649/matera-geo-capitale-europea-della-cultura-2019.html>;
  Montemurro, "C'è tufo e tufo", *Mathera* 1, <https://www.rivistamathera.it/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/Mathera1-Montemurro-c-e-tufo-e-tufo.pdf>

**D2. "People have lived here since the Palaeolithic, which makes Matera one of the oldest continuously
inhabited places on earth."**
- Verdict: **First half CONFIRMED; second half UNSUPPORTABLE**
- What is true: Palaeolithic occupation is archaeologically attested — the **Grotta dei Pipistrelli** on
  the southern edge of the city is the major Upper Palaeolithic station, with Middle and Upper
  Palaeolithic tools; finds from the Murgia go back to the Lower–Middle Palaeolithic (Domenico Ridola's
  excavations). Neolithic **ditched villages** appear in the late 6th millennium BC — Serra d'Alto,
  Murgecchia, Murgia Timone, Tirlecchia — huts behind rock-cut ditches, with storage pits, ovens and
  cisterns, and later chamber tombs and tumuli over the same sites. The "one of the oldest continuously
  inhabited places on earth" / "third-oldest city in the world after Aleppo and Jericho" claim is
  **travel-press**, and the underlying ranking is acknowledged to be undefinable: it turns on what counts
  as a city and what counts as continuous. Say what the archaeology says instead.
- Source: Parco della Murgia Materana, preistoria, <https://www.parcomurgia.it/pagina.php?id=17>;
  WikiMatera, Matera nella preistoria; en.wikipedia *List of oldest continuously inhabited cities* (on
  the dispute).

**D3. "UNESCO listed the Sassi and the rock churches in 1993, calling them an exceptional record of how
people lived with a difficult landscape over nine millennia."**
- Verdict: **PARTLY RIGHT — the year is right, the "nine millennia" is not UNESCO's wording**
- What is true: inscribed **1993** (at the session held in Cartagena de Indias) as **"The Sassi and the
  Park of the Rupestrian Churches of Matera"**, property area **1,016 ha**, on criteria **(iii), (iv) and
  (v)**. UNESCO's own text says:
  > "The property was first occupied during the Palaeolithic period and shows evidence of continuous
  > human occupation through several millennia until the present day…"
  > Criterion (iii): "…an outstanding example of a rock-cut settlement, adapted perfectly to its
  > geomorphological setting and ecosystem and exhibiting continuity over **more than two millennia**."
  > Integrity: "…the highland plateau on the opposite side of the ravine which show evidence of human
  > settlement for **over 2000 years**."
  The "nine millennia" figure is Italian secondary usage (it.wikipedia says "oltre nove millenni"), not
  the inscription. Also quotable and better than the old paraphrase: UNESCO calls it "this remarkable and
  intact troglodyte settlement", containing **"more than a thousand dwellings and a large number of shops
  and workshops"**, and — directly relevant to the reading's arc — the Authenticity statement:
  > "There was some interruption when the entire population of the Sassi was relocated in the 1950s…
  > While the abandonment of the area led to some degradation, the return of people from the 1980s has
  > restored the traditional use and function of the property, and rejuvenated the spirit and feeling of
  > the place."
- Source: UNESCO World Heritage Centre, Statement of Outstanding Universal Value,
  <https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/670/> (retrieved in full).

**D4. "A house in the Sassi is a room cut horizontally into the rock, with a built façade added in front
of it."**
- Verdict: **CONFIRMED — and Carlo Levi describes exactly this**
- What is true: UNESCO: "The earliest houses in the settlement were simple caves enclosed by a wall of
  excavated blocks on the two *grabiglioni*, Sasso Caveoso and Sasso Barisano." Levi's 1945 text, in his
  sister's voice: "Sono grotte scavate nella parete di argilla indurita del burrone: ognuna di esse ha
  sul davanti una facciata; alcune sono anche belle, con qualche modesto ornato settecentesco… in quello
  stretto spazio tra le facciate e il declivio passano le strade, e sono insieme pavimenti per chi esce
  dalle abitazioni di sopra e tetti per quelle di sotto." The streets are the neighbours' roofs — a
  better sentence than the old text's.
- Source: UNESCO SOUV; Levi, *Cristo si è fermato a Eboli* (Einaudi 1945), the Matera passage.

**D5. "There is no natural water in the gorge, so every house had a cistern beneath it."**
- Verdict: **SUBSTANTIALLY RIGHT, but "every house" is an unsourced universal**
- What is true: the rainwater-harvesting system of channels, filters and rock-cut cisterns under the
  dwellings is the standard account and is what UNESCO's "harmoniously integrated into the natural
  terrain and ecosystem" rests on; the Palombaro Lungo was built by joining pre-existing caves and a
  spring-fed well. But no source I found states that every dwelling had its own cistern; it is repeated
  in guidebooks. Write it as "house after house had its own cistern" or attribute.
- Source: as D6.

**D6. "The Palombaro Lungo … is fifteen metres deep and was only rediscovered in the 1990s."**
- Verdict: **PARTLY RIGHT — the year is 1991; the depth figure is disputed**
- What is true: the great cistern lies under **Piazza Vittorio Veneto**; it holds **over five million
  litres**; it is about **fifty metres long**. Depth is given as **c. 14 m** (Matera Welcome), **c. 15 m**
  (most guides) and **c. 18 m** (it.wikipedia) — pick one and attribute, or say "some fifteen metres".
  It was formed by connecting pre-existing caves and a bell-shaped spring well from the 16th century
  onward, enlarged and completed in the **late 19th century** (1882 to a design by an engineer named Rosi
  in one account; 1893, under a Neapolitan architect, in another), waterproofed with **cocciopesto**
  against the porous calcarenite. It went out of use **in 1927** (or "in the 1930s") when the **Acquedotto
  Pugliese** reached the city. It was **brought to light in 1991 during the works to build Piazza Vittorio
  Veneto**; the man who explored and documented it was **Enzo Viti**, a technical draughtsman and student
  of underground Matera, who crossed the flooded cistern **in a rubber dinghy**.
- Source: it.wikipedia *Palombaro lungo*; Matera Welcome,
  <https://www.materawelcome.it/it/luogo/palombaro-lungo/>. **Both secondary; the conflicting figures are
  in the sources themselves.**

**D7. "Palombari."**
- Verdict: **UNVERIFIABLE as used**
- What is true: *palombaro* is the name of the cistern (and in Italian generally means a diver); I found
  no source calling the cistern-diggers *palombari*. The old reading does not in fact use the word, but
  the brief asked: if the rewrite wants a name for the men who cut the cisterns, it needs a source first.

**D8. "Houses opened onto a shared courtyard, the vicinato, with a communal oven and a well, and the
courtyard rather than the family was the social unit."**
- Verdict: **CONFIRMED for the courtyard; the sociological claim is an interpretation, and it has a
  named author**
- What is true: the *vicinato* is a documented settlement and social form — a cluster of dwellings facing
  a shared, bounded courtyard, often with a well (*cortile a pozzo*) — and it was studied precisely as a
  social unit by the 1950s surveys of **Tullio Tentori** and **Ludovico Quaroni's** circle, and by
  **De Rita**, when Matera became, as one source puts it, the European capital of anthropology,
  sociology and planning. A restored example, the **Vicinato a Pozzo** in Rione Malve, was opened as part
  of the Parco della Storia dell'Uomo in 2019. The communal oven is separately documented in the Pane di
  Matera disciplinare (F23). So the claim can be made and attributed to the 1950s surveys rather than
  floated.
- Source: Insula Europea, "Il Vicinato: un fenomeno antropologico e sociologico unico al mondo",
  <https://www.insulaeuropea.eu/2023/05/11/il-vicinato-un-fenomeno-antropologico-e-sociologico-unico-al-mondo/>;
  Comune di Matera / MuseiMatera on the Vicinato a Pozzo.

**D9. "More than a hundred rock churches, dug and frescoed between the eighth and thirteenth centuries."**
- Verdict: **CONFIRMED and conservative — the number is 155**
- What is true: **155 rock churches are recorded in the territory of Matera**; the Parco della Murgia
  Materana holds about **150**. The foundational survey is the **Circolo La Scaletta's census, published
  as *Le chiese rupestri di Matera* (De Luca, 1966)** — that is, the count exists because a group of

**D10. "…by Byzantine and Benedictine monks."**
- Verdict: **PARTLY RIGHT — this is the superseded reading of the evidence**
- What is true: the older scholarship worked from what is now called the **"tesi panmonastica"**, which
  treated every cave, crypt and hypogeum in southern Italy as the work of Greek (Basilian) monks fleeing
  iconoclasm, and read all its art as Byzantine mediation. That blanket attribution has been dismantled;
  the churches are now read as a mixture — Byzantine-influenced communities of immigrants from Cappadocia,
  Armenia, Syria and Asia Minor; **Benedictine and Lombard** foundations; and **small lay communities**
  who cut and used these spaces themselves, many of the caves later becoming dwellings or animal shelters.
  Architecturally the churches are hybrids: Latin plans with Byzantine detail, Greek plans with Latin
  liturgical arrangements. **Cosimo Damiano Fonseca** is the historian to name for the reappraisal and for
  the idea of a medieval "ideology of the cave". Write "monks and lay communities", not "monks".
- Source: Donato Giordano, "Chiese rupestri di Matera" (Academia),
  <https://www.academia.edu/15179206/Chiese_rupestri_di_Matera>; it.wikipedia *Chiese rupestri di Matera*.

**D11. "The frescoes in the Crypt of Original Sin … are ninth-century and were only cleaned in this

- Verdict: **CONFIRMED**

**D16. "The book was read across Italy and the Sassi became a national embarrassment."**

**D27. "The Pollino, Italy's largest national park, holds the loricate pines that grow nowhere else in

  royalties in twenty years**. There is a substantial and contested literature on the environmental and
  health effects and on whether the money has changed the regional economy.
- Source: en.wikipedia *Val d'Agri oil field*; EIB project page; ERICA project,
  <https://www.ericaproject.eu/basilicata-oil-fields-between-economy-environment-and-health-concerns/>

## Section 3 — Candidate openings

Each is a scene, with the source and what it opens onto.

**O1. Luisa Levi cannot find the city. (Matera, about eleven in the morning, late summer 1935.)**
A doctor from Turin gets off the train at Matera with a permit to have stamped and a guidebook that
promises a picturesque town with a museum and some curious troglodyte dwellings. She looks around and
there is no town: a bare plateau, eight or ten marble government palaces standing in the dust, some of
them unfinished. She walks until she reaches a street with houses on one side and a precipice on the
other. *In that precipice is Matera.*
— Source: Levi 1945, quoted verbatim at D15. Opens onto: the shape of the Sassi, the caves, and the
children who chase her down the mule track asking not for money but for quinine. The strongest opening
available, because the reversal is inside the scene: the city is there, and she is standing on its roofs.

**O2. The children ask for the wrong thing. (Same scene, lower down the gorge.)**
She thinks the crowd of children following her wants alms and stops and hands out her small change so
they can buy sweets. That is not what they want. What they are shouting, in a dialect she can't follow
until she stops, is *dammi 'u chinì* — give me the quinine. She goes into the museum to look at the Greek
vases, and when she comes out they are still there in the sun, waiting for her to bring it.
— Source: Levi 1945, verbatim at D15. Opens onto: malaria, the state of the Sassi, and what a book
published ten years later did to Italy.

**O3. Togliatti names it. (Matera, 1948.)**
The leader of the Italian Communist Party comes to Matera to see for himself how people are living, and
leaves with a phrase that will be attached to the town for forty years: *la vergogna nazionale*, the
national disgrace. Two years later the prime minister comes to look at the same caves.
— Source: WikiMatera; Gazzetta del Mezzogiorno (D16). Opens onto: the political sequence 1948 → De
Gasperi's visit of 23 July 1950 → Colombo's draft → Law 619 of 17 May 1952. Overturns the common
assumption that the phrase was De Gasperi's or Levi's.

**O4. A father of seven asks to be moved. (Sassi, 1951–55, in Tentori's field notes.)**
An anthropologist with a clipboard is going door to door through the Sassi for a commission funded by
American relief money and chaired by a German-American sociologist. A man with seven children tells him
his house is damp and too small for the nine of them, that they all sleep in one room, that there is no
lavatory and no window, and that it is a house *that should not be used even for animals*. He is not
resisting the evacuation. He is asking for it.
— Source: *Matera 55* via Museo Laboratorio (D19). Opens onto: the whole argument of whether the
clearance was a deportation — a prejudice overturned inside the first paragraph.

**O5. Enzo Viti crosses the square in a dinghy. (Matera, 1991, under Piazza Vittorio Veneto.)**
Workmen rebuilding the main square break into a void. A technical draughtsman who has spent years mapping
underground Matera goes down into it with a rubber dinghy and paddles fifty metres along a rock-cut hall
holding five million litres of water that nobody alive had seen.
— Source: it.wikipedia *Palombaro lungo*; Matera Welcome (D6). Opens onto: the cistern system, the
waterless gorge, and how a town with no river kept itself alive.

**O6. A farmer remembers a ghost. (Gravina di Picciano, 1 May 1963.)**
A man tells Raffaello de Ruggieri, a young lawyer in a Matera cultural circle, that as a boy he sheltered
from the weather in a cave and woke to find a figure staring at him with its eyes wide open. De Ruggieri
understands that what the boy saw was not a ghost but a fresco, and the circle goes looking along the
cliffs until it finds a ninth-century painted church that nobody had recorded.
— Source: WikiMatera and press accounts of the Circolo La Scaletta, founded 7 April 1959 by nineteen
young people including de Ruggieri, later mayor of Matera (D11, and the 1966 census at D9). Opens onto:
the rock churches, the 155 of them, and the fact that the count exists because a group of Materani went
and found them.

**O7. Pietro Turzio stays. (Craco, until 1995.)**
Thirty years after his town was ordered out, one man is still living in his palazzo on a crumbling ridge,
because the house the state assigned him is no use to his elderly father.
— Source: Il Post (D26). Opens onto: Craco, the landslides, and what "abandoned in 1963" actually means
when you follow it to the last man.

**O8. A geologist walks Craco on the last day of the year. (30 December 1963.)**
Walter Brugner spends a day on the ridge with the cracks still opening and writes the sentence nobody in
the town wants: this place will have to be moved.
— Source: Il Post (D26). Opens onto: the 800,000 years of uplift, the clays, the 400 mm of rain in three
days in November 1959, and the difference between an act of God and an act of drainage.

**O9. The women of Senise thread on the eleventh of August.**
In the pepper alley — *u strittul ru zafaran* — the oldest hands in the town are teaching anyone who turns
up how to thread pods on twine so that each one lies at 120 degrees to the last, because that is the angle
at which air gets between them and they dry instead of rotting.
— Source: the disciplinare's own specification of the spiral (art. 4) plus the festival, organised by the
ASSA association with the IGP consortium and the Museo Etnografico del Senisese
(<https://sagreautentiche.it/sagre/u-strittul-ru-zafaran-il-vicolo-del-peperone/>). Opens onto: why the
pepper is shaped the way it is, and why it can hang on a wall all winter.

**O10. A stamp is recut when a man dies.**
A shepherd on transhumance carves a piece of wood into a handle with a cockerel or a chain or a saint on
top, and on the end of the handle the initials of the man whose bread it will mark. When that man dies the
stamp is retired and a new one is cut with his son's letters. The same object, given to a woman by a
suitor, is kept if she means yes and handed back if she means no.
— Source: Turismo.it; the disciplinare's own art. 7 (F23). Opens onto: the communal oven, the once-a-week
bake, and the loaf that has to last until the next one. Living continuation: **Massimo Casiello**, who
left an IT job to carve bread stamps in a workshop on Via Ridola, a few doors from the museum that holds
the old ones (<https://www.massimocasiello.it/>).

**O11. The baker presses two letters into the loaf.**
Before it goes into the oven every loaf takes a stamp on its crown — not the family's initials any more
but **MT**, because that is what the protected designation requires.
— Source: Pane di Matera disciplinare art. 6 (F22). Opens onto: what the IGP kept and what it replaced;
a small, exact image for the whole argument about tradition written down.

**O12. A starter that begins with fruit.**
The rules for the bread of Matera tell you to take a kilo of durum semolina and 250 grams of the pulp of
ripe fresh fruit that has been steeping in water, and work them together, and wait ten hours at blood heat
for it to double.
— Source: disciplinare art. 6, verbatim (F17). Opens onto: the mother, the yeasts of the place, and why
the bread's legal definition begins with fruit rather than flour.

**O13. Rivetti tells the sculptor which way to look.**
A count flies over Rio, comes home to Maratea and commissions a Christ for the mountain above the town.
His instruction to Bruno Innocenti is precise and contrary: from far off it must seem to be facing the
sea; in fact it must face the land, and the church, and the people — because God became man for us.
— Source: it.wikipedia *Statua del Redentore*; IvyTour (D28). Opens onto: Maratea, the thirty kilometres
of Tyrrhenian coast, and a statue that is not made of what everyone says it is made of.

**O14. A tree that was already old when the churches were cut.**
On a rock face in the Pollino stands a pine whose first ring formed in AD 789 — dated in 2018 by counting
what was left of it and radiocarbon-matching cores taken from its exposed roots, because the inside of the
trunk was too far gone to read.
— Source: *Ecology*, 16 May 2018 (D27). Opens onto: the loricate pine, the park, and a scale against which
nine thousand years of Matera can be set honestly.

## Section 4 — Unguarded claims

Statements in readings 3 and 4 and in the recipes that are plausible and widely repeated but for which no
primary or institutional source was found. Attribute, hedge, or cut.

1. **"Infant mortality was around four times the national average."** (D13) No source. Every
   circulating number is either unsourced or incoherent.
2. **"Passed without opposition"** of Law 619/1952. (D17) No parliamentary record found.
3. **"About 15,000 people, roughly half the town."** (D12) Sources give 15,000 / 17,000 / 20,000 and
   half / two-thirds / 60%.
4. **"The book was read across Italy and the Sassi became a national embarrassment."** (D16) True in
   outline, but the causal chain runs through Togliatti (1948) and De Gasperi (1950); as written it
   credits the book with the whole outcome.
5. **"Byzantine and Benedictine monks"** cut the rock churches. (D10) The blanket monastic attribution
   is the discarded "panmonastic thesis".
6. **"The courtyard rather than the family was the social unit."** (D8) An interpretation from the 1950s
   surveys, not a fact; attribute to Tentori/De Rita.
7. **"Every house had a cistern beneath it."** (D5) Guidebook universal.
8. **"Cicero and Martial both mention Roman soldiers bringing it home from Lucania."** (F26) Wrong on
   both; the source is Varro.
9. **"The cornetto … three peaks for the Trinity."** (F24) Folklore, with a competing practical
   explanation; not in the disciplinare.
10. **"Mollica … replaced cheese, which cost money."** (F34) The nickname is attested, the economics are
    assumed. The Armento 976 origin story is a legend.
11. **"Eggs were sold rather than eaten"** (strascinati recipe headnote). Plausible, undocumented.
12. **"A few seconds too long and it is thrown away, which is why the frying is done by whoever in the
    house is trusted with it."** Charming, and the bitterness is real; the household custom is nobody's
    documented observation.
13. **"The chemistry at 170 °C — caramel and carbon a second apart."** (F10) The writer's inference. No
    food-science source.
14. **"Caciocavallo podolico … aged five or six years."** (F30) At the extreme end of a range.
15. **"Matera … one of the oldest continuously inhabited places on earth."** (D2) Undefinable and
    disputed.
16. **"Nine millennia"** attributed to UNESCO. (D3) UNESCO says "more than two millennia".
17. **"A marble Christ."** (D28) It is cement with Carrara marble chips.
18. **"Craco, 40 km south."** (D25) About 55 km, and inland rather than due south.
19. **"The town that was cleared as a disgrace now has a housing shortage."** (D23) The documented
    phenomenon is displacement and price, not shortage.
20. **"Pane di Matera … salt and water and nothing else"; "one to two kilos"; "nine days at two kilos";
    "baked in a wood oven".** (F15, F18, F20, F21) All four contradicted by the disciplinare, in the
    reading and in the recipe.
21. **"Hung outside in the shade to dry for weeks."** (F6) Reversed: shade for 2–3 days, then sun.
22. **"The locals call the powder zafarana."** (F8) *Zafarana* is the pepper.
23. **"Three shapes: pointed, hooked and blunt."** (F4) The third is *tronco*, truncated, "dog's nose".
24. **"Peperone di Senise IGP since 1996."** Correct — but note that many sources say 2016, and the
    disciplinare in force is the 2020 amendment (F1, F2).
25. **Craco founded by Greeks from Metaponto in 540 BC.** (D26) A weakly sourced en.wikipedia claim; the
    firm date is the name *Graculum* in a document of 1060.
26. **"Ferricelli … the name goes back to Greek colonisation."** (F35) Local claim, no evidence.
27. **"The only fish that could reach the mountains"** (baccalà recipe). Rhetorical; salt cod was one of
    several preserved foods, and the reason it travelled was the Church's fast days as much as geography.

## Section 5 — What the old text misses

Only items that reach a plate, a loaf, a cheese, or the landmark itself.

**Food**

1. **The sourdough mother is legally required to start with fruit.** 1 kg semolina, 250 g of the pulp of
   ripe fresh fruit steeped in water, ten to twelve hours at 26–30 °C. (F17) The single best fact in
   either disciplinare and completely absent from the reading and the recipe.
2. **Baker's yeast is in the rules**, 0.5–1 kg per 100 kg of semolina, alongside 20–30 kg of mother.
   (F15) The romance of "nothing else" is false, and correcting it is more interesting than repeating it.
3. **Twenty per cent, and four named wheats** — Cappelli, Duro Lucano, Capeiti, Appulo, grown in the
   province. (F16)
4. **The loaf scales: 500 g to 10 kg, seven days to fourteen.** (F18, F20) The keeping time is a function
   of the size, which is the actual mechanism the reading is reaching for.
5. **The mandatory MT stamp** (F22), the direct descendant of the family stamps — and the fact that the
   history of the communal ovens and the *capofamiglia* initials is written into the IGP's own
   justification (F23), with dates: eleven ovens in 1857, about fifteen in the 1950s, 130 bakers now.
6. **The IGP even scores the burnt smell**: burnt odour 3.0–4.5 on a sensory scale assessed to ISO
   13299. (F19)
7. **The Senise drying sequence** — three days in the dark, then threaded at 120°, then into the sun
   until 10–12% water. (F6, F7) Precise, physical, and the opposite of what the reading says.
8. **The powder is protected and the milling is prescribed** (F8) — *zafarana* is not a by-product but a
   named form of the IGP.
9. **The pepper is nearly extinct as a crop.** Two hundred hectares before the **Monte Cotugno dam**
   drowned the growing land; eight certified hectares in 2020; thirty-six in 2021; a national market for
   *cruschi* that those hectares cannot possibly supply; ICQRF listing it among the fifty most
   counterfeited Italian foods in 2020; the State anti-counterfeit seal issued to forty firms. (F13) A
   real and current story about a product the reading treats as timeless.
10. **Craco is inside the pepper zone.** (F5) The ghost town of reading 4 is one of the twelve communes
    of reading 3 — a free structural link between the two readings.
11. **Varro's actual sentence**, and Martial's actual two lines, and Apicius's recipe with bay, rue,
    pine nuts and smoke. (F26, F27)
12. **Lucanica di Picerno is a 1970s revival of the name**, with a protected recipe of wild fennel, black
    pepper and sweet or hot pepper, an 18-day minimum cure, and a U-shape that comes from the drying rack.
    (F29)
13. **Podolica numbers**: 1,100–1,900 litres a lactation, hand-milked beside the calf because the cows
    will not accept a machine, peak March to May. (F31) That is *why* the cheese is rare — better than
    saying it is rare.
14. **"Podolico" has no legal protection.** (F32) A reader in a shop should know it.
15. **Crapiata.** The Matera dish the landmark reading's *vicinato* actually produced: a soup of chickpeas,
    broad beans, cicerchie, peas, beans, lentils, wheat, maize and new potatoes, cooked on **1 August** to
    mark the end of the harvest, each woman of the *vicinato* bringing a handful of what she had. It is
    still made as a festival in **La Martella** — the village the evacuated families were moved to. Source:
    AIFB, <https://www.aifb.it/prodotti-tipici-italiani/legumi-crapiata-matera/>; Sapori dei Sassi. This is
    the one dish that ties the food reading and the landmark reading together at both ends.
16. **The 1951–54 parliamentary inquiry** as the primary source for what people actually ate, with
    **Grassano** — Levi's first place of confinement — as one of its sample communes, given two volumes of
    its own. (F38)

**Matera and the landmark**

17. **UNESCO's actual sentences** (D3), including the Authenticity paragraph on the 1950s removal and the
    return from the 1980s — which is the reading's own arc, stated by the listing itself.
18. **What Law 619 actually did**: sealed the emptied caves permanently shut at state expense, and gave an
    assignee one month to move into his new house or lose it. (D17)
19. **La Martella, Quaroni and Olivetti** (D20): the clearance produced one of the most studied
    experiments in post-war European housing, designed to reproduce the *vicinato* it had destroyed.
20. **Friedmann, Tentori and *Matera 55*** (D19): the argument that the inhabitants wanted to go, in their
    own recorded words.
21. **155 rock churches, counted by the Circolo La Scaletta in 1966** (D9) — and the 1963 discovery of the
    Crypt that came out of the same search (D11).
22. **Levi's own figure, twenty thousand** (D15), and the detail of trachoma and the flies.
23. **The count of who lives there now**: about 1,500–3,000 in the Sassi, against 15,000–20,000 then, with
    values up roughly tenfold since 1993. (D23)
24. **Italus, first ring AD 789** (D27) — a datable object older than every church in the gorge.
25. **The Christ's material and its instruction** (D28).
26. **Craco's last inhabitant, 1995** (D26).
27. **Val d'Agri** (D30): Europe's largest onshore oilfield and €2 bn of royalties sit under the region the
    reading calls the poorest in Italy. If the reading keeps any claim about poverty, this is the fact that
    complicates it honestly.
