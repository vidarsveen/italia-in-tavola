## 1. What the neighbouring regions have already spent

Swept from the seven reading files (`puglia`, `campania`, `molise`, `abruzzo`, `toscana`, `lazio`, and
`calabria` which is **not yet rewritten**). **Three of Basilicata's four readings have their old spine spent
by a neighbour.** In order of danger:

**1. Aglianico is Campania's grape and Campania spent it across two readings.** Campania 1 has a section
headed "Grapes with borrowed names" that already runs the whole of Basilicata 2's old argument: the Greek
derivation ("the name is not recorded at all before the fifteenth century, and it first appears in print, as
*aglianiche*, in 1520"), the DNA finding, the late ripening ("picked from the second half of October into
November, and it keeps acidity and tannin that a southern summer ought to have burned away") and the ageing
arc ("Young, it is black fruit behind a wall. After ten years it turns savoury and smoky, with sour cherry,
tobacco and iron"). Campania 2 then spends Taurasi in full, with the ageing rules, the 472 hectares, and a
tasting table whose palate line is "Full body, very high acidity, firm drying tannin, long savoury finish".

→ **Basilicata 2 cannot be built on the name dispute, the tannin-and-acidity arc, or the ageing curve.** All
three are Campania's, and the old text's "The name nobody can settle" and "What it does in the glass" are
almost a paraphrase of them. What is unspent and belongs to the Vulture alone: the **altitude and the
November harvest as a different mechanism from Irpinia's**, the **Superiore/Riserva rules as Basilicata's own
document**, and — if the research supports it — the live question of whether Vulture Aglianico is a distinct
biotype from Taurasi's. The comparison with Taurasi is now a *clause of contrast*, not a section. One further
gift: "Barolo of the south" appears in no other file, so the phrase is free, but the old text already calls
it "a marketing phrase", and Campania has the better claim to the argument.

**2. Campania 1 has already argued the opposite of Basilicata 1's volcanic-soil claim.** Its lead is "Everyone
assumes the volcano did that. It was the sand", and it states plainly what volcanic ground does and does not
do: "What the volcanic ground does do is drain fast and hold the day's heat. Growers will add that it is poor
and rich in potassium, and that this stresses the vine into small, concentrated berries." It then catches
Irpinia being called volcanic when its consortium describes calcareous clay. Lazio 1 makes the same move
("No soil study links that chemistry to the taste of the wine").

→ Basilicata 1's old sentence — "dark, porous and full of potassium, it drains fast, and it holds the heat of
the day into the night" — is now **a repeat of a sibling file, word for word in substance**, and
**quiz 1.2's scored answer is that sentence**. Reading 1 must say what is true of *this* volcano
specifically and attribute the growers' claims as claims, as Campania does. Repeating the usual
volcanic-soil story would also contradict a sibling reading, which is worse than repeating it.

**3. Puglia 3 has already written Matera's bread, under Altamura's name.** It carries the wood oven and the
crust ("That crust must be at least three millimetres thick"), the keeping ("built to outlast an absence …
the fortnight is the length of the absence and not a promise about the loaf; no shelf life is written down
anywhere"), the ban on home baking, **the communal oven and the loaf stamp**: "The baker pressed the initials
of the head of the household into each loaf with a wooden or iron stamp before it went in, because the oven
was shared and the dough was not."

→ Basilicata 3's old text has the same three facts in nearly the same words ("Families baked once a week in
the communal oven and stamped each loaf with a carved wooden or terracotta seal bearing the head of the
household's initials"). **The stamp and the shared oven cannot be told twice.** Matera's loaf needs what is
its own: the IGP's own wheat and shape rules, and whatever the research says distinguishes it. Note also that
Puglia states the keeping time is *not* documented for Altamura, while Basilicata's old text gives Matera
precise keeping times (7 days / 9 days) — if those are in Matera's disciplinare, that contrast is the honest
and interesting thing to write, and it must be stated as a difference between two documents, not as a
correction of Puglia.

**4. Horace is spent, and Puglia pointed at Basilicata while spending him.** Puglia 3 has a section headed
"Horace, and the town he could not name", which reads the town as "Venusia, **which was Horace's own
birthplace and is in Basilicata**".

→ Basilicata may keep Horace *born* at Venosa (the Puglia ledger reserved exactly that), but the Satires
journey, the bread joke and the metre joke are gone. One clause, and it must not re-tell the poem.

**5. The drove roads are exhausted across four files.** Abruzzo 3 and 4 and Molise 3 and 4 have between them
spent the tratturi and their 111.6 m width, the Dogana at Lucera and Foggia, the 1592 count of 4,471,496
sheep, the named shepherds in the Foggia registers, the cheesemaker who walked with the flock, and the
animal that could not finish the walk; Puglia 4 supplies the Tavoliere end; and **Lazio 3 has already
debunked the shepherd's-pantry story** ("The transhumance is real, but the pantry in the story is not in the
records").

→ Basilicata 3 may not reach lucanica or caciocavallo by way of transhumance. Neither may it explain the
region's food by a pantry carried on a road.

**6. Caciocavallo hung in pairs has been written three times** (Molise 3 in full, from the curd up, with the
*a cavallo* etymology and three rival ones; Puglia 3's key facts; Calabria's one clause), and **Molise 3 has
already drawn the kinship of the dragged pastas**: "They belong to the same dragged family as Puglia's
orecchiette and are built the same way." Puglia 3 owns orecchiette's technique outright, down to the
*sfèrre* and the thumb.

→ **Basilicata's caciocavallo must arrive through the Podolica cow, not through the hanging**, and Podolica
is genuinely unspent — no other file mentions the breed. **Strascinati may not be explained as a dragged
shape**; what is left to it is what it is dressed with. *Pasta filata* is already a glossary term.

**7. Craco is Rocca Calascio's shape, and Abruzzo owns both the emptying and the film crews.** Abruzzo 4:
"the people left for America and the northern cities, and by 1957 nobody lived up there at all", plus a
paragraph on *Ladyhawke*, *The American* and *The Name of the Rose*. Campania 2 owns the depopulating
earthquake (Irpinia 1980), Campania 1 the state-ordered evacuation (Vesuvius 1944, ten to twelve thousand
moved), Molise 4 the hill villages that "emptied slowly instead of being rebuilt".

→ **Craco gets a clause, not a section**, and the film-set list must not be repeated for it. Matera's own
film history is a different claim (a city hired to play Jerusalem) and survives, but it cannot be told with
Abruzzo's cadence. **The 1952 evacuation of the Sassi is still Basilicata's own** and is the strongest
unspent thing in the region: no other file has a state emptying a city by law.

**8. The poverty frame is spent twice over, in two different directions.** Toscana 4 owns *cucina povera* as
a proud name; **Puglia 3 owns the surplus-that-left frame** — "the region was never poor in what it grew …
What the kitchen had was what stayed behind after the ships were loaded" — and Abruzzo 3 and Molise 3 both
own "the household made it and gave it away".

→ Basilicata 3's old opening ("Every Italian region claims *cucina povera*. Basilicata has the strongest
claim of any of them") was spent before it was written, and the obvious replacement frame is spent too.
Basilicata needs a third road. The one asset no other region has is that **Basilicata's poverty was measured
by the state**, in the 1951–53 parliamentary inquiry and the post-war surveys, and that what those documents
record is a *diet*. A reading framed on what the inquiry found on the table is Basilicata's alone — pending
the research.

**9. Free ground, confirmed absent from all seven files.** *Peperoni cruschi*; **lucanica/luganega** and the
Roman-army etymology; **Carlo Levi** and the word *Mezzogiorno*; **Arbëreshë**; **Matera**, the **Sassi**,
cave houses, cave churches and **calcarenite**; **Podolica**; **mollica** (but see the caveat: the phrase
"breadcrumbs instead of cheese" is already a headed note in `content/recipes/puglia.js`, so the reading and
the Puglia recipe layer would echo); a **Christ or saint statue above a coast**; "Barolo of the south".

**10. Calabria is next after Basilicata and is not yet rewritten.** It currently owns the rare-tree line
("the giants of the Sila"), the Sila and Aspromonte parks, and the chilli. **Basilicata is first to the
Pollino in the rewrite order, so it may define the loricate pine** — and this ledger is the notice to
Calabria's pass that it must then defer. Calabria's Santa Maria dell'Isola already occupies the
"photogenic thing on its own rock" slot that Maratea's Christ would fill, so Maratea stays a clause.

### 1a. The template risk, which is larger than any single collision

Six of the eight Puglia and Campania readings open on a **concrete, dated, slightly oblique anecdote** whose
relevance lands a sentence or two later (empty beer bottles collected in Brindisi in 1943; a McDonald's
opening in Altamura in 2001; a woman setting a stone alight in 1866; a pizzeria that failed; growers meeting
at Bacoli in January 2025; a count bringing forty families over from Noci). Seven of the eight close on a
**short flat aphoristic reversal** ("They make them behind the door."; "It wrote both into the rules.";
"…for a reason that has nothing to do with the mountain."; "the register goes on naming trees one at a
time.").

→ This is now a house style, and Basilicata would be the ninth and tenth instance of it. **At least two of
the four readings must open by some other device and close by some other device** — and the closing is the
more urgent of the two, because the flat one-line reversal is the easier habit and it has run seven times.
Candidates for a different close: ending inside a scene rather than on a verdict; ending on a number that
has been earned; ending on something still unfinished. This is logged so the reader reviewers can be told to
listen for it, and it goes in every reviewer packet.

**Barred for Basilicata 1–4** (carried forward, plus the Puglia lessons):

- a named producer, writer or agronomist introduced with a date (Lazio 2, Abruzzo 1, Molise 2, Campania 2);
- an archaeological feature or structure described in the present tense (Lazio 4, Abruzzo 4, Molise 4) — which
  rules out the obvious **Sassi** and **Palombaro Lungo** openings for reading 4, and the obvious **Barile
  cellar** opening for reading 1;
- a hypothetical person or animal (Abruzzo 2, Abruzzo 3);
- a fronted prepositional place phrase before the subject (Lazio 2, Lazio 3, Abruzzo 1);
- a lead ending on a withheld name (three in a row in Molise);
- a bare quotation as the first thing heard (Umbria: a listener cannot hear quotation marks) — which matters
  here, because the obvious reading-4 lead is the Carlo Levi passage;
- **agentless passives with a plural subject being moved or gathered, the agent arriving two sentences later**
  (Puglia 2 and Puglia 4 were heard as one shape despite differing in subject and arc);
- more than **one** of the four leads ending on a correction or a debunk, and none in reading 4;
- two consecutive leads fronting a time phrase (Umbria 4 / Marche 1).

Added by the sweep, and binding:

- **something being counted** is now spent (Puglia 1 opens on a count and closes on the counting continuing);
- **a business or institution opening on a date** is spent (Puglia 3);
- **an oblique dated anecdote whose relevance lands a sentence or two later** is the house opening in six of
  the last eight readings (§1a) — at least two of Basilicata's four must not use it;
- **a short flat aphoristic reversal as the last line** has run seven times in eight (§1a). This is the
  tightest constraint in the region: at most one of the four may close that way, and the ledger names which
  before drafting.

**Still unspent, to be allocated one per reading before drafting:** a measurement being taken; a price or a
transaction; a refusal — someone declining to do what they were told; a prejudice overturned inside the
sentence; a crowd doing one thing. Reading 4 has a strong claim on **a refusal** (the Sassi evacuation was
resisted) and reading 3 on **a measurement** (the inquiry recording what was on the table), pending the
research.

## 3. Map and scene facts, stated up front

(A fact reviewer flags true-but-unsourced geography, so these go in the ledger before the drafts. Figures
marked ⚑ are for the research to confirm or correct.)

- Basilicata is the **instep** of the boot, between Campania to the west, Puglia to the north and east, and
  Calabria to the south. It is the only mainland southern region with a coast on **both** seas: a short
  Tyrrhenian strip at **Maratea** ⚑ (about 30 km) and a longer Ionian shore along the **Metapontino** ⚑.
- **Two provinces**: **Potenza** (the regional capital, in the west) and **Matera** (in the east). Population
  about ⚑ 535,000 and falling; among the least populous Italian regions. The old text's "half a million
  people, two provinces and eight per cent flat land" needs ISTAT's own altimetric split ⚑ (mountain / hill /
  plain), because the region is usually given as roughly half mountain and most of the rest hill.
- The old name is **Lucania**, which is what the sausage is named after and what Carlo Levi's book calls it.
  Both names are in play and the reading should not treat them as interchangeable without saying so ⚑.
- **North**: the **Vulture–Melfese**, an isolated volcano east of the Apennine chain with **Melfi**,
  **Rionero in Vulture**, **Barile**, **Rapolla**, **Ginestra**, **Maschito** and **Venosa** around it. The
  Arbëreshë villages are here (Barile, Ginestra, Maschito ⚑) and two more in the far south (San Costantino
  Albanese, San Paolo Albanese ⚑). Venosa is on the eastern edge, towards Puglia.
- **Centre**: **Potenza** (the highest regional capital in Italy ⚑), the **Lucanian Dolomites** at
  Castelmezzano and Pietrapertosa, and the upper valleys of the rivers that run south-east to the Ionian —
  **Bradano**, **Basento**, **Cavone**, **Agri**, **Sinni**. The **Val d'Agri** holds the largest onshore
  oilfield in western Europe ⚑, which is the one fact about modern Basilicata that most contradicts the
  region's reputation, and which must not be forced into a food paragraph if it does not reach one.
- **East**: **Matera**, on the edge of the **Murgia** limestone plateau above the **Gravina** torrent — the
  same rock as Puglia's Murge, which is a real connection and a collision risk, since **Puglia 3 owns
  Altamura**, twenty-odd kilometres away across the provincial line ⚑.
- **South**: **Senise** on the Sinni above the Monte Cotugno reservoir ⚑; the **Pollino** massif on the
  Calabrian border, with Basilicata's highest ground ⚑ (Serra Dolcedorme, about 2,267 m); **Craco** and the
  **calanchi** badlands in the Cavone–Bradano country; **Aliano** and **Grassano**, where Levi was exiled ⚑.
- **Ionian coast**: **Metaponto** and **Heraclea** (Policoro) were Greek colonies ⚑, which is where Magna
  Graecia touches Basilicata — and Calabria 1, not yet rewritten, is titled "Magna Graecia and the oldest
  vineyard", so that material is **Calabria's** and Basilicata may take a clause at most.
- Directions that must not be got backwards: Vulture is in the **north**, Matera in the **east**, Senise and
  the Pollino in the **south**, Maratea on the **south-west** (Tyrrhenian) coast, Metaponto on the
  **south-east** (Ionian).

### Reading 1 — "Vulture: wine on a volcano"

**Spine.** The mountain's last act built everything the reading needs, and the old text never connects
them: the final eruptions dug the two lakes, the rock they threw out is soft enough to cut a cellar into,
and the law now draws the vineyard between two altitudes on its flanks. **Not** a volcanic-soil reading —
Campania owns that argument and has already corrected it (§1.2).

**Lead** (wine O6). The first Albanians reach Barile about 1477 after Scutari falls; more come in 1534 from
Corone, and in 1597, 1664, 1675 (W28). They cut shelters into the soft tuff of the slope and call the place
the **Sheshë** (W27). The shelters became cellars; there are **131** of them (W27); the town still speaks
Arbëresh while Rionero has lost it (W29).

**Sections.**
1. *The cellars and who dug them.* The Sheshë, 131 cellars, constant cellar temperature without help
   (W27). Three of the fifteen wine communes — Barile, Ginestra, Maschito — are Arbëreshë foundations
   protected under Law 482/1999 and Regional Law 40/1998 (W29, 4.12). **Pasolini filmed the Nativity of
   *Il Vangelo secondo Matteo* in these cellars in 1964** (W50) — one sentence, and it stays in reading 1,
   because reading 4 has its own film history and must not repeat him (D24).
2. *What the mountain actually did.* The last activity was the phreatomagmatic pair that dug the
   Monticchio maars, **132 ± 12 ka and 141 ± 11 ka** (W2) — **not** "tens of thousands of years", and
   **English Wikipedia's 40 ka is wrong by a factor of three** (W2). The lakes *are* the craters (W5, 4.1),
   maars rather than a caldera, with a beech wood at only ~650 m. **Extinct is the correct official word**,
   and the reading should say why: the Protezione Civile's 10,000-year line, which Vulture is on the far
   side of by a factor of thirteen (W3) — and unlike the Alban Hills, which this course got wrong in Lazio.
   The mountain still outgasses mantle CO₂ (W4), so "finished" is the wrong word even though "extinct" is
   the right one.
3. *The lake that took notes.* A hundred metres of sediment in Lago Grande, ~133,000 years, **more than
   340 tephra layers** from volcanoes up to 540 km away, the reference archive by which Italian eruptions
   are dated (W6, 4.2). The crater beside the vineyard is how science knows when Vesuvius went off.
4. *Where the vines are allowed to be.* The disciplinare requires **200–700 m** (W10) — a rule, not an
   observation. Fifteen communes (W20). Latitude: the Vulture sits a few minutes **north** of Naples, not
   on its latitude (W11) — the old text used "the latitude of Naples" to argue it should be too hot.
5. *The other business.* Two million bottles a day at Melfi, fourth in Italy for sparkling mineral water,
   a regional billion litres (W7, 4.15). Same volcano, and the water pays better.

**Closing beat.** Back inside the Sheshë: the cellars are still where the town keeps its wine, dug by people
who arrived with nothing and found the rock would cut.

**Moved to the Key facts box** (and the box must then be grepped for each — the Puglia lesson, §1): DOC
**DPR 18 February 1971**, GU 129 of 22 May 1971 (W14); Basilicata's **only DOC for thirty-two years**, until
Terre dell'Alta Val d'Agri in 2003 (W15, 4.16); 100% Aglianico, the rules naming two registered varieties
(W16); DOC minimum 12.5% at consumption and release **from 1 September of the year after the harvest** —
about eleven months, not "a year" (W17); ISTAT's altimetric triple, **46.8% mountain, 45.1% hill, 8.0%
plain** (W12); population 522,952 (W12); coasts ~30 km Tyrrhenian and ~35–40 km Ionian (W13, D29); Monte
Vulture 1,326 m (W1).

**Cut, with reasons.** "A cone of basalt" — the magmas are melilititic–carbonatitic; write "lava, tuff and
ash" (W1). **"Full of potassium"** — no soil analysis of Vulture vineyards exists (W8). **"It drains fast
and holds the heat of the day into the night"** — unsourced *and* it contradicts the cold-nights paragraph
that follows it (W9); the drainage survives, the heat-holding goes. "It is the same instinct that produced
the cave city at the other end of the region" — no source links Barile's cellars to the Sassi, and they are
100 km apart in different rock (W17 unguarded); keep only as an explicit simile or cut. "Never roofed" of
the Incompiuta → "never finished" (W31).

### Reading 2 — "Aglianico, the Barolo of the south"

**Spine.** Not the name dispute and not the tannin arc — Campania has spent both (§1.1). This reading is
about **a grape that is picked last and why**, and about **a wine the law forces to wait**. The mechanism
the old text never gives: sugar is ripe in September and **phenolic** ripeness arrives weeks later, and
that gap is the whole explanation of both the November harvest and the wine's architecture (4.8).

**Lead** (wine O10). Most of Italy has finished picking by the end of September, and 2026 was the earliest
vintage on record, ten to fifteen days ahead and three weeks in places (W25). On the Vulture they have not
started. Aglianico is not picked before the second half of October and often not before November (W25).
*Caution:* "later than almost anywhere else" has no ranking behind it (W25, unguarded 4) — write the
contrast, not the superlative.

**Sections.**
1. *Why it waits.* Small berries, thick waxy skins exceptionally rich in anthocyanins and condensed
   tannins; high sugar with high acidity retained; the wait is for phenolic ripeness (W38, 4.8). State the
   diurnal mechanism as general viticulture and **invent no numbers** — there are no measured figures for
   the Vulture (W26).
2. *The clock the law starts.* The DOCG's three years run **from 1 November of the harvest year**, twelve
   months in wood and twelve in bottle; the Riserva needs **twenty-four months in wood** and is released
   from 1 November of the fifth year (W19, 4.4). So a Superiore is in its fourth calendar year before it
   can be sold. **DOCG 2010** (DM 2 August 2010) — and **do not let a reviewer "fix" it to 2011**, which is
   a later modification and the date English Wikipedia gives (W18).
3. *Seventy named vineyards.* The DOCG carries **70 menzioni geografiche aggiuntive**, a cru map inside the
   rule book, from Accovatura to Vizzarro (W21, 4.5). One of them, **Notarchirico**, is also a Lower
   Palaeolithic site at Venosa dated by Vulture's own ash (W21, 4.6).
4. *One grape or two.* For forty-seven years the national register carried **two entries — 002 Aglianico
   and 266 Aglianico del Vulture** — until **DM 30 May 2018** (GU 133, 11 June 2018) recorded each as a
   synonym of the other (W35, 4.9). Three biotypes, one variety, one descent (W34). This replaces the old
   text's name-origin section as the reading's identity material, because Campania owns the etymology.
   The etymology itself gets **one hedged clause**: three live derivations, first printed appearance of the
   name in **1520**, and **the DNA question is open, not closed** — the largest recent study (De Lorenzis
   et al. 2019) finds a robust link between South Italian and Greek genotypes, the opposite of what the old
   text asserts (W34). **This is one of the region's two permitted staged disputes** (§6).
5. *The rest of Basilicata's wine* — replacing the old "Rosé, sparkling and the everyday bottle", which
   describes a wine that does not exist. **There is no rosato in the DOC** (W22, W40): the DOC has exactly
   two types, red and **spumante** (traditional method, ≥9 months); a pink Aglianico is made but sold as
   **Basilicata IGT**. Then the two appellations that currently appear nowhere in the course's prose
   (§4a): **Matera DOC (2005)** and **Grottino di Roccanova DOC (24 July 2009)** (F40). This section fixes
   the named-in-spoken-prose gap and a factual error at the same time.

**Closing beat — the one permitted flat reversal.** Through the first half of the twentieth century the
wine left in **four- and five-quintal barrels on carts** for the railway station, bound for **Piedmont**,
where it was used to make other people's wine taste better (W44, 4.11) — the region whose wine it is now
compared to.

**Moved to the box.** Taurasi DOC 1970 / DOCG 1993 and Aglianico del Taburno DOC 1986 / DOCG 2011, with the
contrast that both allow 15% of other grapes while **Aglianico del Vulture requires 100%** (W37); Italian
plantings ~9,000–10,000 ha, Campania 79% and Basilicata 12% (W36); production about 2.5 million bottles a
year of which the DOCG is roughly 150,000 (W23); the Consorzio, constituted 12 April 1986 (W24).

**Cut, with reasons.** "Everybody agrees" → attribute the ranking to **Ian D'Agata**, who reports the
consensus and then dissents upward (W32). "DNA shows little link to Greek varieties" → rewritten as a live
argument (W34). "Drink from six years, the best keep twenty" traces to **Sotheby's** via Wikipedia — a
merchant's estimate, not a fact (W39); if the tasting table keeps it, the **tasting card must change with
it in both languages, character for character** (§4, the Abruzzo lesson). "The bottle that actually appears
on tables in Potenza and Matera" — unsourced, and **Matera is not in the Vulture**, it is 100 km away in
the other province with its own DOC (W41). "It appears in Basilicata and Campania and nowhere much else" —
contradicted (W36). "The Superiore rules exist to stop the wine reaching the market before it is drinkable"
— an unsourced reading of legislative intent; replace with the 1 November clock, which is the fact (W19).
"The comparison to Barolo is a marketing phrase" — origin unattributable (W52); the old text's hedge is the
right instinct, keep it hedged and do not attribute it to anyone.

### Reading 3 — "Peperoni cruschi and the bread of Matera"

**Spine.** Not *cucina povera* — Toscana owns it as a proud name, Puglia owns the surplus-that-left frame,
and Basilicata's old opening sentence was spent before it was written (§1.8). This reading is about **a
kitchen built on drying and keeping, which the state has since written down** — and the route to the
poverty, where it is needed, is the **1951–54 parliamentary inquiry**, which surveyed 58,000 families,
treated Basilicata as one of nine study areas and gave **Grassano — Levi's first place of confinement —
two volumes of its own** (F38). That is primary, dated, parliamentary, and no other region has used it.
**"The poorest region in Italy" is wrong**: Calabria is lower in every series that can be dated, and the
inquiry itself found 33.2% poverty in Basilicata against 37.7% in Calabria (F37). Write "among the
poorest".

**Lead** (food O9). In the pepper alley at Senise — *u strittul ru zafaran* — pods are threaded on twine so
that each lies at **120° to the last**, because that is the angle at which air gets between them and they
dry instead of rotting (F6, F7, and the festival).

**Sections.**
1. *The drying, in the right order.* The old text has it **backwards** (F6): the pods go first onto cloths
   or nets in **dry, aired rooms away from the light for two or three days**, and only then are they
   threaded and **put in the sun** until water reaches 10–12%. Harvest from the **first ten days of
   August**, by hand, repeatedly, because the crop ripens in stages. Serte 1.5–2.0 m (F7). The variety is
   selected for a thin pericarp, 13–15% dry matter (F3, F11). Three types — **Appuntito**, **Uncino** and
   **Tronco**, which is *truncated*, "dog's nose", not "blunt" (F4). *Zafarana* is the pepper, not the
   powder, and it is a real Arabic loan from *zaʿfarān*, saffron — the colour transferred, not the flavour
   (F8). The **powder is inside the rules**, with the oven treatment and the milling prescribed (F8).
2. *The frying.* 170–180 °C for two or three seconds (F10). The mechanism that is actually supported is
   physical: residual water flashes to steam and sets the thin pericarp into a brittle sheet, and the pod
   finishes crisping **as it cools, not in the oil** (F10). The bitterness is real; **the household custom
   of one trusted fryer is nobody's documented observation** (unguarded 12) — attribute or cut.
3. *A crop that nearly went.* Up to **200 ha** around 1990; the **Monte Cotugno dam** drowned much of the
   growing land; **8 certified hectares in 2020**, **36 in 2021**; named by ICQRF among the **fifty most
   counterfeited Italian foods** in 2020; a State anti-counterfeiting seal issued to its **forty** firms
   (F13). The honest formulation is arithmetic, not accusation: a few dozen hectares cannot supply a
   national snack.
4. *The bread.* Four claims in the old text and in the recipe are **wrong against the disciplinare**:
   it is **not** "sourdough, salt, water and nothing else" — **0.5–1 kg of compressed baker's yeast** per
   100 kg of semolina is in the rules alongside 20–30 kg of mother (F15); weights are **500 g to 10 kg**,
   not 1–2 kg (F18); keeping is **7 days at 1 kg and 14 at 10 kg**, and **no nine-day figure exists**
   (F20); a **wood oven is optional** — wood *or* indirect heat, with wood earning an extra line on the
   label (F21). What replaces the romance is better: **the mother must be started with 250 g of the pulp
   of ripe fresh fruit** steeped in water, worked into a kilo of semolina and held at 26–30 °C (F17). At
   least **20% of the semolina** must come from local ecotypes and old varieties — **Cappelli, Duro
   Lucano, Capeiti, Appulo** (F16). Crust ≥3 mm, and the IGP **scores the burnt smell** 3.0–4.5 on a
   sensory scale assessed to ISO 13299 (F19).
5. *The stamp, which is the one thing Puglia cannot have twice.* Per §1.3, the communal oven and the
   family initials are Altamura's. Basilicata's unspent version is that the practice **survives as law and
   has changed hands**: every loaf must now be stamped **MT** before it goes in (F22), where the women once
   knew their own by the initials of their *capofamiglia* — a story the IGP writes into its own
   justification, with dates (eleven ovens in 1857, about fifteen in the mid-1950s, 130 bakers now), and
   with stamps **carved by shepherds on transhumance**, retired and recut when the eldest man died, and
   given by a suitor to a woman who kept it if she meant yes (F23). Two or three sentences, and the
   comparison with Altamura stated as two documents differing, not as a correction of Puglia.
6. *Pork, cheese and the two dishes the sheet promises.* **Varro, not Cicero and Martial** (F26): *De
   lingua Latina* V.111 — the soldiers learned it from the Lucanians — is the whole basis of the story, and
   Martial XIII.35 is a two-line gift-tag naming a Picene sow. **Apicius II.4** gives an actual recipe,
   with bay, rue, pine nuts and smoke (F27). The cognates are genuine (F28). **Lucanica di Picerno IGP**
   (Reg. UE 2018/1615) is a **1970s revival of an ancient name**, not an unbroken line (F29). Caciocavallo
   podolico arrives **through the cow, not the hanging** (§1.6): 1,100–1,900 litres a lactation, about five
   a day, **hand-milked beside the calf because the cows will not take a machine**, peak March to May
   (F31) — which is *why* it is rare. **"Podolico" has no DOP or IGP**, only a Slow Food presidium (F32),
   which a reader in a shop should know. **Lagane e ceci** is named here, through **Horace, *Satires*
   I.6.115** — *porri et ciceris … laganique catinum* — and Horace was born at Venosa, which reading 1 has
   already established (F39); whether *laganum* was boiled or baked is a real scholarly question and can
   be attributed to **Ullman (1912)**. **Baccalà alla lucana** is named, salt cod with fried dried peppers
   (F41). Both currently exist only as chips and recipes (§4a).
7. *Mollica.* The nickname *il formaggio dei poveri* is attested across the Mezzogiorno (F34); **the
   economic explanation is lore, not documented history**, and the Armento-976 origin story is a legend
   with no source. Attribute or drop — and note **quiz 3.3's scored answer is that explanation**.

**Closing beat.** **Crapiata**: chickpeas, broad beans, cicerchie, peas, beans, lentils, wheat and new
potatoes, cooked on **1 August** to end the harvest, each woman of the *vicinato* bringing a handful of what
she had — and still cooked as a festival in **La Martella**, the village the evacuated families were moved
to (Section 5 item 15). It hands the reader to reading 4 with the *vicinato* already in their ear.

**Free structural link, to be used once:** **Craco, the ghost town of reading 4, is one of the twelve
communes of the pepper zone** (F5).

### Reading 4 — "Matera: the cave city"

**Spine.** A city emptied by law and then filled again — the strongest unspent material in the region,
because no other reading in the course has a state emptying a city (§1.7). The correction that reshapes it:
**the book did not do it on its own.** The order is **Levi 1945 → Togliatti's phrase in 1948 → De Gasperi's
visit of 23 July 1950 → Colombo's draft → Law 619 of 17 May 1952** (D16), and **"la vergogna nazionale" is
Togliatti's**, not Levi's and not De Gasperi's. **This is the region's second permitted staged dispute**
(§6); everything else is stated in its corrected form without performing the correction.

**Lead** (food O1). A doctor from Turin gets off at Matera with a permit to have stamped and a guidebook
promising a picturesque town with a museum and some curious troglodyte dwellings. There is no town — a bare
plateau and a few government palaces in the dust. She walks to a street with houses on one side and a
precipice on the other, and the city is in the precipice. Luisa Levi, Carlo's sister, **a doctor** (D15).
Do not front the date; open on the looking.

**Sections.**
1. *What she saw.* Levi's own sentences are far better than the old paraphrase and should be quoted
   sparingly rather than summarised (D15): the two inverted funnels, the shape "with which, at school, we
   imagined Dante's hell", descending *di girone in girone*; the streets that are floors for the houses
   above and roofs for those below (D4); dogs, sheep, goats and pigs on the floor, the whole family in one
   cave — **"Così vivono ventimila persone"**, Levi's own figure of twenty thousand; the children with
   trachoma and the flies; and the crowd shouting not for money but **«dammi 'u chinì»**, give me the
   quinine. **Never open on the quotation** (Umbria) — the lead is the arrival, the quotation lands here.
2. *How the city works.* The rock is the Pleistocene **Calcarenite di Gravina**, easily cut, resting on the
   compact Calcare di Altamura beneath (D1) — and it is called *tufo* locally, which is a misnomer: neither
   volcanic tuff nor calcareous tufa. **Palombaro Lungo**: under Piazza Vittorio Veneto, fifty metres long,
   **over five million litres**, some fifteen metres deep (sources differ, so attribute), waterproofed with
   *cocciopesto*, out of use in 1927 when the Acquedotto Pugliese arrived, and **brought to light in 1991**
   during the works on the square, crossed by **Enzo Viti in a rubber dinghy** (D6). "Every house had a
   cistern" is a guidebook universal — write "house after house" (D5). The *vicinato* is real and was
   studied as a social unit by **Tentori** and Quaroni's circle in the 1950s; the claim that the courtyard
   rather than the family was the social unit is **their interpretation** and must be attributed (D8).
3. *The churches.* **155** rock churches in the territory, about 150 in the park (D9) — and the count
   exists because the **Circolo La Scaletta**, nineteen young Materani, went and found them and published
   the census in 1966. **Not "Byzantine and Benedictine monks"**: that blanket attribution is the discarded
   *tesi panmonastica*, and the churches are now read as a mixture of immigrant communities, Benedictine
   and Lombard foundations and **small lay communities** who cut and used them themselves (D10). The
   **Cripta del Peccato Originale**, 8th–9th century, by the "Pittore dei Fiori di Matera", found abandoned
   on **1 May 1963** and reopened in 2005 (D11).
4. *Emptied.* **Law 619 of 17 May 1952**, seventeen articles (D17), and what it actually did: paid for the
   **permanent sealing-up of the condemned dwellings** — "locked" is not a metaphor — and gave an assignee
   **one month** to move in or lose the house. **"Passed without opposition" has no parliamentary record
   and must go** (D17). The population figure is a range, not a fact: **fifteen to seventeen thousand,
   between half and two-thirds of the town** (D12). **The "four times the national average" infant
   mortality has no source at all** and every circulating number is incoherent (D13) — replace with what is
   supported: Matera recorded in 1949–50 as the Italian city with the highest infant mortality, and Levi's
   eyewitness trachoma.
5. *The surprise in the middle* (rule 14), and the reason food O4 is not the lead. Tentori's field notes
   record people **asking to be moved**: a father of seven whose house is damp and too small for nine, who
   all sleep in one room, with no lavatory and no window, a house that **"should not be used even for
   animals"** (D19). The museum of peasant civilisation argues the clearance was a deliverance, on the
   commission's own material in ***Matera 55***. The reading must not present the evacuation only as
   something done *to* people.
6. *Where they went.* **Borgo La Martella**, 1951–54, promoted by **Adriano Olivetti** through UNRRA-Casas
   on **Friedrich Friedmann's** social study, designed by **Ludovico Quaroni**'s team — a neighbourhood
   plan that deliberately tried to reproduce the *vicinato* it had replaced (D20). And it is where crapiata
   is still cooked (reading 3's closing beat).
7. *Filled again.* **Law 771 of 11 November 1986**, 100 billion lire, the Sassi State-owned and leased to
   the comune, which grants **sub-concessions** to those who undertake to restore (D21) — the mechanism the
   old text only gestures at. **UNESCO 1993**, criteria (iii)(iv)(v), 1,016 ha — and **"nine millennia" is
   not UNESCO's wording**: the inscription says **"more than two millennia"** (D3). Quote the Authenticity
   paragraph, which states the reading's own arc: the interruption of the 1950s relocation, and the return
   from the 1980s restoring "the traditional use and function of the property". European Capital of Culture
   **2019**, with 388,158 arrivals and tourist spending of €121.3 m (D22).
8. *The rest of the region*, kept short. **Craco**: about **55 km** from Matera and **inland**, not "40 km
   south" (D25); the chronology is 1963 landslide and evacuation, 1968 decree, 1972 flood, 1980 final
   order, and **Pietro Turzio, the last inhabitant, left in 1995** (D26). The **Pollino** is genuinely
   Italy's largest national park at 192,565 ha (D27), and Basilicata is first to it in the rewrite order,
   so it may define the **loricate pine** — with **Italus**, dated in 2018, first ring **AD 789**, the
   oldest scientifically dated tree in Europe. **Maratea**: 21.13 m, **not marble but cement with Carrara
   marble chips**, by Bruno Innocenti, 1963–65, and the old text's "which the town will explain to you at
   length" becomes the explanation, because **Rivetti's instruction survives**: from far off it should seem
   to face the sea, but in fact it faces the land and the church, "perché Dio si è fatto uomo per noi"
   (D28).

**Closing beat — a number that has been earned, not an aphorism.** The Sassi held fifteen to twenty thousand
people; they now hold somewhere between 1,500 and 3,000, with values up roughly tenfold since the
inscription, and residents who came back in the 1990s leaving again (D23). **"Housing shortage" mis-frames
it**; the supported sentence is that people are priced out of the houses their grandparents were moved out
of.

**Do not use**: "one of the oldest continuously inhabited places on earth" — undefinable and travel-press
(D2); write what the archaeology says instead — the Grotta dei Pipistrelli, and Neolithic ditched villages
from the late 6th millennium BC.

**Val d'Agri** (D30) — Europe's largest onshore oilfield, in production since 1996, over €2 bn in royalties
in twenty years — goes in the **Key facts box, not the prose**, unless the poverty claim survives into the
prose, in which case it is the fact that complicates it honestly. It fails the delete-the-food test.
