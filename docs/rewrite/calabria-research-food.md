# Calabria (IT-78) — fact research for readings 3 and 4

Research pass for the rewrite of reading 3 ("'Nduja, chilli and the Calabrian pantry") and reading 4
("Tropea and the Costa degli Dei"). Audited against `content/calabria.js` lessons 3–4 and
`content/recipes/calabria.js` (five recipes: fileja, fileja con 'nduja, ragù di capra, pesce spada alla
bagnarese, cipolle di Tropea ripiene).

Method note: the session's WebSearch budget ran out partway through; the majority of the work was done by
fetching primary and institutional pages directly (MASAF/Qualigeo/Qualivita, consortium sites, ARSAC,
Istat, Ministero della Cultura's ethno-anthropological catalogue, Italian Wikipedia with its citations,
Project Gutenberg, 1911 Encyclopædia Britannica on Wikisource). Where a claim rests on a blog, a press
article or Wikipedia, it says so and which language edition. Several intended sources could not be
reached and are named in §4 so the writer does not assume they were checked.

---

## Section 1 — Food claim audit

### 'Nduja

**F1. "'Nduja comes from Spilinga, a village below Monte Poro in the province of Vibo Valentia."**
- Verdict: CONFIRMED, with a correction of position.
- What is true: Spilinga sits at 455 m on the **northern foothills** of Monte Poro, not "below" it in the
  sense of on the coast; the Poro plateau tops out at 710 m. Population 1,346 (31 May 2026). The eight
  comuni of the Poro plateau are Spilinga, Zungri, Rombiolo, Filandari, Joppolo, Caria, Nicotera, Limbadi.
- Source: it.wikipedia "Spilinga" and "Monte Poro" (Italian edition; the Spilinga history section is
  flagged incomplete on the page itself).

**F2. "Made from pork trimmings and fatback worked together with a very large quantity of sun-dried
Calabrian chilli."**
- Verdict: CONFIRMED in substance; the proportions should be given because they are the whole point.
- What is true: Slow Food's Ark of Taste entry specifies second-choice pork cuts — *sottopancia*
  (belly), *guanciale* (jowl), trimmings of shoulder and leg — with **about 300 g of chilli per kilo of
  meat**. Italian Wikipedia gives the ratio as **70% meat / 30% chilli** plus salt. A Spilinga producer's
  own description names jowl, belly, lard, shoulder and throat trimmings plus "a portion of lean", and
  says the fat/lean balance is the critical variable, without publishing a number. Historically the mix
  also used offal — spleen, stomach, lung, heart — which the modern commercial product has dropped.
- Source: Fondazione Slow Food, Arca del Gusto, "'Nduja di Spilinga"; it.wikipedia "'Nduja";
  monteporo-spilinga.it (producer, so a commercial source).

**F3. "Packed into a wide casing."**
- Verdict: PARTLY RIGHT — the casing has a name and the name is worth having.
- What is true: the traditional casing is the **blind gut, the *orba*** (plural *orbe*; also *orbara*),
  which gives the rounded shape; larger formats use *crespone* or *muletta*. Commercial sizes run from
  ~400 g *orbetta* pieces to 11 kg.
- Source: Slow Food Ark of Taste; it.wikipedia "'Nduja"; producer listings (Livasì, Nduja San Donato).

**F4. "Lightly smoked and aged for three to six months."**
- Verdict: PARTLY RIGHT — the sources disagree sharply and the reading should not present one number flat.
- What is true: three figures circulate. (a) Slow Food, describing the traditional artisan product: cold
  smoking **for at least ten days** with aromatic resins, then maturing **at least a year**. (b) Producers
  and trade descriptions: **90–180 days**, i.e. the three-to-six-month figure, "depending on size".
  (c) The producer page above: 3–6 months in cool, dark, ventilated cellars on the Poro plateau. The honest
  statement is that smoking is short and light and cold, and ageing runs from about three months for small
  formats to a year for a traditional *orba*.
- Source: Fondazione Slow Food (most authoritative on the artisan version); monteporo-spilinga.it;
  commercial listings. No official disciplinare exists yet to settle it (see F5).

**F5. "Not, at the time of writing, protected by a DOP or IGP."**
- Verdict: CONFIRMED, and still true as of the latest reachable reporting (February 2024).
- What is true: the IGP application for **'Nduja di Spilinga** was begun in **2008** and is still not
  granted. The Consorzio 'Nduja di Spilinga (president Francesco Fiamingo) drives it; the Calabria Region
  gave a binding favourable opinion on the disciplinare; a delegation met MASAF officials (led by Roberta
  Cafiero) in Rome on **8 February 2024**, when the dossier was described as in its final phase with only
  minor clarifications outstanding. The proposed zone is Spilinga **plus seven contiguous comuni** of the
  Monte Poro area — not Spilinga alone, which was the 2019 proposal. What 'nduja does have: **PAT**
  (Prodotto Agroalimentare Tradizionale) listing, and **Slow Food Ark of Taste** status — the Ark, note,
  not a Presidium.
- Crucially: the ministry stated at that meeting that other Calabrian territories may legitimately market
  their product as "'nduja" or "'nduja di Calabria"; only "di Spilinga" would be restricted. That is the
  fact behind every supermarket jar.
- Source: Qualivita news (13/14 Oct 2020); Corriere della Calabria, 8 Feb 2024; LaC News24, 17 Oct 2019;
  Fondazione Slow Food.

**F6. "The name is usually traced to the French *andouille*, by way of the Napoleonic period, though that
is an etymology rather than a document."**
- Verdict: the hedge is RIGHT and can be made sharper.
- What is true: three derivations circulate and none is documented. (a) French *andouille*. (b) Latin
  *inductilia* / *inducere*, "things to be introduced", i.e. stuffed things. (c) The parallel with
  Piedmontese *salam d'la doja* (*doja* = the clay jar, possibly from Latin *dolium*) — which matters,
  because a Piedmontese cognate weakens a purely French route. The Napoleonic transmission story (Joachim
  Murat, king of Naples 1808–1815) is offered by Italian food bodies as a hypothesis alongside a
  16th-century Spanish *embutidos* route; neither is attached to a document. Dialect variants: *anduja*,
  *anduglia*, *induja*, *'ndugghia*, *'nduda*.
- Note on sources: I could **not** reach a linguistic authority (Rohlfs's *Nuovo dizionario dialettale
  della Calabria*, or Treccani's dictionary entry — the Treccani URL I tried returned the homepage). Every
  source I did reach is a food-culture site or Wikipedia. So: the reading should keep its hedge and should
  not name a linguist.
- Source: it.wikipedia "'Nduja"; AIFB (Associazione Italiana Food Blogger) product page; Slow Food.
  English Wikipedia's "'Nduja" has **no etymology section at all** — its nine citations are recent
  food-press pieces and cookbooks.

**F7. Spilinga's festival.**
- Verdict: CONFIRMED and dated.
- What is true: the **Sagra della 'Nduja** has been held on **8 August** every year **since 1975**, and is
  described in local sources as the first gastronomic-folkloric festival in Calabria.
- Source: it.wikipedia "'Nduja" and "Spilinga"; regional tourism pages (secondary).

**F8. Production volumes; how much 'nduja sold as such is made in Calabria.**
- Verdict: UNVERIFIABLE from any source I reached.
- What is true: no consortium, ministry or Qualivita figure for tonnage, turnover or number of producers
  was findable (the 2019 and 2024 press pieces explicitly carry none). What *can* be said with a source is
  the structural fact in F5: the word "'nduja" is not protected, so anyone anywhere may use it. The trade
  claim that most 'nduja is made outside Spilinga follows from that but has no published number behind it.
- Recommendation: state the legal fact, not a share.

**F9. Recipe claim: "Roughly half of it is fat."**
- Verdict: UNVERIFIABLE as a number; directionally right.
- What is true: the recipe in `recipes/calabria.js` says "roughly half of it is fat". No official
  composition table exists (no disciplinare). Slow Food's cut list and the 70/30 meat-to-chilli ratio imply
  a very high fat fraction of the meat portion, but nobody publishes a fat percentage.
- Recommendation: "mostly fat cuts" rather than "half".

### Chilli

**F10. "Chilli arrived from the New World and found a region that took to it completely."**
- Verdict: PARTLY RIGHT — the timing in the old text is implied to be early and the documentary record is
  late. This is the most useful correction in the food reading.
- What is true, with dates: Columbus described chilli in a report of **15 January 1493**; on the second
  voyage (1494) his physician Diego Álvarez Chanca brought it back and it was presented at the Spanish
  court. The Italian botanist **Pier Andrea Mattioli described it in 1568**, calling it *pepe d'India*.
  For Calabria specifically the documentary trail is 17th–19th century, not 16th: **Tommaso Campanella**
  mentions *piper rubrum indicum* medicinally (the *Medicinalium*, commonly dated 1635; the article citing
  it gives no full citation); **1770**, Grimaldi records that chilli's preserving property answered the
  problem of meat spoiling through "ignorance and negligence"; **1792**, a treatise still calls *Capsicum
  frutescens* "exceedingly rare" in botanical gardens; **1804**, Columella catalogues regional varieties
  (sweet Spanish red and yellow, stronger green and cherry types); **1848**, Pugliese observes peasants
  eating preserved green peppers with bread for lunch. Landowners are recorded paying labourers in dried
  chilli.
- Why it spread: it displaced black pepper, which had been an expensive import, and anyone could grow it
  in a garden. Neither Brillat-Savarin (1825) nor Artusi (1891) includes it — it stayed "the spice of the
  poor" into the twentieth century.
- Source: Accademia Italiana del Peperoncino, "Storia" (an interested party, but the fullest narrative);
  iCalabresi, "Peperoncino e Calabria, storia di un grande amore (nato male)", which distinguishes sourced
  from unsourced claims and is the origin of the 1770 / 1792 / 1804 / 1848 citations above. These are
  secondary reports of primary texts; the writer should attribute cautiously ("a Calabrian treatise of
  1770 records…") rather than quote.

**F11. "It is the most aggressively spiced kitchen in Italy."**
- Verdict: UNVERIFIABLE. No measurement exists — no per-capita chilli consumption series by region, no
  comparative scoville study. It is a reputation, universally repeated, including by the region's own
  promotional bodies.
- Recommendation: either attribute it as reputation or replace it with something measurable (the 30%
  chilli in 'nduja is a fact and does the same work).

**F12. "The chilli both flavours and preserves it."**
- Verdict: PARTLY RIGHT — defensible as a contributing factor, wrong as the mechanism.
- What is true, from food science: Capsicum extracts and capsaicin do have measurable antimicrobial
  activity. In a controlled meat study, chipotle pepper oleoresin at **0.5% w/w** in beef hamburgers
  significantly reduced total aerobic bacteria, *Staphylococcus aureus*, *Brochothrix thermosphacta*,
  moulds, yeasts and lactic acid bacteria over storage (Olivas-Méndez et al., *Foods*, 2022). Other work
  reports a minimum lethal concentration of Capsicum extract against *Salmonella typhimurium* of about
  1.5 ml per 100 g of minced beef. The same authors state the practical limit plainly: fat content in meat
  blunts the effect, the dose needed in food is higher than in vitro, and sensory acceptance caps how much
  can be added.
- The honest statement for 'nduja: what preserves it is salt, the drop in water activity, the acidification
  of a fermenting meat, the cold smoke and months of drying; the chilli — at an unusually high 25–30% — is
  plausibly a real contributor rather than only a flavour, and was historically believed to be one (see the
  1770 record in F10). Say "helps preserve" and name the salt-and-drying mechanism first.
- Source: Olivas-Méndez et al., "Antioxidant and Antimicrobial Activity of Rosemary and Garlic Essential
  Oils and Chipotle Pepper Oleoresin on Beef Hamburgers", *Foods*, 2022 (PMC9319248); secondary summaries
  of Capsicum/Salmonella work.

**F13. The Diamante peperoncino festival — founding date and organiser.**
- Verdict: CONFIRMED, with names and dates the old text lacks.
- What is true: the first **Peperoncino Festival** was held at **Diamante in 1992**, conceived by the
  journalist **Enzo Monaco** as "1492–1992: cinquecento anni piccanti" — a riposte to the Columbus
  quincentenary celebrations in Genoa, which ignored the one American import that changed how southern
  Italy eats. The **Accademia Italiana del Peperoncino** was founded about two years later (c. 1994) and
  has organised it since; it claims over 2,000 members and delegations including Mexico City, Paris and
  New York. The festival runs Wednesday to Sunday on the second weekend of September. Attendance: at least
  100,000 a year on average since 1992; **223,000 in 2022**. The 2026 edition is the 34th.
- Source: peperoncinofestival.org "Festival Story" (organiser); it.wikipedia "Diamante"; CalNews (2026
  edition, 9–13 September).
- Bonus for §3: Diamante is also a **mural town** — the project began in **1981** under the painter Nani
  Razetti with mayor Evasio Pascale, with 85 artists from around the world painting the alleys.

### Cipolla rossa di Tropea

**F14. "Cipolla Rossa di Tropea Calabria IGP (2008)."**
- Verdict: CONFIRMED.
- What is true: registered **27 March 2008**, published in the EU Official Journal **L 86 of 28 March
  2008**. (I could not open EUR-Lex to confirm the regulation number; Qualigeo's page cites a later
  amending act, Reg. (UE) 2013/264, which should not be quoted as the registering regulation.)
- Source: Qualigeo (Fondazione Qualivita); it.wikipedia citing GUCE L 86, 28.03.08; Gazzetta Ufficiale
  17 April 2008 (the national publication).

**F15. "Grown on the Tyrrhenian coast between Nicotera and Campora San Giovanni, sweetest around Briatico
and Capo Vaticano" — i.e. it is not only Tropea.**
- Verdict: CONFIRMED and worth making explicit: the name contains a town, the product does not belong to it.
- What is true: the IGP zone covers comuni in **three provinces — Vibo Valentia, Catanzaro and Cosenza** —
  along the mid-to-upper Tyrrhenian coast, from Nicotera (VV) to Campora San Giovanni (CS). Production is
  concentrated between Briatico and Capo Vaticano. The official name is "Cipolla Rossa di Tropea
  **Calabria**" precisely because the zone is regional, not municipal.
- Source: Qualigeo; it.wikipedia "Cipolla rossa di Tropea Calabria"; Consorzio di Tutela.

**F16. The three types the rules recognise.**
- Verdict: MISSING from the old text and worth having.
- What is true: **cipollotto** (harvested mid-April to end of May, white-pink-violet, sweet and tender);
  **cipolla da consumo fresco** (mid-May to mid-June, white-red to violet); **cipolla da serbo**, the
  storage onion (mid-June to end of July). Three native ecotypes are distinguished by shape and by
  photoperiod-driven earliness: *Tondo Piatta* (early), *Mezza Campana* (mid-early), *Allungata* (late) —
  the *Allungata* is the long one in the photographs.
- Source: Qualigeo; agraria.org; it.wikipedia; Consorzio.

**F17. Production figures.**
- Verdict: AVAILABLE, and the old text has none.
- What is true (certified IGP production, Qualivita/Qualigeo): 2019 21,448,912 kg; 2020 18,440,318 kg;
  2021 22,928,847 kg; 2022 20,135,655 kg; **2023 17,966,196 kg**, turnover €10.78 m. **115 registered
  operators.**
- Source: Qualigeo/Osservatorio Qualivita.

**F18. "Sweet enough to eat raw… a winter without sharp swings in temperature, moderated by the sea, and
cool silty soils" — and the recipe's mechanism note (low pungency, sulphur compounds).**
- Verdict: PARTLY RIGHT — the explanation is the accepted agronomic one; the *measurable* basis is thinner
  than the recipe implies.
- What is true: the onion's sweetness is attributed to climate and soil in the disciplinare and by the
  consortium. The chemistry in the recipe note (pungency is created at the moment of cutting, when
  alliinase meets sulphur precursors; how much there is depends on sulphur availability and growing
  conditions) is correct general onion science. But **ARSAC**, the Calabrian regional agricultural agency,
  publishes a nutrition table for the Tropea onion taken from the Italian National Nutrition Institute
  (1997) and USDA (1998) — i.e. **generic onion values, not measurements of this cultivar** (water 91.1 g,
  available carbohydrate 5.7 g, fibre 1.1 g, 26 kcal per 100 g) — and states that the flavonoid profile of
  Tropea red onions is "qualitatively and quantitatively similar to other red onions". It gives **no
  pyruvate figure**. The widely repeated "low pyruvate content explains the sweetness" line appears in
  trade and press material without a measured value anywhere I could reach.
- Recommendation: keep the climate-and-soil explanation, drop or hedge any implication that the sweetness
  has been quantified. Do not print a pyruvate claim.
- Source: ARSAC, "La Cipolla Rossa di Tropea: aspetti salutistici e nutrizionali"; Consorzio; the pyruvate
  line traced only to commercial pages (agricolaconforti.it and similar).
- One measured detail worth using: removing the outer layer costs roughly **70% of the anthocyanins**
  (ARSAC).

**F19. "The Phoenicians (or Greeks) brought the onion."**
- Verdict: NO SOURCE ESTABLISHES THIS — and the consortium itself hedges.
- What is true: the Consorzio di Tutela writes "*sembra che* la cipolla sia stata introdotta dai Fenici
  nella zona del vibonese" — *it seems that*. It cites Strabo (bk 6) and Aristotle (*De Republica* bk 7)
  for onions being grown along this coast in antiquity, but, as one Italian account puts it, no ancient
  source specifies **which kind of onion**. The consortium's own first hard evidence is far later: the
  **Enciclopedia agraria Reda (1936–39)** provides "the first organised statistical surveys of onion
  cultivation in Calabria", and the crop spread commercially in the **Bourbon period**, when northern
  European markets began asking for it.
- Recommendation: this is a clean example of the course's house style — say that the official body itself
  says "it seems", and that the first count is from the 1930s.
- Source: consorziocipollatropeaigp.com, "La sua storia"; it.wikipedia (which repeats the Phoenician claim
  **without** hedging — a good illustration of why Wikipedia is not usable here).

**F20. "Sold in plaited strings from stalls and vans along the coast road."**
- Verdict: UNVERIFIABLE in any institutional source; universally photographed and true to observation.
  No document needed, but it should be written as observation, not as a rule of the trade.

### Fileja

**F21. "A short length of dough rolled around a thin reed or a knitting needle… so that it catches a heavy
sauce."**
- Verdict: CONFIRMED, with names the old text lacks.
- What is true: fileja is typical of the **province of Vibo Valentia**; the dough is **durum semolina and
  water**, and the reference work behind the Wikipedia entry is Oretta Zanini De Vita, *La pasta. Atlante
  dei prodotti tipici* (2004). The rod is the *ferretto*; the traditional one is a thin cane, the
  **dinaciulu** (also given as *danacu*, cut from the stalk of a wild plant called *gutamara*). The name is
  usually connected with *filare*, to spin. Regional synonyms: *filatelli*, *filateddhi*, *maccarruni i
  casa*, *maccaruni aru firriattu*, *strangugghi*. English Wikipedia describes the tube as "approximately
  20 cm" long, which is the long *ferrazzuoli* form; the everyday fileja is a short finger-length.
- Source: en.wikipedia "Fileja" (citing Zanini De Vita 2004 and Machado & Prete 2015); it.wikipedia
  "Cucina calabrese"; Italian food press for *dinaciulu*/*gutamara*.

**F22. Is fileja egg-free? (The old text never says.)**
- Verdict: CONFIRMED egg-free — semolina and water only. Worth stating in the reading, since the recipe
  depends on it.
- Source: as F21.

**F23. "The ferretto dates from Greek colonisation."**
- Verdict: NO SOURCE ESTABLISHES THIS — same verdict the project reached for Basilicata's *ferricelli*.
- What is true: the Italian Wikipedia entry on *maccheroni al ferretto* — the family that includes busiate,
  ceppe, frizzuli, maccarones de busa, macaron del frèt — gives them "humble origins", made by women
  avoiding waste, and cites Slow Food's *Dizionario delle cucine regionali italiane* (2010) and Doglio
  (1993). It makes **no Greek, Arab or medieval origin claim**. Nineteenth-century foreign travellers
  mention the pasta in their journals (asserted by Italian food sites without a citation), which is the
  earliest documentary date anyone offers.
- Recommendation: do not stage a Greek origin even to knock it down twice in the course; say the tool is a
  cane or a knitting needle and leave the antiquity out.

**F24. "Fileja col ragù di capra" — is goat ragù documented as Calabrian?**
- Verdict: CONFIRMED, though only in food-encyclopaedic rather than scholarly sources.
- What is true: the Italian Wikipedia survey of Calabrian cooking names **ragù di capra**, pairs fileja
  with goat, and lists *Capra a ragù* and *Crapettu all'usu i Riggiu* among regional dishes; at Bova the
  female goat is *lastra*. Goat and kid are grilled and stewed across the Aspromonte and Sila.
- Source: it.wikipedia "Cucina calabrese".

### Other pantry items

**F25. Caciocavallo Silano DOP — what is specifically Calabrian.**
- Verdict: the old text's use is safe but the course has now spent this cheese three times elsewhere.
- What is true: registered **1 July 1996**; cow's milk; coagulated at 36–38 °C with calf or kid rennet;
  curd ripened 4–10 hours, stretched by hand, sealed in water at 80–85 °C, brined at least six hours, then
  **hung in pairs on a beam for at least 30 days**; "Extra" ≥ 4 months, "Gran Riserva" ≥ 9 months. The zone
  covers five regions — **all five Calabrian provinces**, plus Campania, Molise, Puglia and Basilicata.
  2023: 1,430,138 kg, €15.7 m turnover, €5.4 m exported.
- The only Calabria-specific hook: **the name is the Sila's**. "Silano" is the plateau in reading 4. That,
  and nothing else, is what makes this cheese belong to this region rather than to four others.
- Source: Qualigeo/Qualivita.

**F26. Pecorino Crotonese DOP.**
- Verdict: CONFIRMED as a DOP; the old text gives no date.
- What is true: registered **26 November 2014**. Sheep's milk; **kid rennet**; coagulation 36–38 °C for
  40–50 minutes; four types — fresco, semiduro (60–90 days), stagionato (over 90 days), da grattugia. Zone:
  comuni in the provinces of **Crotone, Catanzaro and Cosenza**. It is tiny: 2023 production 94,502 kg,
  €1.08 m turnover, 30 operators.
- Source: Qualigeo; MASAF disciplinare of 25 October 2013 (the PDF is image-only and could not be text-read);
  Consorzio Pecorino Crotonese (disciplinare, September 2021); modification approved by Reg. (UE) 2023/2728
  of 28 November 2023.
- Caution: Italian Wikipedia's page on **Pecorino del Monte Poro** asserts DOP status under Reg. 2081/92.
  I could not corroborate that anywhere; treat it as doubtful and do not name it as a DOP.

**F27. Swordfish and the Bagnara/Scilla fishery — the boats.**
- Verdict: CONFIRMED, with figures, from the Italian Ministry of Culture's own catalogue.
- What is true: the modern boat is the ***passerella***, a motorised felucca with a bow gangway **about
  30 metres** long for the harpooner and a metal spotting mast **over 20 metres** high. The older boat was
  the *untru* (also *luntro*), about 6 m long with a 3 m mast, crewed by six — four rowers, a spotter up
  the mast and the harpooner at the bow, who is called ***u lanzaturi***, the launcher. The harpoon line is
  coiled in a wicker basket and pays out as the fish runs. Two eyes were painted on the bow against bad
  luck.
- Source: Ministero della Cultura, Catalogo generale dei Beni Culturali / culturalimentare.beniculturali.it,
  "Pesca del pesce spada a Bagnara Calabra" (ICCD record, 20th century).

**F28. The season, and whether the fishery still operates at any scale.**
- Verdict: PARTLY RIGHT / partly UNVERIFIABLE.
- What is true: the season is the warm months — the ICCD record says the fish come inshore in summer to
  spawn where the water is warmer; a secondary source gives **April to September**. The ICCD record
  presents the practice as surviving in motorised form rather than as history. Bagnara's **Gran Galà del
  Pesce Spada** reached its **61st edition in 2026** (24–26 July), which implies continuity from about 1965.
- What I could **not** establish: how many feluche work the Strait today, catch volumes, or the current
  ICCAT rules (the ICCAT site fetch returned only navigation). Mediterranean swordfish is the worst-managed
  stock of the species; IUCN rates *Xiphias gladius* Near Threatened overall and Italian Wikipedia states
  the Mediterranean population is the least sustainably exploited, with catches and individual sizes
  falling. Maximum size over 4.5 m and 400 kg; average about 3 m.
- Recommendation: do not write "still hunted from a handful of boats" or any number. Write the boat, the
  roles and the season, and say the stock is under pressure.
- Source: ICCD as above; it.wikipedia "Xiphias gladius"; event listings for the Gran Galà.

**F29. "Swordfish alla bagnarese" and salmoriglio (recipe).**
- Verdict: the dish name is local usage, not a codified thing; the sauce name is right.
- What is true: Bagnara Calabra is on the Calabrian shore of the Strait, population 9,097 (May 2026). Its
  economy is given as swordfish fishing, agriculture and tourism. The *bagnarote* — women who carried fish
  and goods in baskets and sold them door to door — are commemorated by a statue in the town and are a
  better-documented Bagnara subject than the recipe name. No institutional source defines "alla bagnarese".
- Source: it.wikipedia "Bagnara Calabra".

**F30. "A fishery the Greeks described" (reading and recipe).**
- Verdict: UNVERIFIED. The usual citation is Polybius (preserved in Strabo) on harpooning swordfish in the
  Strait, but I could not open a text of either to confirm it, and the Ministry of Culture record
  explicitly says it gives **no historical references predating modern documentation**.
- Recommendation: either verify Strabo I.2.15 / Polybius XXXIV directly before writing it, or write
  "described in antiquity" without naming the Greeks as observers, or drop it.

**F31. Bergamot: "grown on a narrow strip of the Ionian coast around Reggio Calabria, which supplies most
of Italy's crop."**
- Verdict: WRONG by understatement, and the geography is half right.
- What is true: the province of Reggio Calabria supplies **about 80% of world production** (it.wikipedia
  "Citrus bergamia"), and the figure of **about 90% of world production of DOP bergamot essential oil** is
  the one the region's own institutions use (Telemia, 2026, reporting on the Museo Nazionale del
  Bergamotto — no attribution given for the number). Not "most of Italy's crop": effectively all of it, and
  most of the world's. Cultivated area about **1,500 hectares**, average annual essential-oil production
  about **100,000 kg**, and roughly **200 kg of fruit to one kilo of essence**. The DOP zone is a coastal
  strip of about **100 km in the province of Reggio Calabria, from Villa San Giovanni to Gioiosa Jonica** —
  which wraps the tip, so "Ionian coast" is most of it but not all.
- Source: it.wikipedia "Citrus bergamia"; ARSAC; Qualigeo; Telemia (press, for the 90% figure).

**F32. Bergamot: the DOP, and whether it is edible.**
- Verdict: the DOP protects **the oil, not the fruit**, which the old text does not say.
- What is true: "**Bergamotto di Reggio Calabria – Olio essenziale**" DOP, established in Italian law
  25 February 2000 n. 39 and registered by Reg. (CE) 509/2001, published **15–16 March 2001**. Three
  cultivars: **Femminello, Castagnaro, Fantastico** (*Citrus bergamia* Risso). Cold pressing only, with
  the water/oil emulsion separated by centrifuge; yield 350–750 g of oil per 100 kg of fruit; maximum 450
  plants and 400 quintals per hectare. 29 registered operators (Qualigeo), which publishes no production
  or turnover series for it.
- Edibility: the whole fruit is rarely sold retail; the juice is markedly bitter (naringin) and is used
  commercially as a bittering agent in other juices; segments are used to flavour tea in place of lemon.
  So: usable, not eaten out of hand.
- Uses of the oil: perfumery (it is a base component of eau de Cologne) and **Earl Grey**. The frequently
  repeated claim that bergamot is in a very large share of the world's perfumes has **no source** I could

- Name: from Turkish *bey armudu*, "the lord's pear".
- Source: it.wikipedia "Bergamotto di Reggio Calabria - Olio essenziale" and "Citrus bergamia"; Qualigeo;

**F33. Liquorice: "Liquirizia di Calabria DOP (2011). About 1,000 hectares yield roughly 2,500 tonnes of

- Source: Qualigeo/Osservatorio Qualivita; ARSAC; the DOP disciplinare summary.

**F35. "Calabrian liquorice root has been reckoned the best in the world since at least the nineteenth

**F36. Sardella ("preserved whitebait with chilli") — the fact the old text misses.**

- What is true: sardella, or *rosamarina*, "Calabrian caviar", is a paste of *novellame* — the fry of
  sardine and anchovy, traditionally no more than 3 cm — with ground red chilli, salt and wild fennel seed.

**D4. "Tropea was named the most beautiful village in Italy in 2021" — which competition, run by whom.**

**D5. "Santa Maria dell'Isola… a monastery on its own rock" / facts box: "a former Benedictine house."**
- Verdict: PARTLY RIGHT — it is a **sanctuary church**, and it was Byzantine before it was Benedictine.
- What is true: the building on the rock is a **church and sanctuary**, dated to the **7th–8th centuries**
  in origin. The rock was first a hermits' and **Byzantine (Basilian) monks'** retreat; **Benedictines held
  it from the 11th century**, when it appears among the possessions of **Montecassino** as *Sancta Maria de
  Tropea cum omnibus pertinentiis suis*, and Robert Guiscard pushed the passage from Greek to Latin rite
  around **1060**. So: Byzantine first, Benedictine from the eleventh century, a sanctuary today — with an
  Assumption procession carrying an 18th-century Holy Family group.
- Source: it.wikipedia "Santuario di Santa Maria dell'Isola di Tropea"; borghipiubelliditalia.it (which
  gives "founded by Orthodox monks, 6th–7th c., Benedictine from the 11th").

**D6. "The rock was an island until silt joined it to the shore."**
- Verdict: the island is CONFIRMED; **the silt is not** — the attested cause is an earthquake.
- What is true: the rock was "completely surrounded by the sea". Italian accounts attribute its joining to
  the mainland to the **1783 earthquake and the anomalous wave that followed it**; Italian Wikipedia says
  only that it was once surrounded by sea and does not date the joining. No source I reached mentions
  silting.
- Recommendation: write the earthquake, hedged ("local accounts put it down to the 1783 earthquake"), or
  write only that it was an island and no longer is.
- Source: it.wikipedia "Santuario di Santa Maria dell'Isola di Tropea"; calabria.travel and asfalantea.it
  (regional history sites) for the 1783 attribution.

**D7. "The present building is largely medieval with later repairs, reached by a long stair cut up the
rock."**
- Verdict: PARTLY RIGHT — the earthquakes matter more than "later repairs" suggests, and the stair has a
  date.
- What is true: damaged by the **1783** earthquake and seriously damaged by the **1905** earthquake, after
  which the portico was made symmetrical, the bell tower centred and pinnacles added — "today little of the
  original structure is preserved". The **staircase was built around 1810**, i.e. after 1783, so the climb
  the visitor makes is a post-earthquake work, not a medieval one.
- Source: it.wikipedia "Santuario di Santa Maria dell'Isola di Tropea".

**D8. "On a clear day Stromboli is visible on the horizon, smoking."**
- Verdict: UNVERIFIED in any source I reached. Widely said, and geographically unremarkable (Stromboli lies
  roughly 100 km west-north-west). The Costa Viola page attributes the coast's violet sunsets partly to
  sulphurous particles from Stromboli, which is the nearest thing to corroboration I found.
- Recommendation: keep it only as what people on the terrace say, or drop "smoking".

**D9. "The Strait of Messina narrows to 3.1 km between Torre Faro and Villa San Giovanni."**
- Verdict: PARTLY RIGHT — the width is right; the two points usually named are different.
- What is true: the minimum width is **3.14 km**, given between **Villa San Giovanni (Calabria) and
  Messina**, corresponding to the **Punta Pezzo–Ganzirri** line. Torre Faro stands at Capo Peloro beside
  Ganzirri, so the sentence is close but not the standard pairing.
- Source: it.wikipedia "Stretto di Messina".

**D10. "Its currents and a whirlpool at the northern end are the origin of Scylla and Charybdis" / "the
tides of two seas meet in it… swallowing the sea three times a day."**
- Verdict: CONFIRMED, and the mechanism can be stated precisely.
- What is true: the Tyrrhenian and Ionian are in **opposite tidal phase**, with a level difference of up to
  **27 cm**, which reverses the exchange through the narrows every six hours: the ***montante*** carries
  denser Ionian water north beneath lighter Tyrrhenian water, the ***scendente*** carries Tyrrhenian water
  south over it. Peak speeds reach **200 cm/s**, and over 20 km/h when factors coincide. Named whirlpools:
  **Cariddi** south of Capo Peloro and **Scilla** off the Calabrian shore, formed on the *montante*, and
  **Garofalo** near Punta San Raineri on the *scendente*. Homer's Charybdis swallows and vomits the sea
  three times a day (*Odyssey* XII), which the old text has right.
- Source: it.wikipedia "Stretto di Messina".

**D11. "The rock is still called Scilla, with a Ruffo castle on top of it, and below the castle the
fishermen's quarter of Chianalea is built with its feet in the water."**
- Verdict: CONFIRMED.
- What is true: **Castello Ruffo** stands on the rock, fortified in the Magna Graecia period and
  strengthened under Rome (the page gives no construction dates). **Chianalea** is the fishermen's quarter,
  nicknamed "the little Venice of the South", its houses built against the water. Population about
  **4,381**. Scilla is one of the towns of the **Costa Viola** (with Palmi, Bagnara Calabra, Seminara and
  Villa San Giovanni, ~47,000 people together), a coast of terraced vineyards falling to the sea, whose
  name is traditionally attributed to Plato's sight of the violet light at sunset.
- Source: it.wikipedia "Scilla (Italia)" and "Costa Viola".

**D12. "Gerace sits on a rock above the Ionian plain with the largest Norman cathedral in Calabria."**
- Verdict: the rock is CONFIRMED; **"largest Norman cathedral" is UNVERIFIABLE from the sources I reached.**
- What is true: Gerace sits at **500 m** on a rock of **Miocene sandstone** at the south-eastern end of the
  plateau linking the Serre to the Aspromonte; population **2,266 (31 May 2026)**. The Duomo is of
  "difficult dating", with clear alterations across periods; it is a three-naved basilica divided by **20
  spolia columns** and two T-shaped pillars, with a crypt beneath. The columns come from the abandoned
  Greek city of **Locri Epizefiri**, whose people moved inland around the 7th century under pressure from
  pirates and malaria. Italian Wikipedia gives **no dimensions and does not call it the largest church in
  Calabria** — a claim repeated everywhere in tourist copy. Gerace holds the Touring Club's Orange Flag;
  the page does not list it among the Borghi più belli d'Italia.
- Recommendation: write the spolia columns from Locri — a far better fact than a superlative — and drop
  "largest" unless verified.
- Source: it.wikipedia "Gerace".

**D13. "The Cattolica di Stilo, a small ninth-century church of brick with five domes on a Greek-cross
plan, four columns inside."**
- Verdict: PARTLY RIGHT — the dating is a range, and the plan is described differently.
- What is true: built between the **9th and 10th centuries**, before the Norman conquest; its history was
  obscure until the 19th century, when the canon Michelangelo Macrì first documented it. The plan is
  **central, square**, with **four internal columns** dividing the space into nine bays; walls of brick and
  mortar **70 cm** thick; **five small domes** (one central, four at the corners) built in *opus
  reticulatum* and tiled in a diamond pattern — Bertaux and Ferrante considered them original rather than
  copies of Cypriot models. The four columns are reused: one has an inverted Ionic base under an upside-down
  Corinthian capital. Frescoes run from the late 10th to the 15th century, with a *Dormitio Virginis*
  dated **1552**, marking the shift from Byzantine to Latin rite. "Cattolica" is from Greek *katholikì*,
  the term for a privileged first-rank church with a baptistery under Byzantine administration.
- Source: it.wikipedia "Cattolica di Stilo".

**D14. "It carries Arabic inscriptions as well, which suggests it may have served both communities."**
- Verdict: PARTLY RIGHT — the inscriptions are real; the inference should be stated as scholars state it.
- What is true: **two *shahada*** — Islamic professions of faith — were found **in 1997** on internal

  point in the Middle Ages; how they were made is unclear. That is narrower than "served both communities"

- Also: since **2006** the Cattolica has been a candidate for UNESCO World Heritage listing in a group of

- Source: it.wikipedia "Cattolica di Stilo".

**D15. "Sila National Park (1997) covers 737 km² of granite plateau and forest, rising to Botte Donato at
1,928 m."**
- Verdict: CONFIRMED on area; two details need care.
- What is true: instituted by **law 344 of 8 October 1997**, definitively constituted by **DPR of 14

  Botte Donato, given as 1,929 m** on the park's Wikipedia page (the old text's 1,928 m is within the

  *laricio*, locally "pino silano"), with beech above about 1,400 m. The park has the **highest proportion

- On rank: Sila is **not** among Italy's largest parks — the **Pollino is Italy's largest national park at
  192,565 hectares** (88,650 in Basilicata, 103,915 in Calabria), instituted 1988–1990, a UNESCO Global

- On "granite": the Sila batholith is granitic and the description is standard, but I did not find it
  stated in a source I could cite — treat as safe but unverified here.
- Source: it.wikipedia "Parco nazionale della Sila" and "Parco nazionale del Pollino"; MASE park pages.

**D16. "The tallest trees, the so-called giants of the Sila, are larch pines of great age in a fenced

- Verdict: **WRONG on species.** They are not larches.
- What is true: the Giants of the Sila are **Calabrian black pines, *Pinus nigra* subsp. *laricio*** (the

- Source: it.wikipedia "Riserva naturale I Giganti della Sila".

**D17. "Aspromonte National Park covers the mountains of the far south… rises to nearly 2,000 metres."**
- Verdict: CONFIRMED; dates and figures available.

**D18. "In the Aspromonte villages the Grecanico dialect is still spoken by the old."**
- Verdict: CONFIRMED but far more precise numbers exist, and the origin question is a live dispute the
  reading can stage — with a warning (see the constraint note: Puglia's reading already stages the same
  dispute for Griko).
- What is true: **Greko / greco-calabro** survives in the **Bovesia** — **Bova, Bova Marina, Gallicianò

  Protected under **Italian law 482/1999** on linguistic minorities, with limited implementation; the

- The dispute: **Gerhard Rohlfs**, the German philologist, argued descent from the Doric Greek of **Magna

- Calabria-specific hook for the reading: **Bova** (915 m, **489 inhabitants at 31 May 2026**) is the

- Source: it.wikipedia "Dialetto greco-calabro"; en.wikipedia "Calabrian Greek"; Treccani (Romano, on the

**D19. Roghudi — the emptied village.**

- What is true: Roghudi Vecchio, on the southern slopes of the Aspromonte, was declared wholly

**D20. The Riace bronzes — absent from the old text.**
- Verdict: MISSING, and they are the region's most famous objects. Full detail:
- Found on **16 August 1972** by **Stefano Mariottini**, a Roman diving on holiday, about **230 m off
  Riace Marina** (220 m in another account) at **8–10 m** depth near the submerged rocks of Porto
  Forticchio, in the Locride. He reported it to the superintendent **Giuseppe Foti** on **17 August**; the
  official record of that day describes "two male figures… one lying on its back, the other on its side".
  Carabinieri divers raised **statue B on 21 August and statue A on 22 August 1972**.
- **Statue A** about **460 BC**, Severe Style; **statue B** about **430 BC**, in the Polykleitan tradition —
  radiocarbon on organic residues in the casting cores shows they are **not the same age**. Heights
  **1.98 m** and **1.97 m** without tenons; lost-wax bronze with inlaid eyes, lips and teeth. No consensus
  on who they are, who made them (Phidias, Pythagoras of Rhegion and Onatas of Aegina have all been
  proposed) or what monument they came from.
- Restoration: Reggio 1972–75; Florence 1975–80 (Giachetti, Formigli); ICR 1992–95; Palazzo Campanella,
  Reggio, 2009–13, finished 21 December 2013.
- Shown at the Florence archaeological museum from **15 December 1980** — **250,000 visitors in the first
  month** — and at the Quirinale in Rome in **July 1981**, **300,000 in twelve days**.
- Now in a climate-controlled room on level D of the **Museo Archeologico Nazionale di Reggio Calabria**,
  which reopened **30 April 2016** after nearly seven years closed (from November 2009), and recorded
  **201,016 visitors in 2022**. The same museum holds the Philosopher's Head from the Porticello wreck, the
  Reggio Kouros and the Dioscuri group from Locri.
- Source: it.wikipedia "Bronzi di Riace"; it.wikipedia "Museo archeologico nazionale di Reggio Calabria";
  Comune di Riace; il Mulino, "16 agosto 1972".

**D21. The 1783 Calabrian earthquakes — absent from the old text except by implication.**
- Verdict: MISSING and load-bearing for D6, D7 and the region's landscape.
- What is true: five shocks — **5 February 1783 (Mw 7.1, epicentre Oppido Mamertina), 6 February (5.9),
  7 February (6.7, Soriano Calabro), 1 March (5.9, Polia), 28 March (7.0, Borgia-Girifalco)**. Deaths
  **30,000–50,000** overall, about **30,000 in Calabria out of a population of 440,000 — 6.8% of the
  region** — and about 630 at Messina. Over **180 settlements** wholly or nearly destroyed; Oppido
  Mamertina lost 4,500; Polistena 2,261 of 4,600. At **Scilla** a tsunami killed on the order of **70% of
  the population**, striking people who had fled to the beach. The **Cassa Sacra** confiscated church
  property in Calabria Ulteriore to pay for reconstruction, and in practice enriched nobles who bought the
  land at auction. Bradyseismic effects around Bagnara and Scilla; sand liquefaction left circular craters
  on the plains; rivers changed course.
- Source: it.wikipedia "Terremoto della Calabria meridionale del 1783".

**D22. The 1908 Messina earthquake on the Calabrian shore.**
- Verdict: MISSING from the old text.
- What is true: **28 December 1908, 05:21**, magnitude about **7.1**. About **80,000 dead in Messina** out
  of some 147,000; about **15,000 in Reggio Calabria** out of some 78,000 (other estimates put Calabrian
  deaths as high as 40,000); roughly **120,000 in all**. The tsunami came as three great waves, reaching
  **6–12 m** on the Calabrian coast and **13 m at Pellaro**; Pellaro, Lazzaro and Gallico were devastated,
  and the backwash pulled boats, bodies and the injured out to sea.
- Source: it.wikipedia "Terremoto di Messina del 1908".

**D23. Emigration.**
- Verdict: MISSING; here is the only figure I could source with a period attached.
- What is true: Treccani states that **600,000 people left Calabria in the first fifteen years of the
  twentieth century**, after a demographic surge (up 40% in the first half of the 19th century); a second
  fall came in **1961–71**, when the population dropped 2.8%. Italian Wikipedia's emigration article gives
  only the aggregate "almost three million from Calabria, Campania, Puglia and Sicily, 1900–1920", with no
  Calabrian breakdown. Present population 1,824,052 (30 June 2026), falling.
- Recommendation: use the Treccani figure, dated and attributed; do not invent a percentage.
- Source: Treccani "Calabria"; it.wikipedia "Emigrazione italiana" and "Calabria".

**D24. Capo Colonna and the single column (not in the old text; a candidate for reading 4's Ionian side).**
- What is true: at Capo Colonna near Crotone stood the sanctuary of **Hera Lacinia**; of the Doric temple
  **one column remains standing, over 8 m tall**, of fluted drums. A second column **fell in an earthquake
  in 1638**. The site was quarried over centuries — Gissing records that a bishop pulled the temple down in
  the sixteenth century to build his palace, and that the basement stones went into strengthening the
  harbour after the **1783** earthquake. Hannibal sacked the treasury when leaving Italy and left bronze
  tablets recording his Italian campaigns on the temple walls. The archaeological park includes a sacred
  way about 60 m long and a 16th-century lighthouse.
- Source: it.wikipedia "Capo Colonna"; Gissing, *By the Ionian Sea*, ch. VII.

**D25. "Behind the beaches the region is high and empty… almost nobody on the beach goes up."**
- Verdict: the first half is supported (91% hill and mountain; see F40); the second half is an assertion
  with no source. No visitor-distribution data for Calabrian coastal versus inland tourism was findable.

## Section 3 — Candidate openings

The rewrite's rule: open on something happening to someone somewhere. Eleven candidates, each with its
source and what it opens onto. The constraint flags are at the end.

**O1. The diver, 16 August 1972.** Stefano Mariottini, a Roman on holiday, is snorkelling 230 metres off
Riace Marina in about eight metres of water when he sees what he takes for a human arm in the sand. He
reports it the next day to the superintendent Giuseppe Foti; on 21 and 22 August, Carabinieri divers bring
up two Greek bronzes older than almost anything standing on land in Italy.
→ Opens onto: Magna Graecia as the thing under Calabria rather than behind it; the museum at Reggio;
the sea as the region's archive. Source: it.wikipedia "Bronzi di Riace"; Comune di Riace. Unique to
Calabria.

**O2. The man at the end of the gangway.** On a summer morning off Bagnara, a spotter climbs twenty metres
of steel mast while the harpooner walks thirty metres out over the bow on a plank, so far forward that the
boat behind him is irrelevant; the crew call him *u lanzaturi*, the launcher. The harpoon line is coiled in
a wicker basket so it can run when the fish does.
→ Opens onto: the swordfish, the Strait, the dish of thin slices with oil and oregano. Source: Ministero
della Cultura / ICCD, "Pesca del pesce spada a Bagnara Calabra". Unique to Calabria and Sicily's facing
shore.

**O3. Diamante, 1992.** A journalist named Enzo Monaco watches Italy spend the Columbus quincentenary on
ships and ignore the one thing off those ships that changed how southern Italians eat, and answers with a
festival called "1492–1992: five hundred spicy years". Thirty-four editions later it draws over two hundred
thousand people to a town of five thousand.
→ Opens onto: chilli, and the long gap between 1493 and the moment Calabria actually took it up (F10).
Source: peperoncinofestival.org; it.wikipedia "Diamante"; attendance 223,000 (2022).

**O4. A Calabrian writer, 1770.** Grimaldi records that the chilli answers a practical problem: meat
spoiling through ignorance and negligence. A treatise of 1792 still calls the plant exceedingly rare in
botanical gardens; by 1848 Pugliese sees labourers eating preserved green peppers with bread for lunch, and
landowners paying wages in dried chilli.
→ Opens onto: chilli arriving as economics, not romance — and the honest version of "the chilli preserves
it". Source: iCalabresi's survey of the documentary record. **Verify the Grimaldi citation before naming
him**; the article names no first name or title.

**O5. Rossano, 1731.** A family that had been drying and boiling liquorice root as a sideline to its estates
since the 1500s writes down the founding of a *concio* — a works, not a farm outbuilding. It is still
running, and the fifteenth-century palazzo beside it became a museum in 2001.
→ Opens onto: liquorice, and the 1911 Britannica line that British confectioners' preferred juice came
from Calabria and was sold as Solazzi and Corigliano. Source: it.wikipedia "Amarelli"; amarelli.it;
Britannica 1911.

**O6. Easter Sunday, 4 April 2021.** A television programme on Rai 3 announces the result of a public vote
and Tropea — a town whose resident population has fallen by a fifth since 2008, to about 5,600 — becomes
the most beautiful village in Italy. The following August the streets do not fit the people in them.
→ Opens onto: Tropea, the Costa degli Dei as a 1990s marketing name, and what fifty kilometres of coast
does to a region that has the lowest income per head in Italy. Source: Ministero del Turismo; Istat via
it.wikipedia and Treccani.

**O7. Spilinga, 8 August.** A village of 1,346 people at 455 metres on the north side of Monte Poro has
held the same festival on the same date since 1975, for a sausage that still has no protected name — the
ministry told the consortium in February 2024 that anyone in Calabria may go on calling their product
'nduja.
→ Opens onto: 'nduja, and the difference between a place and a word. Source: it.wikipedia "Spilinga";
Corriere della Calabria, 8 February 2024.

**O8. Bova, 21 May 2016.** In a village of under five hundred people at 915 metres, a museum opens named
for Gerhard Rohlfs — the German philologist who argued that the Greek spoken here came down from Magna
Graecia and not from Byzantium, against colleagues who said the opposite. Somewhere between five hundred
and two thousand people still speak it.
→ Opens onto: the Grecanica, the Aspromonte, the argument staged in the place it is about. Source:
it.wikipedia "Bova (Italia)" and "Dialetto greco-calabro".

**O9. Cotrone, a night of storm in 1897.** George Gissing gets off a train at ten at night into a loud
storm, finds a dark stone staircase under a colonnade — "this was the hotel, the whole of it" — and eats
food he calls poor, monotonous and infamously cooked. Within days he is ill enough to need a doctor, who
becomes the subject of his ninth chapter.
→ Opens onto: the Ionian side, Capo Colonna's single column, and the difference between what a traveller
found and what the coast sells now. Source: Gissing, *By the Ionian Sea* (published 1901; journey
December 1897), chs. VII–IX; a plaque at Catanzaro records his stay at the Albergo Centrale with the
innkeeper Coriolano Paparazzo. **Caution: I could not read chapter IX. The doctor is commonly named as
Riccardo Sculco; verify before printing the name.**

**O10. Summer 1847.** Edward Lear and John Proby walk through Calabria drawing it; the tour is cut short by
the uprising at Reggio in the autumn, and the book comes out in London in 1852 as *Journals of a Landscape
Painter in Southern Calabria*.
→ Opens onto: the mountains nobody visits, and a Calabria that has always been reached by outsiders on
foot and left early. Source: it.wikipedia "Edward Lear" (dates, Proby, route, the Reggio uprising);
en.wikipedia (publication 1852). **The Gutenberg text was not reachable; no passage can be quoted.**

**O11. Fallistro, a fenced five hectares.** Fifty-eight Calabrian black pines, the tallest 43.6 metres,
some of them standing since the 1620s, in a grove of 5.44 hectares that the state made a reserve in 1987
and handed to the FAI to run in 2016 — the last unlogged corner of a plateau that supplied timber for
centuries.
→ Opens onto: the Sila, which is 80% forest, the most forested national park in Italy. Source:
it.wikipedia "Riserva naturale I Giganti della Sila".

**Constraint check — what is genuinely Calabria's own:**
- Safe and unduplicated: **'nduja** (a spreadable fermented chilli salame with no parallel elsewhere in
  Italy); the **harpoon fishery of the Strait** and its 30-metre gangway; **bergamot**, where one province
  holds roughly 80% of world production; the **liquorice concio** and the 1911 Britannica line; the
  **Greko** of the Bovesia with its own named dispute and its own museum; the **Riace bronzes**; the
  **Tropea onion** as an IGP whose zone is three provinces wide; the **Giants of the Sila**.
- Already spent elsewhere, avoid or invert: **cucina povera** as a frame (Toscana, Puglia and Basilicata
  all refuse or own it — Calabria has the strongest numerical claim to poverty, F38, so if it is used at
  all it should be used as an Istat fact with a year, not as a mood); **stretched-curd cheese hung in
  pairs** (Caciocavallo Silano has now appeared in Molise, Puglia and Basilicata — F25 shows the only
  Calabrian angle left is that the cheese is named after the Sila); **dried peppers on strings and a
  near-extinct pepper** (Basilicata, just written — so Calabrian chilli must be carried by the 1992
  festival and the 18th-century documents, not by strings on a wall); **transhumance and drove roads**
  (Abruzzo, Molise); **a ghost town emptied by landslide** (Craco in Basilicata — which makes **Roghudi**,
  D19, a near-repeat; use it only inside the language story, not as a ruin scene).
- Not found, though asked for: **no named grower, onion-plaiter or swordfish spotter** appears in any
  source I could reach. Section 3 cannot supply one; if the reading wants a named person on the coast road
  it will have to be found in reporting, not invented.

## Section 4 — Unguarded claims

Plausible, widely repeated, and with no primary source found:

1. **"About 1,000 hectares yield roughly 2,500 tonnes of [liquorice] root a year."** No source. The only
   published series (certified DOP) is 27–44 tonnes a year, 2021–2023, from 31 operators (F33).
2. **"The 1928 Encyclopaedia Britannica."** The text is from the **1911** edition (F35).
3. **"Calabrian liquorice root has been reckoned the best in the world."** The sourced claim is narrower:
   the juice best appreciated in the United Kingdom was Calabrian (F35). Amarelli itself does not claim it.
4. **"[Bergamot oil] sits in a very large share of the world's perfumes."** Trade lore. No figure found in
   any source, including the DOP documentation and the region's own agency.
5. **"Costa degli Dei… marketed since the 1950s."** No source; regional reporting says the 1990s (D1).
6. **"A sandstone cliff" at Tropea.** No source names the rock (D2).
7. **"The rock was an island until silt joined it to the shore."** No source mentions silt; the attested
   cause is the 1783 earthquake and its wave (D6).
8. **"Stromboli is visible on the horizon, smoking."** No source (D8).
9. **"The largest Norman cathedral in Calabria" at Gerace.** Tourist-copy superlative; not in the
   encyclopaedic entry (D12).
10. **"The most aggressively spiced kitchen in Italy."** No measurement exists (F11).
11. **"The chilli both flavours and preserves it."** Overstated as a mechanism (F12).
12. **"Aged three to six months"** stated flat, when Slow Food says at least a year for the traditional
    product (F4).
13. **"Roughly half of it is fat"** (recipe). No composition data exists (F9).
14. **"A fishery the Greeks described"** (reading and recipe). Unverified; the ministry's own record
    disclaims pre-modern references (F30).
15. **"Pecorino crotonese, hard and sharp from the Ionian pastures"** — the DOP zone is Crotone, Catanzaro
    and Cosenza, which is not only the Ionian side; and the cheese is very small in scale (F26).
16. **The Tropea onion's sweetness attributed to measurable low pungency.** No published pyruvate figure
    for this cultivar (F18).
17. **"Grown on the sandy coastal strip north and south of Tropea."** True of the concentration, not of the
    IGP zone, which spans three provinces and about 100 km of coast (F15).
18. **"Its oil… supplies most of Italy's crop"** (bergamot) — an understatement rather than an error, but
    unsourced as written (F31).
19. **"No refrigeration"** stated of the place rather than of the period (F39).
20. **The Amarelli museum as "the second most visited business museum in Italy."** Press claim, no figures
    (F34).

Sources I could not reach, so that nobody assumes they were checked: EUR-Lex (the onion's registering
regulation number); the MASAF Pecorino Crotonese disciplinare (image-only PDF); Treccani's dictionary entry
for *'nduja*; Rohlfs's Calabrian dialect dictionary; the full text of Gissing chapters IX onward and of
Norman Douglas's *Old Calabria* beyond chapter VII; Edward Lear's *Journals* (not on Gutenberg or reachable
on archive.org); ICCAT's swordfish recommendations; the Liquirizia di Calabria consortium site (DNS
failure); any peer-reviewed measurement of Tropea onion pungency.

## Section 5 — What the old text misses (only where it reaches a plate, a product or the landmark)

1. **The Riace bronzes are not mentioned at all** (D20). They are the most famous objects in Calabria, they
   came out of the sea in living memory, and they are half an hour from the swordfish coast the reading
   already describes.
2. **'Nduja's chilli proportion — about 30% of the mix, 300 g per kilo of meat** (F2). The reading says
   "a very large quantity"; the number is what makes the product sound impossible and then true.
3. **The casing has a name, the *orba*, the blind gut** (F3), and the shape of the sausage follows from it.
4. **'Nduja's legal position** (F5): the IGP has been in process since 2008 and the ministry has said other
   Calabrian producers may keep using the name. That is the fact behind every jar sold abroad.
5. **The onion's three types and its 115 producers across three provinces** (F16, F17). The reading treats
   it as a Tropea speciality; it is a regional industry of about 18–23 million kg a year.
6. **The consortium's own hedge on the Phoenicians** (F19) — better than the legend, and in the house style.
7. **Sardella's legal squeeze** (F36): the derogation for fishing fry was withdrawn in 2010 and the jars now
   often contain imported icefish. The reading lists sardella in a facts box and says nothing about it.
8. **Bergamot is a near-monopoly, roughly 80% of world production, and the DOP covers the oil, not the
   fruit** (F31, F32). The reading undersells it as "most of Italy's crop".
9. **Fileja is egg-free semolina-and-water, from the province of Vibo Valentia, and its cane has a name —
   *dinaciulu*** (F21, F22). The recipe depends on the first fact and the reading never states it.
10. **Pecorino Crotonese is a DOP of 2014 and is tiny** (F26); Caciocavallo Silano is named for the Sila,
    which is the only thing making it Calabrian in a course that has already used it three times (F25).
11. **Tropea's "most beautiful village" title is a Rai 3 television vote announced on 4 April 2021**, not a
    heritage listing (D4) — and the town has lost a fifth of its residents since 2008 (D3).
12. **Santa Maria dell'Isola was Byzantine before it was Benedictine, and the stair was built about 1810**
    (D5, D7). The reading calls it a monastery and implies a medieval climb.
13. **The 1783 earthquakes** (D21) explain the joined rock, the ruined sanctuary, Scilla's tsunami and the
    Cassa Sacra; **1908** (D22) explains the coast between Villa San Giovanni and Reggio. Neither is in the
    reading, which nonetheless leans on the landscape they made.
14. **The Giants of the Sila are pines, not larches, and there are 58 of them in 5.44 hectares run by the
    FAI** (D16) — a straight species error in the current text.
15. **Sila's real distinction is forest cover (about 80%, the highest of any Italian national park), not
    size** — Pollino, also partly Calabrian, is the country's largest park (D15).
16. **Calabria is last in Italy on GDP per head — €21,700 in 2024, against a Centre-North 1.75× higher**
    (F38). The reading asserts poverty without a number; the number exists and is current.
