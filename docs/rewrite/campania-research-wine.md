Research done 2026-09-16 for the rewrite of `content/campania.js` readings 1 and 2 (`window.READINGS['IT-72']`).
Claims extracted from the existing text; verdicts CONFIRMED / CORRECTED / DISPUTED / UNSUPPORTED / CANNOT VERIFY.
Labels: **[producer]** the estate's own account, **[promotional]** consortium/marketing/tourism, **[press]** trade or
local press, **[reference]** encyclopaedia or appellation mirror, **[academic]** peer-reviewed, **[calculated]**

**Note on primary sources.** `catalogoviti.politicheagricole.it` (the Ministry's register) again refused or returned
nothing usable, and the Regione Campania *disciplinare* PDFs came back as unparseable binary. Every rulebook quoted
below therefore comes from mirrors (agraria.org, disciplinare.it, quattrocalici, assovini, italianwinecentral), which

**Headline: the two readings contain one causal error that is the spine of reading 1 (volcanic soil does not stop

## Part A — verification

### Reading 1

### W1. "Campania is the oldest wine country in Italy"
**Verdict: UNSUPPORTED as a "first", and the archaeology points elsewhere.**
The oldest wine residue identified in Italy is from **Monte Kronio, near Sciacca in Sicily**: copper storage jars
excavated in 2012, dated to the **fourth millennium BC**, with tartaric acid and its salt recovered from the residue
(University of South Florida with Italian institutions, published in *Microchemical Journal*). Before that find, the
oldest evidence in Italy was a **3,000-year-old wine press from Sardinia** (Nuragic, found in the 1990s).
URLs: https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/discovery-shows-italians-have-been-making-wine-6000-years-180964701/ ,
https://www.sciencedaily.com/releases/2017/08/170824141201.htm **[press reporting academic work]**
**Recommendation:** drop "oldest wine country in Italy". What is defensible and much more specific: Campania holds the
oldest *written* evidence of wine-drinking in the western Mediterranean (W2), and the densest surviving population of
ungrafted vines in Italy (W12).

### W2. **Nestor's Cup — the fact the reading does not have and should**
**Verdict: CONFIRMED, and it is the best-documented single object in this whole file.**
A Rhodian clay cup, found in **1954** in **Tomb 168** of the San Montano cemetery at **Pithekoussai** (modern Lacco
Ameno, Ischia), the Euboean trading settlement founded in the 8th century BC. Scratched into it after firing, in three
lines, is one of the earliest surviving inscriptions in the Greek alphabet and the earliest surviving hexameter verse:
*"I am the cup of Nestor, good for drinking. Whoever drinks from this cup, desire for fair-crowned Aphrodite will
seize him instantly."* Dated to the **second half of the 8th century BC**. A 2021 study of the cremated remains in the
tomb (PLOS ONE) found they belonged to at least three individuals, upsetting the long-told story that it was buried
with a child.
URLs: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nestor%27s_Cup_(Pithekoussai) **[reference]** ,
https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8494320/ **[academic]**
**Why it matters here:** it is a joke about drinking, written down on an island in the Bay of Naples, before Rome was
a city. It carries the Greek-colony point without the unsupported "the Greeks brought the vines" claim (W3).

### W3. "The Greeks who founded Cumae and Naples in the eighth century BC brought vines with them"
**Verdict: CORRECTED on two counts.**
- **Cumae: confirmed.** Founded c. **740–730 BC** by Euboean colonists from Pithekoussai, under the joint founders
  Megasthenes of Chalcis and Hippocles of Cyme; the first Greek colony on the Italian mainland.
  URL: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cumae **[reference]**
- **Naples: wrong by about three centuries.** The 8th-century foundation in the bay is Cumae (and Pithekoussai
  before it). *Neapolis* — the "new city" beside the older Parthenope — is a later foundation, conventionally placed
  in the 6th–5th century BC. Writing "Cumae and Naples in the eighth century BC" puts a date on Naples that no source
  supports. Say "Cumae, above the bay, around 740 BC" and leave Naples out of the date.
- **"brought vines with them" is the disputed half.** Domesticated *Vitis vinifera* is attested in southern Italy in
  the **Middle/Late Bronze Age**, i.e. before the Greek colonies, with the genetic evidence converging on an oriental
  origin arriving through the Aegean with the Mycenaeans. What the 8th-century Greeks are credited with is trained
  vine cultivation, varieties and the drinking culture, not the introduction of the vine as such.
  URL: https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10570292/ **[academic — "Disentangling the origins of viticulture
  in the western Mediterranean"]**
**Safe wording:** "The Greeks who settled the bay found vines already growing and brought their own ways of training
and drinking them."

### W4. "called the whole peninsula *Oenotria*, the land of trained vines"
**Verdict: CORRECTED twice — the extent is wrong and the meaning is contested.**
- **Extent:** *Oenotria / Enotria* named the **south** — the Cilento, central and southern Basilicata, and the part of
  modern Calabria north of the Catanzaro isthmus — in the writers of the 8th to 4th centuries BC. Not the whole
  peninsula.
- **Meaning:** Greek Οἰνωτρία, from οἶνος (*oinos*, wine) **or** from οἴνωτρον (*oinotron*, the pole that supports a
  vine) **or** from the eponymous king Oenotrus, son of Lycaon, in Dionysius of Halicarnassus and Strabo. The Italian
  Wikipedia entry explicitly calls the wine derivation "una credenza comune, senza fondamento" — a common belief
  without foundation.
URLs: https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enotria , https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oenotrians **[reference]**
**Recommendation:** if the name is used at all, stage it as contested in half a sentence. "The land of trained vines"
is one of three readings and is the one an etymologist would file under folk etymology.

### W5. "Then came phylloxera, war and industry, and by 1970 the region's wine was mostly anonymous bulk"
**Verdict: the *conclusion* is supported by present-day data; the 1970 date is UNSUPPORTED.**
- Present day: of the 2022 vintage, **70% of Campania's wine was bulk**, 19% DOP, 11% IGP.
  URL: https://www.quattrocalici.it/regione/campania/ **[reference]**
  A different dataset (UIV/ISTAT via assovini) gives DOP 38.4% and IGP 22.4% of a 1,664,000 hl total —
  **flagged disagreement**, and the two are probably measuring different years and different bases. Do not print a
  precise percentage; "most of it still leaves in bulk" is safe on either figure.
  URL: https://www.assovini.it/italia/campania/item/81-i-vini-della-regione-campania **[promotional]**
- **No source I reached gives a 1970 figure.** What is sourced for the modern period is a collapse in area: the 7th
  General Census of Agriculture recorded **17,155 ha of vines against 23,281 ha in 2010, a fall of 26.3%**.
  URL: https://agricoltura.regione.campania.it/statistica/statistica_VII_censimento.html **[official]**
  (Note the census figure and the 24,190–26,000 ha figures quoted elsewhere are not measuring the same thing; see W6.)
- Wine Spectator's obituary of Antonio Mastroberardino states the family property had to be brought back "from the
  brink of devastation due to the combined effects of economic depression, phylloxera and war" — the same three
  causes the reading names, in a reputable source, but about one estate, not the region.
  URL: https://www.winespectator.com/articles/italian-winemaker-antonio-mastroberardino-dies-at-86-49541 **[press]**

### W6. Campania's size, for scale (the readings carry no numbers at all)
**Verdict: available and worth using, with the caveat that sources disagree on the base.**
- Vineyard area: **24,190 ha** (assovini, undated), **~26,000 ha in 2023, about 4% of the national total**
  (inumeridelvino/press), **17,155 ha at the 2020 census** (official, and the lowest because it counts farms
  differently). **Flagged disagreement — attribute whichever you use.**
- Production: **1,664,000 hl** (assovini) or **1.03 million hl for the 2022 vintage** (quattrocalici).
- Denominations: **4 DOCG, 15 DOC, 10 IGT**.
URLs: https://www.assovini.it/italia/campania/item/81-i-vini-della-regione-campania **[promotional]** ,
https://www.quattrocalici.it/regione/campania/ **[reference]**

### W7. "Vesuvius, still active, last erupted in 1944"
**Verdict: CONFIRMED and datable to the day.** The eruption began on the afternoon of **18 March 1944** and lasted
about ten days, effusive then explosive.
URLs: https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eruzione_del_Vesuvio_del_1944 **[reference]** ,
https://www.ov.ingv.it/index.php/storia-vesuvio/1944 **[official — Osservatorio Vesuviano, INGV]**

### W8. Facts box: "Vesuvius last erupted in March 1944, destroying vineyards and two villages; about 700,000 people
live in the red zone"
**Verdict: CORRECTED in detail, CONFIRMED on the red zone.**
- The villages were **partially**, not wholly, destroyed: lava invaded **Massa di Somma** and **San Sebastiano al
  Vesuvio** and reached Cercola. Roughly **10,000–12,000 people** were evacuated, mostly to Portici.
- **The 26 deaths were not from lava**: they were caused by **roofs collapsing under the weight of fallen ash**, at
  Terzigno, Pagani and elsewhere. That is a better and more surprising fact than "destroying vineyards".
- **"destroying vineyards" is UNSUPPORTED** in anything I read. Say what is documented instead.
URLs: https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eruzione_del_Vesuvio_del_1944 **[reference]** ,
https://www.liberoricercatore.it/18-marzo-1944-lultima-grande-eruzione-del-vesuvio/ **[press]**
- **Red zone: CONFIRMED.** **25 comuni, about 700,000 inhabitants** and about 380,000 vehicles; the perimeter was
  redrawn in 2013 and again in 2014, raising the population to be evacuated from about 550,000 to about 700,000; the
  plan aims to empty it in **72 hours**. Red zone 1 is the area exposed to pyroclastic flows, red zone 2 to roof
  collapse from ash — the same mechanism that killed people in 1944.
URLs: https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zona_rossa_del_Vesuvio **[reference]** ,
https://www.protezionecivile.gov.it/it/approfondimento/aggiornamento-del-piano-nazionale-di-protezione-civile-il-vesuvio/ **[official]**

### W9. "West of the city the Campi Flegrei… are a collapsed caldera of **forty vents**"
**Verdict: CORRECTED — the number is wrong and no source gives forty.**
- INGV and the Protezione Civile: after the Neapolitan Yellow Tuff eruption there have been **more than 70 eruptions**
  in the last 15,000 years, and today **at least 24 volcanic structures and craters** are recognisable, some of them
  under the sea. The caldera is about **12–13 km across**.
- A different count, for the volcanic *district* including Procida and Ischia: **19 mostly monogenic craters in
  65 km²**. **Flagged disagreement** — the counts measure different things; do not print a single number without
  saying what is being counted.
- The last eruption was **Monte Nuovo, 29 September 1538**, a week-long eruption that built a 130 m cone and ended
  about 3,000 years of quiet.
URLs: https://rischi.protezionecivile.gov.it/it/approfondimento/campi-flegrei/ **[official]** ,
https://www.ov.ingv.it/index.php/flegrei-storia-eruttiva/le-eruzioni-principali/monte-nuovo **[official]**

### W10. "the ground still steams at Solfatara"
**Verdict: CONFIRMED.** Since 1538 the caldera's activity has been bradyseism plus **fumarolic and hydrothermal
activity localised in the Solfatara area**. (Note for the photo caption: the Solfatara crater has been closed to the
public since the fatal accident of September 2017 — verify current status before writing a sentence that invites the
reader to walk in.)
URL: https://www.ov.ingv.it/index.php/monitoraggio-sismico-e-vulcanico/campi-flegrei/campi-flegrei-storia-eruttiva **[official]**

### W11. "Inland, ancient eruptions dropped ash over the hills of Irpinia, where it weathered into the yellow tuff
that gives the village of Tufo its name"
**Verdict: broadly CONFIRMED, but the reading over-volcanises Irpinia, and reading 2 then contradicts itself.**
- Tufo is named for its tuff and the Greco vineyards sit on «terra gialla», yellow earth, some of them directly over
  the abandoned mine galleries. URL: https://www.lucianopignataro.it/a/tufo-dalle-miniere-di-zolfo-al-greco/681/ **[press]**
- **But Irpinia's soils are described by the growers' own sources as calcareous-clay with superficial volcanic
  deposits** — «terreni calcarei-argillosi … con depositi vulcanici superficiali» — not as volcanic ground.
  URL: https://consorziovinidirpinia.it/territorio/ **[promotional]**
  Reading 2 says Fiano grows "on clay and limestone", which is right; reading 1 implies the whole inland zone is
  volcanic, which is not. Fix reading 1, not reading 2.

### W12. **"It is also loose and sandy, which is why phylloxera… could never move through it" — the central causal
claim of reading 1**
**Verdict: CORRECTED. The reading attributes to *volcanic* soil a property that belongs to *sand*, and gets the
mechanism and the dates wrong.**
- **The mechanism is physical, not chemical.** Phylloxera needs cohesive soil to move between roots and to hold
  moisture; in loose sand its galleries collapse and it desiccates, breaking the life cycle. The thresholds quoted in
  the literature: soils with **less than about 3% clay**, or **more than about 65–70% sand**, are largely
  inhospitable.
  URL: https://www.winewithseth.com/winewiki/sandy-soils/ **[reference — wine-education site, not primary]**
- **The same property, not volcanism, protects Colares in Portugal and the Sable de Camargue in France** (sandy
  fraction 80%+ by mass). Those are the comparisons that make the mechanism legible, and they are not volcanic at all.
  Same URL.
- **Italian sources for Campania say the same thing, in the same order**: «La ragione per cui in Campania ci sono
  ampie zone non toccate dalla fillossera è la natura sabbiosa del territorio. Il parassita non riesce a riprodursi
  nei terreni sabbiosi.»
  URLs: https://www.quicampiflegrei.it/2022/10/31/vulcani-flegrei-e-vini-a-piede-franco-in-prephyilloxera/ **[press]** ,
  https://www.slowfood.it/slowine/storie-terra-piede-franco-nei-campi-flegrei/ **[press]**
  One producer-facing source does assert a "chemical-physical" reason; it is the weaker formulation and I would not
  follow it. URL: https://www.cibotoday.it/citta/napoli/vini-vulcanici-vesuvio-campania-cantine.html **[press]**
- **The dates are wrong.** Phylloxera reached **France in the 1860s**; in **Italy the first confirmed outbreak is
  1879**, at Valmadrera (Como) and Agrate (Milano), with vine decline noticed near Lecco from 1875. By December 1894
  it was in 27 provinces; Tuscany's first certain detection is 1888; it was still being fought into the 1930s. "After
  1870" is wrong for France (too late) and for Italy (too early).
  URLs: https://www.guadoalmelo.it/la-fillossera-arriva-in-italia/ **[press]** ,
  https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daktulosphaira_vitifoliae **[reference]**
- **What I could NOT find: any dated record of phylloxera arriving in Campania specifically**, or any survey of how
  much of the region it reached. Do not write a date for Campania.
**Safe wording:** "The louse travels through soil; in sand it cannot. Where the ash and pumice lie deep and loose —
the Campi Flegrei, the lower slopes of Vesuvius, parts of Ischia — the vines were never grafted, and the same is true
of sandy ground with no volcano anywhere near it, in Portugal and in the Camargue."

### W13. "Vines on Vesuvius and in the Campi Flegrei still grow ungrafted, on their own roots, some of them well over
a century old"
**Verdict: CONFIRMED in substance, but every source is press or producer, and the *best* documented old ungrafted
vines in Campania are somewhere the reading does not mention.**
- Campi Flegrei: «un'elevata concentrazione di viti a piede franco», «viti centenarie a piede franco che hanno
  resistito all'attacco della fillossera», vineyards generally at 150–200 m.
  URL: https://www.assovini.it/italia/campania/item/147-campi-flegrei-doc **[promotional]**
- Vesuvius: Casa Setaro farms **12 ha of ungrafted vines at 200–350 m**; Cantine Olivella 15 ha planted on Monte
  Somma; Le Lune del Vesuvio 4 ha at about 300 m inside the national park. **[producer/press]**
  URL: https://www.cibotoday.it/citta/napoli/vini-vulcanici-vesuvio-campania-cantine.html
- There is a standing international conference on the subject — «Viticoltura a piede franco. Incontri internazionali
  tra Campi Flegrei e Vesuvio», held 22–24 January 2025 at Bacoli — which is evidence that the phenomenon is real
  enough to organise around, and gives the rewrite a dated, checkable hook.
  URL: https://www.lucianopignataro.it/a/viticultura-a-piede-franco-incontri-internazionali-tra-campi-flegrei-e-vesuvio-22-24-gennaio-2025/266871/ **[press]**
- **The oldest ungrafted vines claimed in Campania are at Tramonti on the Amalfi coast, not on the volcanoes**: Tintore
  stumps reported at **300 to 500 years old**, on terraces at 300–600 m, yielding under 50 q/ha, picked in early
  November. These are claims made by the estate and repeated by trade press; there is no dating study behind them.
  URLs: https://www.vinitenutasanfrancesco.com/secular-vines/ **[producer]** ,
  https://www.civiltadelbere.com/tintore-di-tramonti-da-vigne-secolari-a-piede-franco/ **[press]**
**Recommendation:** keep "ungrafted", attribute the ages, and do not write "the only place in Europe" or similar.

### W16. Aglianico: the name and the Greek story
**Claim:** "The name was long explained as a corruption of *Hellenic*… though the linguistics are now doubted and the
vine may simply be native."
**Verdict: CONFIRMED, and the reading is being *more* cautious than it needs to be — the evidence against is
specific and citable.**
- **The name does not appear before the 15th century.** «There is also no record of the name Aglianico prior to the
  15th century Spanish conquest of southern Italy», and it first appears in print as **«Aglianiche» in 1520**.
- Three etymologies in circulation: *vitis hellenica*; *Apulianicum*, a Roman name for southern Italy; and Spanish
  *llano*, plain. A fourth, from Manuela Piancastelli, derives it from Greek *agleukinosicos*, "wine without sugar".
- **Modern DNA analysis "does not support" the Greek origin, "revealing little relation to other Greek grape
  varieties"**, and if the vine did come from Greece, no original Greek planting survives.
- The traditional story names the **Phocians** bringing it to Cumae in the 8th century BC — i.e. the very colony in
  W3. It is a story, not a record.
- Aglianicone is **not** a clonal mutation of Aglianico, but DNA does show a close genetic relationship.
URLs: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aglianico **[reference]** ,
https://www.wine-searcher.com/grape-5-aglianico **[press]**
**Recommendation:** name the 1520 first printing and the absence of the name before the Spanish period. That single
fact does more work than "the linguistics are now doubted", because it is checkable and it points at a *Spanish*-era
coinage for a vine everyone calls Greek.

### W18. Fiano and *vitis apiana*
**Claim:** "Pliny may have known it as *vitis apiana*, the vine of the bees, for its sweet ripe grapes."
**Verdict: CONFIRMED as a hypothesis, correctly hedged in the reading — keep the hedge.**
- *Vitis apiana* is named by **Columella and Pliny the Elder** in the 1st century AD; the wine was *Apianum*; the name
  is from *apis*, bee, for the sweetness that attracted them.
- That Fiano descends from it is a hypothesis and is discussed, not settled. The same ancient name has also been
  attached to Muscat Blanc and Muscat of Alexandria, "for which there is no evidence" — which shows how loosely the
  ancient name is being used.
URLs: https://glossary.wein.plus/vitis-apiana **[reference]** , https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fiano_(grape) **[reference]**
- **The link is, however, written into Italian law**: the DOCG permits the traditional mention **«Apianum»** on the
  label beside the denomination, in type no more than half the height of the denomination itself. That is a very good
  detail: the hypothesis has a legal existence.
  URL: https://www.agraria.org/vini/disciplinarefiano.htm **[reference — disciplinare mirror]**

### W19. "Greco, brought from Thessaly according to tradition"
**Verdict: DISPUTED — must be staged as tradition, and the reading is missing the DNA finding that actually matters.**
- The tradition (Pelasgians or Thessalians bringing the vine to Campania) is repeated everywhere and evidenced
  nowhere. Wikipedia's grape article notes ampelographers cannot even agree whether Greco is one variety with clones
  or several varieties bundled under one name.
- **The DNA finding: plantings called "Greco" in Campania are genetically identical to Asprinio** — the grape of the
  Aversa poplars (W37) — while being **distinct from the Greco Bianco of Calabria**, with which it is still widely
  confused "both in the academic and professional spheres". Asprinio and Greco Bianco remain listed as separate
  varieties by the Italian authorities.
URLs: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greco_di_Tufo **[reference]** , https://www.wine-searcher.com/grape-2203-greco **[press]**
**This is a strong, under-used fact:** the austere hill white of Tufo and the thin sharp wine picked off
fifteen-metre poplars on the plain near Aversa are, on the evidence, the same grape.

### W20. "a faintly bitter, smoky finish **from the sulphurous tuff it grows in**"
**Verdict: UNSUPPORTED, and the best source available explicitly declines to make the link.**
Pignataro's piece on Tufo sets up the historical succession — «prima le miniere, adesso il vino», first the mines, now
the wine — and notes that some vineyards sit directly above the abandoned galleries, **but describes no chemical
relationship between the sulphur and the wine**. Producer and promotional pages do assert that the sulphurous subsoil
gives the wine its minerality; that is marketing, not evidence.
URLs: https://www.lucianopignataro.it/a/tufo-dalle-miniere-di-zolfo-al-greco/681/ **[press]** ,
https://www.si-wine.it/blogs/si-wine-blog/greco-di-tufo-cru-di-cantine-di-marzo-tufo-av **[producer/retail]**
**Recommendation:** the coincidence is worth telling — a village that mined sulphur under the vineyards — but write it
as a coincidence of place, not as a flavour mechanism. "Tasters reach for sulphur and smoke when they describe it, in
a village that mined sulphur; nobody has shown the two are connected."

### W21. Falanghina: "the everyday one… grown around the Campi Flegrei and in the Sannio hills"
**Verdict: CONFIRMED but incomplete — there are two Falanghinas and the reading treats them as one.**
- **Falanghina Flegrea and Falanghina Beneventana are distinct varieties, not clones.** DNA work (reported as
  **Costantini et al., 2005**) established the distinction; they differ in **34 of 40 ampelographic characters**.
  Flegrea dominates the Campi Flegrei on volcanic soil; Beneventana is the inland grape of Benevento on clay and
  limestone. **[NOTE: I could not open the Costantini paper itself; the citation comes from wine-education sources.
  Verify before printing the author-and-year.]**
  URLs: https://mag.sommtv.com/2022/08/falanghina/ **[press]** ,
  https://www.vinorandum.com/grapes/falanghina-flegrea **[reference]**
- Etymology: from Latin **falangae**, the stakes vines were tied to — the "staked vine". A competing derivation from
  *falangina*, finger-bones, for the leaf shape, also circulates. Both are **[reference-level, unevidenced]**.
  URL: https://www.quattrocalici.it/vitigni/falanghina/ **[reference]**
- **Beware one claim you will meet:** that Falanghina is "the daughter of the ancient Falerno grape". That is
  presented as an ampelographic hypothesis on Italian wine pages and is not established. Do not repeat it. (Same URL.)
- Scale: Falanghina covers about **5% of Campania's vineyard** and is the region's most planted white.
  URL: https://www.quattrocalici.it/vitigni/falanghina/ **[reference]**

### W22. "**Coda di Volpe**, 'fox's tail', named for the curved shape of its bunch, is the traditional white of Vesuvius"
**Verdict: the etymology is CONFIRMED; the sentence is now WRONG about Vesuvius, and this is the single biggest
content change since the reading was written.**
- Etymology confirmed: Pliny's **«cauda vulpium»**, a variety he lists as suited to pergola training; bunches long,
  tapering, compact, up to 40 cm, with two large wings.
  URLs: https://www.quattrocalici.it/vitigni/coda-di-volpe/ **[reference]** , https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coda_di_Volpe **[reference]**
- **But the Vesuvian white is Caprettone, a different variety.** «Caprettone» was long confused with Coda di Volpe;
  morphological and genetic studies showed substantial differences and "definitively disproved the theory of
  kinship"; DNA work in the early 21st century showed them to be distinct and not closely related. **Caprettone was
  admitted to the National Register of vine varieties on 20 October 2014**, and is present **only in the province of
  Naples**, on the slopes of Vesuvius. Robinson et al. (2013) raised the further hypothesis of close kinship between
  Caprettone, **Ginestra** and **Piedirosso napoletano**.
  URLs: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caprettone **[reference]** ,
  https://www.assovini.it/blog/item/2424-caprettone-lo-scugnizzo-doc-del-vesuvio **[promotional]** ,
  http://catalogoviti.politicheagricole.it/scheda.php?codice=493 **[official register entry]**
- **The law has followed the science.** The 2020 modification of the Vesuvio DOC created the types **Vesuvio
  Caprettone** and **Vesuvio Caprettone spumante** (reported as 80% Caprettone plus up to 20% Falanghina and/or
  Greco). Meanwhile the *Lacryma Christi Bianco* article still reads «Coda di volpe (localmente detto Caprettone o
  Crapettone)» — i.e. the old rulebook language treats them as one grape while a new article treats them as two.
  URLs: https://www.quattrocalici.it/tipologie-vino/vesuvio-doc-caprettone/ **[reference]** ,
  https://www.disciplinare.it/vesuvio-doc.html **[reference — disciplinare mirror]**
**Recommendation:** this is a gift. A grape that was called something else for a century, separated by DNA, entered in
the register in 2014, and written into the appellation in 2020 — with the old name still fossilised inside the same
rulebook.

### W23. Lacryma Christi: the name

  English-language page asserts that "already in the 17th century the name appeared in documents" and that variant

  *De naturali vinorum historia* (Rome, 1596), both of which would be the right places to look; **someone with access

  URLs: https://storienapoli.it/2020/12/02/lacryma-christi-vino-storia/ **[press]** ,

  vineyards, or documenting a Mastroberardino replanting decision after it.** The Wine Scholar Guild's history of
  Taurasi does not mention the earthquake at all. Either find a primary source or write only what is documented: that

    distributed worldwide. **Flagged disagreement** between sources that call 1999 the first harvest and those that
    call 2001 the first vinification.
  - Scale: about **1.5 ha across fifteen areas of the ancient city**, planting density given as **7,000 vines/ha**
    (vinodabere) or **8,000** (Wikipedia) — **flagged disagreement** — against a potential yield of about 40 q/ha.
  URLs: https://vinodabere.it/villa-dei-misteri-il-progetto-vinicolo-di-mastroberardino-a-pompei/ **[press]** ,

## Part B — candidate openings

Ten, each with a named person or institution, a date, a documented act, and a source. Ordered by strength of sourcing.
Every one of them opens on something happening, not on a grape.

### B1. **1866, Tufo: a man on a horse sees a stone burning** *(best in the file)*
**Francesco Di Marzo**, riding through his own country, notices a peasant woman burning a stone. It is sulphur. Within
a few years the ground between **Tufo and Altavilla Irpina** is one of the biggest industrial sites in the south, with
**1,300 men underground** (or 300 at Tufo itself, depending on the source). American sulphur, and then sulphur scraped
out of oil refineries, kills it after the war; extraction stops around **1972** and the last **seven** men leave about
**1983**. The same family had been growing Greco on the hill above since the 17th century, and the vineyards now sit
on top of the empty galleries.
**Documented:** the discovery and its year, the family, the two workforce figures, the cause of the decline, the range
of closure dates, the vineyards above the mines, the Di Marzo family's promotion of Greco, DOC 1970 and DOCG 2003.
**Not documented:** that the sulphur has anything to do with how the wine tastes (W20) — and that gap is itself the
paragraph. **Do not** close the loop the reader expects.
URLs: comune di Tufo; Pignataro; Provincia di Avellino (W35).
*Chain:* a burning stone → a mine → a village with money and a railway station → wine shipped out by rail to France →
a French idea about bottle fermentation → metodo classico Greco di Tufo in a glass today (W34).

### B2. **1996, Pompeii: a laboratory asks a wine family for help with some pips**
Grape seeds come out of the excavation near the **Villa dei Misteri**. The Soprintendenza's **Laboratorio di Ricerche
Applicate**, under **Anna Maria Ciarallo**, calls in the Mastroberardino family. Plaster casts of the voids left by
2,000-year-old vine stakes tell them exactly where the stakes stood; chestnut poles go back into the same holes, rows
north–south, at the AD 79 ground level. The seeds' DNA points to Piedirosso and Sciascinoso. First harvest **September
1999**; the **2001** vintage yields **1,721 bottles**.
**Documented:** the year, the institution, Ciarallo by name, the casts-and-stakes method, the varieties, the bottle
count, the 2008 extension to Aglianico and the 2011 blend, the 1.5 ha.
**Not documented / flagged:** 7,000 vs 8,000 vines per hectare; 90/10 vs 85% Piedirosso; whether 1999 or 2001 counts
as the first wine.
URLs: vinodabere; Civiltà del bere; Wikipedia (W29).
*Chain:* pips in the ash → a hole where a stake stood → the same hole, used again → a wine that is an argument about
what grew there → Piedirosso and Sciascinoso back in the Vesuvian rulebook (W24).

### B3. **The 1960s to now: sixteen thousand hectares of vines up poplar trees, and what is left**
Between Naples and Caserta the vine was married to the poplar: **fifteen metres up, twenty in places**, no wires, one
plant covering **200 m²** and carrying **220 kg**, every operation done from a one-man ladder called a *scalillo*. In
the **1960s** the system covered about **16,000 hectares**. Today it is **under two hundred**, and the Aversa DOC's
whole territory is about **32**. The grape is Asprinio — which DNA says is **Greco**, the same variety that makes the
serious white of Tufo.
**Documented:** the training system and its measurements, the DOC of 31 July 1993, the 22 comuni, the collapse in
area, the Slow Food Presidium, pre-phylloxera ungrafted stumps among the poplars, the Greco/Asprinio identity.
**Not documented:** whether the 16,000 figure has one original source behind its two appearances; how many growers
remain.
URLs: disciplinare.it; Enolò; Editoriale Domani; Slow Food; WineNews (W42, W19).
*Chain:* a vine trained up a tree because the land below had to grow something else → a wine made to be sharp and
cheap → mechanisation arrives and the ladder cannot compete → 16,000 ha become 200 → the same grape, on a hill
seventy kilometres east, sells as a serious white.

### B4. **1954, Ischia, Tomb 168: the oldest joke about drinking in the West**
A clay cup comes out of the San Montano cemetery at **Pithekoussai**, the Euboean trading post in the Bay of Naples,
with three scratched lines in one of the earliest Greek alphabets: *I am the cup of Nestor, good for drinking.
Whoever drinks from this cup, desire for fair-crowned Aphrodite will seize him instantly.* Second half of the **8th
century BC** — before Rome is a city. A 2021 study of the cremated bone in the tomb found **at least three people**,
undoing the long-told story of the boy buried with his cup.
**Documented:** the find year, the tomb, the site, the dating, the inscription, the 2021 osteological revision.
**Not documented:** anything about what was *in* the cup, or which vines the settlers had.
URLs: Wikipedia; PLOS ONE via PMC (W2).
*Chain:* a cup on an island in the bay → wine as something worth writing a joke about → colonies whose name for the
south was Oenotria (contested, W4) → 2,700 years later the same islands still grow vines nobody had to graft (W12).

### B5. **2014: the grape on Vesuvius turns out to have been someone else all along**
On **20 October 2014**, **Caprettone** is entered in Italy's national register of vine varieties. For a century it had
been sold and described as **Coda di Volpe**; morphological and genetic work separated them and disproved the
kinship outright. It grows **only** in the province of Naples, on the volcano. In **2020** the Vesuvio DOC was
modified to create a Caprettone type — while the article covering Lacryma Christi Bianco still reads «Coda di volpe
(localmente detto Caprettone)», the old mistake fossilised in the same rulebook that now corrects it.
**Documented:** the register date, the DNA distinction, the restriction to the Naples province, the 2020 DOC types,
the surviving old wording, the Robinson et al. (2013) kinship hypothesis with Ginestra and Piedirosso napoletano.
**Not documented:** how much Caprettone is planted; whether any producer resisted the change.
URLs: Wikipedia; assovini; catalogoviti register entry; quattrocalici; disciplinare.it (W22).
*Chain:* a name used loosely for a century → a DNA test → a register entry → a new line in a law → a label in a shop
that says something different from the label beside it.

### B6. **23 November 1980, 19:34** *(use with care)*
The ground moves for what felt like ninety seconds; three shocks inside forty seconds, **Mw 6.9**, **2,914 dead**,
**280,000 homeless**, **688 comuni** damaged and half of them with no housing stock left; landslides at Calitri,
Caposele, Calabritto, Senerchia. The reconstruction emptied the countryside further and pushed people out of farming.
**Documented:** all of the above, from CNR, Protezione Civile and Wikipedia.
**NOT documented, and the reading currently asserts it:** any effect on cellars or vineyards, and the Mastroberardino
replanting decision (W29). **Either find a primary source or open on the depopulation, which is documented, and let
the vineyards enter as what was rebuilt afterwards.**
URLs: it.wikipedia Terremoto dell'Irpinia; CNR; Protezione Civile (W29).

### B7. **28 January 2014: the man who produced half a region's DOC wine dies at 86**
**Antonio Mastroberardino**, tenth generation of a house founded in **1878**, the "grape archaeologist". He rebuilt
after depression, phylloxera and war and refused Cabernet and Chardonnay when refusing them was eccentric; for much of
that time his house made **more than half of all Campania's DOC wine**. The **1968 Taurasi Riserva**, made with his
brother Walter, was named one of Wine Spectator's **twelve wines of the century in 1999**. In **1994** the brothers
split: Antonio kept the name and the winery, **Walter took much of the vineyard** and founded **Terredora**.
**Documented:** the dates, the founding year, the half-of-Campania figure, the 1999 list, the split and its terms.
**Not documented:** that he "saved" any variety single-handed — the sources credit him with the decision and the
market, not with the genetics; the two-hectares-of-Fiano figure (W32) rests on a weak source.
URLs: Wine Spectator; Decanter; Wikipedia; Opening a Bottle (W29, W32).

### B8. **1989: an argument about which grape may be called by a Roman name**
Two traditions claimed **Falerno** — one built on Aglianico, one on Primitivo, split geographically across the
Massico: Aglianico and Piedirosso around Sessa Aurunca and Cellole, Primitivo between Mondragone and Falciano. The DOC
decree of **1989** did not choose; it wrote three wines into one name. Meanwhile the ancient wine the name belongs to
was **white**, was divided by Pliny into **three slopes** of which the middle was best, was said to catch fire when a
flame was held to it, was still being poured **sixty years** after the 121 BC vintage, cost **four asses** in a
Pompeian bar when ordinary wine cost one, and **cannot be traced to any grape now grown**.
**Documented:** all of the above, including the Gazzetta citation for the 1989 decree.
**Not documented:** any continuity of variety between the ancient wine and the modern DOC — and Wikipedia states flatly
that the Aglianico suggestion is unlikely, since Falernian was white (W38).
*Chain:* a famous name with nothing attached to it → two modern grapes competing for it → a decree that splits the
difference → a bottle whose label is a legal compromise about antiquity.

### B9. **2005: Falanghina turns out to be two grapes**
DNA work (reported as **Costantini et al., 2005**) separated **Falanghina Flegrea** from **Falanghina Beneventana**:
distinct varieties, not clones, differing in **34 of 40 ampelographic characters** — one on volcanic sand by the sea
west of Naples, one on clay and limestone in the hills inland, both sold under one word on a wine list.
**Documented:** the distinction and the 34/40 figure, in wine-reference sources.
**NOT documented by me:** the paper itself. **Verify the citation before printing the author and year.** (W21)

### B10. **22–24 January 2025, Bacoli: an international conference about vines nobody grafted**
Growers from the Campi Flegrei and Vesuvius, and from other sand country, meet to compare notes on **viticoltura a
piede franco** — the vines the louse never reached. The mechanism is not the volcano: it is the sand. Below roughly
**3% clay**, or above roughly **65–70% sand**, phylloxera's galleries collapse and it dries out, which is why the
same vines survived at **Colares** in Portugal and in the **Camargue**, where there is no volcano at all.
**Documented:** the conference and its dates, the soil thresholds, the Colares and Camargue parallels, the Campi
Flegrei's ungrafted centenarian vines, Casa Setaro's 12 ha at 200–350 m.
**Not documented:** any census of how many ungrafted hectares Campania has; any date for phylloxera's arrival in
Campania. (W12, W13)
