# Emilia-Romagna 4 — Bologna la Grassa and the sfoglia. Outline and ledger (2026-09-14)

Status: batch region, first batch. Title stays. Summary now: *Europe's oldest university, 62 kilometres of
porticoes, two leaning towers, and a sheet of egg pasta rolled so thin you can see the table through it.
Bologna the learned, the red and the fat.* (a list). Proposed: **In Bologna a sheet of egg dough is rolled
until you can read a newspaper through it, and almost everything the city eats is cut from it.**

## Paragraphs

1. **Lead, a scene.** Early morning in a kitchen off the Quadrilatero. A woman heaps flour on a wooden board,
   breaks eggs into a well, kneads, and rolls with a pin a metre long, turning and stretching, until the sheet
   covers the board and the grain of the wood shows through it. Twenty minutes for a sheet a metre across. She
   is a sfoglina, and the sheet is the sfoglia. From it come the tagliatelle, the tortellini and the lasagne.
   The city's three nicknames: the learned, for the university of 1088; the red, for its roofs and its
   politics; the fat, for a kitchen that treats butter, pork and egg pasta as the food groups.
2. **From the sheet** (label). Tagliatelle cut to eight millimetres and dressed with ragù, the slow sauce of
   beef, pancetta, soffritto, a little tomato, wine and milk that the rest of the world calls bolognese and puts
   on spaghetti, which Bologna does not. Lasagne verdi, the sheet coloured with spinach. Tortellini folded
   round a finger and served in capon broth at Christmas, never with cream. The Chamber of Commerce recipes:
   tagliatella 1972 (its width fixed at the 12,270th part of the Asinelli tower), tortellino 1974, ragù 1982:
   the fact that a city deposits its recipes with a chamber of commerce is the story; the numbers go to the box.
3. **The keyhole** (label). The legend, told as legend: an innkeeper at Castelfranco Emilia, midway between
   Bologna and Modena, peeps through a keyhole at Venus asleep and rushes to the kitchen to make her navel in
   pasta. The two cities disagree about the filling and the size, and the 1974 deposit was an attempt to
   settle it.
4. **Towers and porticoes** (label). A hundred towers in the twelfth century, built as fortresses and boasts;
   two left. The Garisenda began to sink while it was being built and leans four degrees; Dante compared it to
   a giant bending over him; in 2023 it was fenced off. The Asinelli beside it, 97 metres, 498 steps. The
   porticoes, required by law from 1288 so that houses could grow over the street, 62 kilometres, the longest
   climbing 3.8 kilometres and 666 arches to San Luca; UNESCO 2021. Piazza Maggiore and the unfinished front of
   San Petronio, marble halfway up and brick above, with the city's story about the Pope. The Quadrilatero
   market since Roman times. Numbers to the box where they do not carry a story.
5. **The rest of the fat city** (label). Mortadella (ordinance 1661, cubes of fat, the ancestor of American
   bologna and nothing like it), cotoletta alla bolognese, friggione, certosino in winter, crescentine and
   tigelle, Lambrusco or Pignoletto. Three at most in prose with verbs; the rest to the box.
6. **Ending, a scene.** The Osteria del Sole off the Quadrilatero, wine since 1465 and no food at all: you
   bring your own from the stalls outside, spread it on the long tables, order a glass. Callback to the
   morning kitchen: the sheet rolled at dawn is on the table by noon.
