# PART A — READING 3, "THE FOUR ROMAN PASTAS"

## Item 1 — The four-dish family order, and the documentary record

### F1. "Ask a Roman to name the city's dishes and four pastas come first, in a family order that everyone agrees on: gricia, cacio e pepe, amatriciana and carbonara."
**VERDICT: CORRECTED.** The sequence is a modern narrative device, not a documented lineage, and it is not even
internally agreed. The same popular sources that call gricia "the oldest" also define it as "cacio e pepe with
guanciale added", which puts cacio e pepe first; both orderings circulate freely, which is what happens when a
story is reasoned backwards from a modern menu rather than forwards from evidence. The only documented relation
among the four is **amatriciana ← gricia**: the tomato-less version is attested alongside the tomato one in
1929/30 and was still standard at Amatrice into the 1960s. Carbonara's documentary trail is separate and later.
Representative example of the device in circulation: https://cardoon.com/journal/the-four-roman-pastas-and-how-theyre-connected

### F2. Earliest datable print appearance — AMATRICIANA (the oldest of the four on paper)
**VERDICT: 1916 for the name; 1927–29 for the first recipe.** Luca Cesari's finding: the name appears "nel 1916
nella rivista mensile 'Noi e il mondo' (supplemento del quotidiano 'La Tribuna')" ("in 1916 in the monthly
magazine *Noi e il mondo*, supplement of the daily *La Tribuna*"), followed the next year by an article in *La
Stampa*. The first recipe, per Cesari, is **Ada Boni, *Il talismano della felicità***: minced guanciale browned
with lard and onion, then fresh peeled tomatoes, dressed with pecorino or parmigiano and pepper, Boni calling it
"caratteristico della cucina romana".
**Date discrepancy, unresolved:** Cesari gives 1927; English Wikipedia's text says 1928 while its own bibliography
says 1929. **Do not print a single year as certain** — write "the late 1920s".
https://www.ricettestoriche.it/2018/10/25/amatriciana-storia-origini-e-aneddoti-di-una-ricetta-mitica/ ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ada_Boni
[not seen directly: the 1916 magazine]

### F3. Earliest datable print appearance — CACIO E PEPE
**VERDICT: 1929/30, Ada Boni, *La cucina romana*, Rome, Edizioni di Preziosa, p. 46** — the earliest that could be
reached, not provably the earliest that exists. Two independent sources place it in that book; English Wikipedia
supplies the page. **Whether the phrase appears in print before 1900 is genuinely open**: no source consulted cites
any pre-1900 occurrence, and the systematic full-text check was blocked (Google Books 429, HathiTrust 403).
https://guerrainfame.it/ricette/cucina_romana ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cacio_e_pepe

### F4. Earliest datable print appearance — GRICIA
**VERDICT: UNVERIFIABLE, and this is the thinnest evidence of the four.** No source reached gives a first printed
appearance of the word *gricia*/*griscia*. The anchors that exist are all indirect:
- **Ada Boni, *La cucina romana* (1929/30)**: after the "matriciana" she gives a **"marchiciana"** variant, without
  tomato, with sliced guanciale. That is the dish, under another name.
- Buccini (2007) cites the name *spaghetti alla marchigiana* from the 1920s.
- **Gosetti della Salda, *Le ricette regionali italiane* (1967), p. 686**: at Amatrice the sauce was still made
  without tomato into the 1960s — i.e. identical to gricia.
- Carnacina–Veronelli, *La cucina rustica regionale* (1966), and Jannattoni carry "spaghetti alla griscia".
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pasta_alla_gricia ; https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pasta_alla_gricia

### F5. Earliest datable print appearance — CARBONARA (the chain, in order)
**VERDICT: CONFIRMED, and it runs 1939 → 1948 → 1950 → 1952 → 1954.**
1. **1939, the name in print.** *De Koerier*, a Dutch-language daily of the Dutch East Indies, **23 August 1939**,
   a Rome correspondent's piece "Sogno romano di una notte di mezza estate", describing two trattorie on piazza di
   Santa Maria in Trastevere and their specialities, among them "spaghetti alla carbonara" — **no recipe**. Found by
   Dutch journalists Edwin Winkels and Janneke Vreugdenhil; authenticated by Alberto Grandi and Luca Cesari.
   *This is the single most consequential find in the whole pass: it predates the American-rations story by five years.*
   [not seen directly — Gambero Rosso, which carries Cesari's article on it, returned 403 to every attempt; the
   citation comes through both Wikipedias. **Verify against the scan before printing as fact.**]
   https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spaghetti_alla_carbonara ; https://www.famigliacristiana.it/societa-valori/chi-ha-inventato-la-carbonara-origini-storia-mm0nfteg
2. **1948.** Renato Mucci, "La più bella piazza di Roma", *Il Giornale di Trieste*, 1 May 1948, p. 3. [not seen directly]
3. **1950, the Italian press.** *La Stampa*, **26 July 1950**, p. 3, article "Il Papa ha 'passato ponte'", on the
   Festa de Noantri in Trastevere and the trattoria Da Cesaretto alla Cisterna: **"Fu questo oste ad accogliere per
   primo gli ufficiali americani giunti in Trastevere parecchi anni or sono in cerca di spaghetti alla carbonara."**
   ("It was this innkeeper who first welcomed the American officers who came to Trastevere several years ago in
   search of spaghetti alla carbonara.") Note what the paper assumes: the officers were asking for a dish **that
   already had a name**, "several years ago". [archiviolastampa.it has a broken TLS certificate; quoted via two
   independent secondary sources] https://passionatefoodie.blogspot.com/2024/02/the-origins-of-spaghetti-alla-carbonara.html
4. **1952, the first printed RECIPE — and it is American.** **Patricia Bronté, *Vittles and Vice: An Extraordinary
   Guide to What's Cooking on Chicago's Near North Side*, H. Regnery Company, Chicago, 1952, p. 34**, describing the
   dish at Armando's (proprietors Armando Lorenzini and Pietro Lencioni): *"Boil 1 1/2 pounds of Tagliarini (thin
   wide noodles)… chop and fry 1/2 pound of Mezzina (Italian bacon). Drain the noodles and the bacon. Take 4 eggs
   and 1/4 pound of grated Parmesan cheese and lightly whip together. Mix everything together and toss over a
   flame. Serves four."* Tagliarini and Parmesan — Tuscan, not Roman. (Month disputed: Italian Wikipedia says
   December 1952, a blog says January; unresolved.)
   https://books.google.no/books/about/Vittles_and_Vice.html?id=hYcwAAAAMAAJ ; https://behindthehedge.wordpress.com/2025/08/06/living-tradition-carbonara-elizabeth-david-italy-iowa-coal-miners-and-chicago/
5. **1954, the first Italian recipe.** *La Cucina Italiana*, **August 1954**, printed as a reader request: "400 g
   spaghetti, 150 g pancetta, 100 g **gruyere**, one clove of **garlic**, two eggs, salt and pepper", the garlic
   crushed and then removed. Pancetta not guanciale, Swiss cheese not pecorino, garlic, no cream — a Milanese
   magazine's version of a Roman dish.
   https://www.lacucinaitaliana.com/italian-food/how-to-cook/carbonara-how-we-did-it-in-50s
6. **1954, England.** Elizabeth David, *Italian Food*, London, Macdonald, November 1954: "Maccheroni alla Carbonara
   (Macaroni with Ham and Eggs)" — ham/coppa in butter, beaten eggs, Parmesan. **After**, not before, the Italian
   magazine.
7. **1955, the first Italian COOKBOOK: Felix Dessì, *La signora in cucina*** — pancetta, eggs, Parmesan, pepper,
   "un uovo ogni etto di pasta, una cucchiaiata di parmigiano per ogni uovo". **Carnacina, *La grande cucina* (1960)**
   fixed the canon and is the first to specify **guanciale** — and also adds **cream**: "Qualche cucchiaiata di
   panna liquida freschissima e molto cremosa". *Guanciale and panna enter Italian print in the same recipe.*
   https://www.ricettestoriche.it/2018/03/11/la-storia-della-carbonara-capitolo-2-gli-esordi-1951-1960/

### F6. "Gricia … is the oldest of the four."
**VERDICT: NOT SUPPORTABLE as documentary history.** It is an inference from ingredients, not from documents:
because tomato reaches Italian pasta sauce late, a tomato-less amatriciana must logically precede the tomato one.
That is a sound culinary argument about *relative sequence* and a weak claim about *age*. On the documents, gricia
is the **worst**-attested of the four. The honest formulation: gricia is plausibly the older *form* of amatriciana,
and is the *latest-named* of the group.

### F7. The documentary order, for the rewrite
**[calculated] from F2–F5.** By first securely dated appearance of the NAME in print:
1. **Amatriciana** — 1916. 2. **Cacio e pepe** — 1929/30. 3. **Gricia** — the dish attested 1929/30 under another
name; the word itself undated. 4. **Carbonara** — 1939.
**All four are documented phenomena of the twentieth century. None has a securely dated printed existence before
1916.** Amatriciana, not gricia, is the oldest of the four on paper — the opposite of the popular story, and the
best single correction available to this reading.

## Item 2 — The transhumance framing

### F8. "Every autumn shepherds drove their flocks down… What they carried had to keep without a kitchen: cured pork, hard cheese, dried pasta, pepper."
**VERDICT: UNVERIFIABLE as stated, and contradicted in part by what IS documented about shepherds' diet.** The
transhumance itself is real and well documented (it is on the UNESCO intangible-heritage list). The *provisions
list* is the problem. Italian accounts of what transhumant shepherds actually ate describe bread, oil, cheese,
eggs, onion, garlic, herbs and wine, and dishes built on stale bread and whey — *pancotto*, *acqua sala*, ricotta
— not dried pasta dressed with pepper. They carried "cose che potessero conservarsi: pane rigorosamente cotto a
legna che durava molti giorni, cipolla, aglio, erbe aromatiche, olio e vino" ("things that could keep: bread
strictly wood-baked that lasted many days, onion, garlic, aromatic herbs, oil and wine"), the typical foods being
"pancotto, acqua sala, una fetta di pane con formaggio".
http://foggiaracconta.altervista.org/blog/tradizioni-tavola/tavola-i-pastori/

### F9. The pepper problem
**VERDICT: NO EVIDENCE FOUND FOR shepherds carrying pepper; the economic evidence runs against it — but do not
overstate the case.** John Munro's price series (University of Toronto) shows that even in "the Golden Age of the
English Labourer" a master mason or carpenter in 1438–39 London needed about **2.25 days' wages for one pound of
pepper**, an Oxford worker about three days'; Munro concludes that town artisans, labourers, small farmers and
peasants would "rarely if ever" have eaten highly spiced dishes. Shepherds sat below those wage levels.
https://www.economics.utoronto.ca/munro5/SPICES1.htm
**The honest limit:** pepper prices fell substantially after 1600, so the argument is *not* that pepper was
unobtainable in 19th-century Italy. The correct verdict is that the shepherd-with-pepper story is **undocumented**,
and that the documented shepherd diet does not include it.
**Note the self-contradiction in the standard source.** English Wikipedia's shepherd-origin sentence for cacio e
pepe rests on a single source (Jenn Harris, 2021) which supports the shepherd narrative **and** states that black
pepper was likely added much later because it was expensive and scarce. The origin story and its own footnote
disagree. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cacio_e_pepe

### F10. What Cesari, Grandi and Moyer-Nocchi actually argue, and who disputes them
- **Luca Cesari** (*Storia della pasta in dieci piatti*, Il Saggiatore, 2021). Method: dated printed sources,
  reconstructed dish by dish. Claims carbonara is "an American dish born in Italy"; that the traditional formula
  settles only at the end of the 1960s; that the charcoal-burner legend is anachronistic; that amatriciana
  originally contained onion. On his own limits: **"We don't know and perhaps never will."** He is also the
  researcher who produced the 1939 citation that damages the flat American-origin thesis, and he resists Grandi's
  absolutism: **"nessuno ha mai creato un piatto dal nulla"** ("nobody ever created a dish from nothing").
  https://www.ricettestoriche.it/2018/03/11/la-storia-della-carbonara-capitolo-2-gli-esordi-1951-1960/ ; https://winenews.it/it/la-carbonara-un-piatto-squisitamente-americano-parola-delluniversita-della-cucina-italiana_501428/
- **Alberto Grandi** (*Denominazione di origine inventata*, Mondadori, 2018), economic historian, Parma. Framework:
  Hobsbawm's invention of tradition. On carbonara: **"The first recipe for carbonara is dated in 1953 Chicago.
  Carbonara did not exist in Italy before; it's Italian-American."** His stated evidence is an argument from
  silence (no cookbook before the 1950s mentions it). **Two corrections to Grandi's own sentence:** the Chicago
  recipe is **1952**, not 1953; and if the 1939 mention holds, "did not exist in Italy before" is false. Grandi —
  who helped authenticate the 1939 find — has since argued the narrower case that Americans *transformed and
  popularised* an existing dish. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alberto_Grandi ; https://www.famigliacristiana.it/societa-valori/chi-ha-inventato-la-carbonara-origini-storia-mm0nfteg
- **Karima Moyer-Nocchi**: oral history of what Italians actually ate. Her interviews with women born under Fascism
  found lard rather than olive oil as the ordinary fat, pasta often unaffordable, and **cheese and pepper
  aspirational rather than staple**. Her line: **"Traditions are always things that are selected and dusted off
  from the past and embellished and sort of made into a collage of who we are."** This is the strongest available
  evidence against the shepherd-provisions story — but it is 20th-century testimony, not 18th.
  *No documented statement by her on carbonara specifically was found; do not attribute one.*
  https://ancestralkitchenpodcast.com/2022/04/29-true-historical-italian-food-with-karima-moyer-nocchi-author-of-chewing-the-fat/
- **Who actually pushed back, and it is not the academy.** The 2023 flashpoint was Marianna Giusti, "Everything I,
  an Italian, thought I knew about Italian food is wrong", *Financial Times*, 23 March 2023 (paywalled, 402).
  The loud responses were institutional and political: **Coldiretti**, the farm lobby — "Un attacco surreale ai
  piatti simbolo della cucina italiana" ("a surreal attack on the dishes that symbolise Italian cuisine") — which
  states its own economic motive, that "la mancanza di chiarezza sulle ricette made in Italy offre terreno fertile
  alla proliferazione di falsi prodotti alimentari italiani all'estero" **[promotional]**; and **Matteo Salvini**,
  then deputy prime minister. Il Post reports Grandi was called a **"traditore della patria"** and received **no
  scholarly rebuttal**. Context: the row broke the week Italy launched its UNESCO bid for Italian cuisine.
  https://www.ilfattoquotidiano.it/2023/03/27/financial-times-attacca-la-cucina-italiana-il-parmigiano-vero-e-del-wisconsin-la-durissima-replica-della-coldiretti-preoccupante/7110383/ ; https://www.ilpost.it/2023/04/29/grandi-carbonara/
- **The one evidence-based rebuttal found: Edward Behr** (*The Art of Eating*), and it is partial. Against Grandi's
  "pasta was only known in Naples until WWI", Behr notes Rome imported so much Neapolitan macaroni **in 1774** that
  local makers had imports prohibited; against the Parmigiano/Wisconsin claim, that Wisconsin cheesemakers' records
  show no Italian cheese production before 1940. *Relevant here:* dried pasta circulating as a traded commodity in
  1774 Rome is documented; shepherds carrying it is not. https://artofeating.substack.com/p/italian-food-really-is-italian-with
- **Massimo Montanari** is NOT a rebuttal of Grandi — they are collaborators. His position: **"non è mai esistito
  da noi un modello unitario, nazionale, di cucina"** ("there has never existed here a unitary, national model of
  cuisine"); against "the myth of origins" as the wrong question altogether.
  https://www.doppiozero.com/giorgio-boatti-intervista-massimo-montanari

## Item 3 — Guanciale

### F11. "Guanciale is pork jowl."
**VERDICT: CONFIRMED.** The EU Amatriciana Tradizionale STG spec defines it as "guancia fresca di suino pesante
rifilata a forma di triangolo partendo dalla gola" ("fresh cheek of heavy pig, trimmed into a triangle starting
from the throat"). https://www.disciplinare.it/amatriciana-tradizionale-stg-disciplinare-di-produzione.html

### F12. "cured with salt and black pepper for several weeks"
**VERDICT: CORRECTED on both halves.**
- **Pepper is typical, not definitional.** No specification makes black pepper mandatory. The Guanciale di Amatrice
  De.Co. sheet allows "pepe o peperoncino" on the exposed face, with "sale marino secco e/o zucchero e/o aglio" in
  the salting.
- **"Several weeks" understates it.** 30 days is the legal floor, not the norm: STG spec, "stagionatura: minimo 30
  giorni dalla salatura". The De.Co. sheet gives salting itself as "salagione a secco, per un periodo variabile dai
  3 ai 7 giorni" or "in salamoia… da 5 a 15 giorni", then "stagionatura per un periodo minimo di 30 giorni".
  **Write "at least a month, usually two or three."**
https://www.infodeco.it/portfolio-item/guanciale-amatrice/

### F13. "It is fattier than pancetta… Bacon is smoked and pancetta is belly."
**VERDICT: CONFIRMED on fat and cut; two cautions.**
- The STG spec says it explicitly: "bianco screziato rosso all'interno, con **prevalenza della parte grassa sulla
  parte magra**" ("with the fatty part predominating over the lean").
- **Caution 1, a trap:** do not distinguish them by cure length. Pancetta Piacentina DOP is matured "per un periodo
  non inferiore ai quattro mesi" — *longer* than guanciale's 30-day minimum. Distinguish by **cut and fat**.
  https://www.qualigeo.eu/prodotto-qualigeo/pancetta-piacentina-dop/
- **Caution 2:** "guanciale is never smoked" would be wrong. Smoking is permitted and traditional in parts of the
  Amatrice zone: "l'asciugatura può prevedere l'affumicatura, esclusivamente con essenze… vegetali". Write
  **"usually unsmoked"**. Also: **there is no "Guanciale Amatriciano IGP"** — it is protected only as a defined
  ingredient inside the STG, plus a municipal De.Co. and the regional PAT lists. Do not call it IGP or DOP.
- US bacon is defined by the belly, not by smoke: 9 CFR 319.107 requires curing and contains **no smoking
  requirement**. "Bacon is smoked" describes practice, not law. https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/9/319.107

## Item 4 — Pecorino Romano DOP

### F14. "aged at least five months"
**VERDICT: CONFIRMED, and there is a second number the reading omits.** MASAF disciplinare, Art. 2 lett. C),
verbatim: **"La stagionatura si protrae per almeno cinque mesi per il formaggio da tavola, per almeno otto mesi
per il formaggio da utilizzarsi grattugiato."** ("Ageing continues for at least five months for table cheese, at
least eight months for cheese to be used grated.") **Five months table, eight months grating** — and since this
reading is about grating it over pasta, eight is the relevant number.
https://www.masaf.gov.it/flex/files/e/9/a/D.5b13efd8a408e3661c36/Disciplinare_di_produzione_Pecorino_Romano.pdf
*Currency note:* a March 2026 amendment confirmed both minimums and added a "Riserva" tier at 18 months, for
wheels made between December and March. [reported by trade press; the EUR-Lex page would not render]
https://www.ruminantia.it/pecorino-romano-dop-nasce-la-riserva-ecco-cosa-cambia-nel-disciplinare/

### F15. "most of it is now made in Sardinia"
**VERDICT: CONFIRMED, and quantifiable: 92% in the 2024/25 campaign.** 39,000 tonnes produced, Sardinia taking
"il 92% del totale, contro l'8% di Lazio e provincia di Grosseto", across "quasi 9mila allevatori e 47 caseifici",
with 60% of saleable production exported. Consorzio data, reported July 2026. (An older Consorzio-sourced page
says "approssimativamente il 95%" — **quote 92% with the year attached**, not a round 95%.)
https://www.ansa.it/sardegna/notizie/2026/07/15/pecorino-romano-dop-39-milioni-di-chili-nellultima-campagna_61389b02-51bb-4930-a01b-37cf54124b44.html

### F16. "which the DOP allows along with Lazio and the province of Grosseto"
**VERDICT: CONFIRMED exactly.** Art. 1: "comprende l'intero territorio delle regioni della Sardegna, del Lazio e
della provincia di Grosseto". Note the asymmetry worth getting right: **all** of Sardinia and **all** of Lazio, but
only **one** Tuscan province. (Same MASAF URL as F14.)

### F17. The salting method
**VERDICT: CORRECTED — dry salting is tradition, not rule.** The spec permits brine: **"La salatura può essere
effettuata a secco e/o in salamoia."** It does not contain the words "a mano", does not prescribe repetition, and
does not mandate dry salting. ONAF describes dry salting as the demanding traditional route: "a secco per
consentire una calibrazione ottimale della sapidità del prodotto… richiede tempi più lunghi e grande esperienza da
parte del casaro". **Correct wording: "traditionally dry-salted by hand over weeks, though the rules also allow
brine."** https://www.onaf.it/index.php?c=index&a=schedaformaggio&id=224

### F18. The Roman-era evidence — Columella
**VERDICT: the passage is REAL; the pecorino attribution is NOT.** Columella, *De re rustica* **VII.8**, is
genuinely titled *De caseo faciendo* and sits inside the book on sheep and goats. Opening: "Casei quoque faciendi
non erit omittenda cura utique longinquis regionibus, ubi mulctram devehere non expedit." On salting: "aspargitur
tritis salibus, ut exsudet acidum liquorem, atque ubi duratus est, vehementius premitur… et rursus torrido sale
contingitur." The quotable line: **"Verum optimus caseus est, qui exiguum medicaminis habet"** ("the best cheese is
the one that has least additive in it"). **Crucially the chapter names no cheese at all** — no regional
designation, nothing resembling "Pecorino Romano", a name postdating him by centuries. It describes a salted,
pressed sheep's-milk cheese; that is the honest limit. https://thelatinlibrary.com/columella/columella.rr7.shtml
*If a named-cheese citation is wanted:* Pliny, *Naturalis Historia* XI.97, does survey named cheeses by district,
e.g. "Cebanum hic e Liguria mittit ovium maxime lacte" — supporting "Romans ranked sheep's cheese by district",
but again naming nothing like pecorino romano. https://www.latin.it/autore/plinio_il_vecchio/naturalis_historia/!11!liber_xi/97.lat

### F19. The legionaries' ration — DO NOT PRINT
**VERDICT: UNVERIFIABLE; a marketing claim. [promotional]** The trail is circular and ends at the consortium,
which asserts it with no ancient citation: "fu stabilita anche la razione giornaliera da dare ai legionari… 27
grammi!" Italian Wikipedia repeats it footnoted to a cheese company and to the Consorzio itself; English Wikipedia
footnotes it to a magazine. **No classical source is ever cited by any of them.** Against it: Polybius VI.39, the
standard primary source on republican military rations, itemises **grain only** — "The allowance of corn to a
foot-soldier is about two-thirds of an Attic medimnus a month…" — and mentions no cheese; Columella VII contains
no reference to soldiers or rations.
https://www.pecorinoromano.com/pecorino-romano/cenni-storici ; https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Polybius/6*.html

## Item 5 — Gricia's name

### F20. "others [derive it] from the *gricio*, the old Roman word for the bread and grocery sellers who came from the Swiss Grisons"
**VERDICT: CONFIRMED — and this is the etymology the reference dictionary gives, the only one it gives.** The
*Vocabolario Treccani* entry **grìcia** derives the dish from "al modo dei **grici**", a name "popolarmente
indicata a Roma nel sec. 19º una categoria di negozianti" of bread and foodstuffs, held to come from the Swiss
**Grigioni** (Ladin *Grischum*), from Valtellina, or possibly Friuli. Treccani defines the dish as "tipico piatto
laziale in cui la pasta… è condita con guanciale tagliato a dadini **o salsiccia sbriciolata** e abbondante
pecorino grattugiato", and describes it as the earlier, tomato-less version of amatriciana.
**Two things for the rewrite: Treccani does not mention Grisciano at all, and it allows sausage as an alternative
to guanciale.** https://www.treccani.it/vocabolario/gricia/
The usual mechanism: the *grici* came from Valtellina, then a subject territory of the Grisons, so the demonym
transferred to the traders — stated in Roman dialect lexicography (Ravaro, *Dizionario romanesco*, Newton Compton,
2005, p. 329). [Ravaro not seen directly; cite Treccani as the authority.]

### F21. "some derive it from Grisciano, a hamlet a few kilometres from Amatrice"
**VERDICT: CONTESTED, and the weaker of the two.** Three standard objections: the hamlet is tiny; the expected
adjective would be *alla grisciana*, not *alla gricia*; and the Amatrice connection is already explained by
amatriciana having been made without tomato there. The scholarly venue for the Grisciano case is **Antony F.
Buccini, "On *Spaghetti alla Carbonara* and related Dishes of Central and Southern Italy", in *Eggs in Cookery:
Proceedings of the Oxford Symposium on Food and Cookery 2006*, Prospect Books, 2007, pp. 42–43** — he argues *alla
grisciana* was reshaped to *alla gricia* by analogy with the occupational names *puttanesca, carbonara,
carrettiera*. **[not seen directly — the Oxford Symposium PDF 404s. Name it, do not quote it.]** Note also that the

### F22. "sometimes called *amatriciana bianca*"

## Item 6 — Cacio e pepe: the mechanism, and tonnarelli

### F24. The 2025 physics paper
**VERDICT: CONFIRMED, with full citation.** Giacomo Bartolucci, Daniel Maria Busiello, Matteo Ciarchi, Alberto
Corticelli, Ivan Di Terlizzi, Fabrizio Olmeda, Davide Revignas, Vincenzo Maria Schimmenti, **"Phase behavior of
Cacio e Pepe sauce", *Physics of Fluids* 37, 044122 (2025)**, AIP Publishing, published 1 April 2025.
**DOI 10.1063/5.0255841**, **arXiv:2501.00536** (submitted 31 December 2024, revised 29 April 2025). Several
authors at the Institute of Science and Technology Austria.
*Verified independently by me via the arXiv API, the arXiv abstract page and Crossref.*
https://arxiv.org/abs/2501.00536 ; https://doi.org/10.1063/5.0255841

### F25. What the paper actually found
**VERDICT: CONFIRMED, and the numbers are quotable.** From the abstract, verbatim: **"we delineate a regime where
starch concentrations below 1% (relative to cheese mass) lead to the formation of system-wide clumps, a condition
determining what we term the 'Mozzarella Phase' and corresponding to an unpleasant and separated sauce"**; and
they observe **"a lower critical solution temperature that we theoretically rationalized by means of a minimal
effective free-energy model"** — i.e. the sauce separates on *heating*, not cooling. The optimum, from the body:
**"The ideal range, as confirmed by both taste and texture tests, lies between 2% and 3%."** Without starch, "increasing
the temperature leads to the abrupt appearance of a giant dense phase", around **65 °C**, with the critical
temperature "slightly above 60 °C". Trisodium citrate at 2% and above gives full stabilisation with a sharp rather
than gradual transition. **The paper won the 2025 Ig Nobel Prize in Physics** "for discoveries about the physics of
pasta sauce, especially the phase transition that can lead to clumping". https://improbable.com/ig/winners/
**Caution:** two fetches of the arXiv HTML gave two different sets of gram quantities for the proposed recipe (both
2.5% starch-to-cheese). **Do not print grams without checking the published PDF; the ratio is safe.**

### F26. The practical chemistry — "too hot and the cheese seizes"
**VERDICT: CONFIRMED for protein aggregation and starch stabilisation; NOT a finding of the paper for fat
emulsification.** The paper: "at high temperatures, cheese proteins can either form clumps upon denaturation or
simply aggregate"; the Mozzarella Phase results from "extreme protein aggregation on heating"; cheese is "casein
organized in micelles together with calcium phosphate". Co-author **Daniel Maria Busiello** on how starch works:
starch **"binding to the cheese proteins, reducing their direct interactions and, consequently, their
aggregation."** The paper treats the solvent as "water, starch, salt, and lipids" but does not mechanistically
explain the lipid contribution — so treat "fat emulsification" as a kitchen explanation, not a finding.
https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/Researchers-have-the-perfect-cacio-e-pepe-recipe-down-to-a-science-literally-180986535/
*Peer-reviewed backing for the protein half if wanted:* Skelte G. Anema, "Heat-induced changes in caseins and casein
micelles…", *International Dairy Journal* 122 (2021), DOI 10.1016/j.idairyj.2021.105136.
*Note:* Serious Eats, McGee and the Guardian could not be fetched from this environment; if a Kenji/McGee line is
wanted in the prose, someone must open those pages by hand.

### F27. "Tonnarelli, a square-cut fresh egg spaghetti, is the traditional shape"
**VERDICT: CORRECTED — contested, and the shape is Abruzzese in origin.**
- What they are: **Treccani** derives the word from "tonderelli", diminutive of "tondo", and calls the format
  **characteristic of Abruzzo**: "pasta all'uovo" in a "formato lungo, a sezione quadrangolare… ritagliata da una
  falda di sfoglia di un certo spessore con lo speciale arnese detto **chitarra**" ("cut from a sheet of pasta of a
  certain thickness with the special tool called a *chitarra*"), which is why they are also called spaghetti alla
  chitarra. Dimensions: "la larghezza del taglio è di 2 mm e lo spessore è circa lo stesso" — **square in section,
  roughly 2–3 mm a side.** https://www.treccani.it/vocabolario/tonnarelli/ ; https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spaghetti_alla_chitarra
- Whether it is "the traditional shape": **most common today, not documentarily the traditional one.** Italian
  Wikipedia says only "Solitamente il tipo di pasta più usato è il tonnarello" ("usually the most used type"), and
  the oldest printed authority — **Ada Boni (1930), p. 46** — lists **tonnarelli or spaghetti**.
- **Suggested safe wording:** "Today it is most often tonnarelli — square-section egg pasta, the Roman cousin of
  Abruzzo's maccheroni alla chitarra — though spaghetti is just as traditional and stands beside it in Ada Boni's
  1930 *La cucina romana*."
- **Rigatoni/mezze maniche as traditional for cacio e pepe: UNVERIFIABLE.** Common in Roman restaurants; no source
  establishes it. Do not assert it.

## Item 7 — Amatriciana

### F28. "it belonged to Abruzzo until 1927"
**VERDICT: CONFIRMED, with three precisions the reading should absorb.**
- Before 1927, Amatrice was in the **mandamento di Amatrice, circondario di Cittaducale, provincia di Aquila degli
  Abruzzi**.
- The instrument is a **Royal Decree-LAW**, not a Royal Decree: **R.D.L. 2 gennaio 1927, n. 1, "Riordinamento delle
  circoscrizioni provinciali"**, published in *Gazzetta Ufficiale* **n. 7 of 11 January 1927**. Verified on
  Normattiva, the Italian state's own legislative database. Operative text: **"Provincia di Rieti con capoluogo
  Rieti, comprendente: i Comuni del circondario di Rieti ed i comuni già costituenti il soppresso circondario di
  Cittaducale."** **Amatrice is not named individually** — it moves as part of the Cittaducale block.
  https://www.normattiva.it/atto/caricaDettaglioAtto?atto.codiceRedazionale=027U0001&atto.dataPubblicazioneGazzetta=1927-01-11
- **Italy's regions did not exist as administrative bodies until 1970.** In 1927 this was a transfer between
  *provinces* (L'Aquila → the new Rieti), with Rieti assigned to the Lazio compartment. "Moved from Abruzzo to
  Lazio in 1927" is fair shorthand — and is how Amatrice itself puts it — but do not imply a regional government
  changed hands.
- Amatrice's own municipal document makes the culinary version: "La 'Salsa all'amatriciana' è legata alla storia
  d'Abruzzo fino alla data del 1927, anno in cui con la creazione della Provincia di Rieti vennero a determinarsi
  nuovi confini tra Lazio e Abruzzo." https://www.comune.amatrice.rieti.it/wp-content/uploads/2016/07/disciplinare_salsa.pdf

### F29. "the town's official recipe… explicitly excludes onion and garlic"
**VERDICT: CORRECTED — the explicit prohibition is in the EU text, NOT in the comune's own document. Do not
attribute it to the comune.**
The comune's actual document ("'SALSA ALL'AMATRICIANA' DISCIPLINARE DI PRODUZIONE", Comune di Amatrice) defines a
**De.Co.** in two versions, *bianca* ("denominata anche 'Gricia'") and *rossa*. Ingredients for the red version,
per 500 g of pasta, verbatim: "125 gr. di 'Guanciale Amatriciano DE.CO.'", "un cucchiaio di olio extravergine di
oliva", "un goccio di vino bianco secco", "6 o 7 pomodori San Marzano oppure 400 grammi di pomodori pelati di
qualità", "un pezzetto di peperoncino fresco o essiccato (eventuale)", "sale e pepe q.b." Serving: "Unire alla
salsa degli **spaghetti** di ottima qualità, cotti al dente… e aggiungere abbondante 'Pecorino di Amatrice' De.Co."
The pan is specified: **"una padella rigorosamente di ferro"** (a strictly iron pan), with a derogation for steel
in restaurants — a nice concrete detail. **There is no sentence forbidding onion or garlic; they are simply
absent from the list.** (Same comune PDF as F28.)
**Note also:** the reading says "in Amatrice itself they use spaghetti" — CONFIRMED by this document.

### F30. The STG registration
**VERDICT: CONFIRMED, date and name.** **Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2020/395 of 6 March 2020**,
entering **'Amatriciana Tradizionale'** in the register of traditional specialities guaranteed; published **OJ L 77,
13.3.2020, p. 1**. (Two dates: adopted 6 March, published/registered 13 March.) Application published OJ C 393,
20.11.2019; EU file TSG-IT-02390. Class 2.21, prepared meals.
**The explicit exclusion lives here, verbatim: "Unlike other similar sauces, 'Amatriciana Tradizionale' does not
provide for the use of ingredients such as garlic, onion or pork belly, which are used in most pasta condiments
and sauces."** (IT: "a differenza di altre salse analoghe non prevede l'impiego di ingredienti quali aglio cipolla
o pancetta.")
Compulsory ingredients: "18-30 % Amatrice pork jowl" (matured "at least 30 days"), "Extra virgin olive oil:
0,5-1 %", "69-81 % tomato 'passata' and/or peeled chopped tomatoes", salt, white wine, "Dried or fresh chilli
and/or pepper: to taste"; recommended "Grated Amatrice pecorino or Pecorino Romano PDO from Lazio" matured "not
less than 6 months". On shape the spec is deliberately loose: "traditionally spaghetti or bucatini, but also short
pasta". https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2020/395/introduction ; https://assets.publishing.service.gov.uk/media/665ed38ed470e3279dd33536/CELEX_52019XC1120_02__EN_TXT_Amatriciana_tradizionale.pdf

### F31. "the first pasta sauce with that protection"
**VERDICT: CONFIRMED as to *pasta sauce* — but it is Italy's THIRD STG, so do not let the sentence imply first
overall.** The Italian TSG set, each date verified: **Mozzarella**, Reg. (EC) 2527/98 of 25 November 1998 (cheese,
registered as an unreserved name); **Pizza Napoletana**, Reg. (EU) 97/2010 of 4 February 2010 (class 2.24, a
bakery product); **Amatriciana Tradizionale**, 2020; **Vincisgrassi alla maceratese**, Reg. (EU) 2022/509 of 24
March 2022 — a baked pasta *dish*, and later, so it does not undercut the claim. A pass through the register (67
registered names) found cured meats, cheeses, beers, breads and dumplings, and **no earlier pasta sauce**.
*Method caveat:* eAmbrosia returns an empty shell to automated fetching; checked via the UK mirror plus the
per-product EU records. https://www.gov.uk/protected-food-drink-names?register=foods-traditional-speciality-guaranteed

### F32. The 2016 earthquake
**VERDICT: CONFIRMED for date and total; the Amatrice figure is genuinely contested — hedge it.**
**24 August 2016, 03:36:32 local**, epicentre near **Accumoli**, about 9 km from Amatrice, depth ~8 km, **Mw 6.0
(INGV)**. Total deaths **299**, confirmed by the Dipartimento della Protezione Civile ("299 vittime").
**Amatrice specifically: sources disagree** — ANSA gives 237, Italian Wikipedia's infobox 239, RaiNews 228.
**Recommended wording: "299 people died, the great majority of them in Amatrice."** (The USGS 6.2 figure could not

https://www.protezionecivile.gov.it/en/approfondimento/terremoto-centro-italia--un-punto-a-sei-mesi-dall-inizio-dell-emergenza--versione-inglese-/

### F33. "restaurants across Italy put the dish on their menus to raise money"
**VERDICT: CONFIRMED on mechanism and originator, with one correction; the total raised is UNVERIFIABLE.**
- **Correction: Paolo Campana is not a chef.** He is a blogger/editor, and he launched **#AMAtriciana** on social

- Mechanism, verbatim: **"per ogni piatto ordinato verranno donati due euro"** — €2 per plate, **one euro from the

- Scale: Dissapore (26 August 2016) reported "oltre 600 ristoratori" in the first days; later accounts say about a

- **No published total was found. Do not state an amount.** (The Gambero Rosso interview with Campana, which would

### F34. "Tomatoes reached the dish in the eighteenth or nineteenth century"
**VERDICT: the two stories usually told for this are UNVERIFIABLE — do not repeat either.**
- **Francesco Leonardi's *L'Apicio moderno* (1790) does not contain an amatriciana**, so far as can be shown. What
  the sources support is a different claim — Leonardi is credited with the first printed pasta-with-tomato-sauce,
  the *culì di pomodoro*. A direct OCR check of two volumes of the second edition (Rome, 1807–08) found "Culì di
  Pomidoro" repeatedly and **no occurrence of "matriciana", "amatriciana", "Amatrice", "griscia" or "cacio e

- **The 1816 Quirinale banquet for Emperor Francis I "alla maniera dei matriciani": UNVERIFIABLE.** It circulates
  on tourism and cooking-school pages, none citing a menu, archive or document; Cesari's account does not include
  it. **[promotional]**
- **What IS documented and is more interesting:** the historic amatriciana **contained onion**. Cesari calls it
  "uno dei piatti più longevi" of the recipe's ingredients from Boni onward, and notes "oggi tende a essere
  esclusa" — the modern no-onion rule is a late codification, not the old recipe. The reading currently presents
  the exclusion as ancient orthodoxy; the opposite is true.

## Item 8 — Carbonara

### F35. "it took shape in Rome in 1944 when American rations of bacon and powdered egg met Roman pasta and pecorino"
**VERDICT: the Gualandi story is real as a testimony and UNVERIFIABLE as history — and a 1939 print mention
undercuts the invention claim entirely (F5).**
- **The man is documented.** Renato Gualandi (1921–2016), born San Lazzaro di Savena near Bologna; won the
  Littoriali del Lavoro for cooking in 1939 at eighteen; opened restaurant "3G" in Bologna in 1951. There is a
  book: Alberto A. Fabbri, *Renato Gualandi: peripezie di un cuoco bolognese*, Slow Food, 2006.
  https://www.bibliotecasalaborsa.it/bolognaonline/events/un-cuoco-bolognese-tra-gli-inventori-della-carbonara
- **The source is a single late interview.** *Il Corriere di Bologna*, **10 July 2009**; Gualandi was 88. His words:
  **"Gli americani avevano del bacon fantastico, della crema di latte buonissima, del formaggio e della polvere di
  rosso d'uovo. Misi tutto insieme e servii a cena questa pasta ai generali e agli ufficiali. All'ultimo momento
  decisi di mettere del pepe nero che sprigionò un ottimo sapore. Li cucinai abbastanza 'bavosetti' e furono
  conquistati dalla pasta."** ("The Americans had fantastic bacon, very good cream, cheese and powdered egg yolk. I
  put it all together and served this pasta at dinner to the generals and the officers. At the last moment I
  decided to add black pepper, which released an excellent flavour. I cooked them fairly runny and they were won
  over by the pasta.") **Recollection about 65 years after the fact, with no contemporary document — no menu, no
  diary, no 1944 paperwork.** https://www.ricettestoriche.it/2018/03/03/storia-della-carbonara-capitolo-5-linvenzione-della-carbonara/
- **The details drift between retellings**, which is the tell. Date and town are stable — **22 September 1944,
  Riccione** — but the venue is the Hotel Vienna in one account, the Grand Hotel Des Bains in another, the Domus
  Mea then the Vienna in a third; the guest list shifts between Alexander, Leese, Clark and Harold Macmillan.
  https://www.cronachedigusto.it/scenari/a-riccione-si-celebra-lo-chef-renato-gualandi-cosi-invento-la-mitica-carbonara/
- **The Rome leg is unestablished.** Italian Wikipedia has him cooking for the Allies in Rome from September 1944
  to April 1945, but the Bologna sources have him following the Allied army **to Bologna** and the Hotel Baglioni.
- **[promotional]** Riccione tourism promotes the 1944 origin, including a standing offer of free lodging to
  anyone producing an earlier recipe.

### F36. "The name suggests the *carbonai*, the charcoal burners of the Apennines"
**VERDICT: UNDOCUMENTED — the most repeated theory, with no pre-war text tying charcoal burners to this pasta.**
Cesari dismisses it: "there are no ancient progenitors of carbonara." The rivals are no better: the **Carboneria**
secret society (an attribution by the American writer John F. Mariani, no documentary link); ***carbonata***,
Renaissance salt-cured pork — whose proponent Jeremy Parzen concedes in his own words, **"While I have no solid
evidence of this, my philological intuition leads me to believe…"**; and the black-pepper-as-coal-flecks reading,
folk explanation only. **Alberto Capatti** offers the most useful framing, reading the name as register rather
than history: the name "probably suggests a mishmash of affordable ingredients, varying in kind and proportion,
but also the plain, natural tastes of people used to eating outdoors."
https://dobianchi.com/2012/10/29/carbonara-origin-name-meaning/ ; https://www.thedailybeast.com/the-mysterious-origins-of-carbonara-italys-most-fought-over-pasta/

### F37. "the first written recipes appear only after the Second World War"
**VERDICT: CONFIRMED** — see the chain at F5. The nearest documented ancestors are cheese-and-egg pastas:
Vincenzo Corrado, *Il cuoco galante* (1778), "cotte si possono servire legate con gialli d'uova"; Ippolito
Cavalcanti (1837/1839), "pasta cacio e ova"; Francesco Palma, *Il principe dei cuochi* (1881), "maccheroni con
cacio e uova" with *sugna* — the egg + cheese + pork-fat triad. Also the Touring Club's *Guida d'Italia* (1931),
strascinati from Cascia and Monteleone di Spoleto with whipped eggs, sausage and pork fat, but no cheese.
**Ada Boni's *La cucina romana* (1930) has no carbonara** — confirmed by three independent sources: "nel manuale di
cucina romana di Ada Boni, assoluto best seller nell'epoca in cui fu pubblicato (1930), questa ricetta non è
nemmeno accennata" ("is not even hinted at"). Nor does *Il cucchiaio d'argento* (1950).
https://www.ricettestoriche.it/2018/03/12/la-storia-della-carbonara-capitolo-1-i-precedenti/ ; https://ilovefoods.it/2016/05/romana-si-romana-no-la-vera-storia-dellamatriciana-della-carbonara/
*First film mention, a usable detail:* **Aldo Fabrizi** in *Cameriera bella presenza offresi* (1951), asking a job
applicant whether she can make "gli spaghetti alla carbonara".

### F38. "No cream, ever."
**VERDICT: the orthodoxy is younger than the reading implies.** **Carnacina (1960)**, the recipe that fixed the
canon and the first in Italian print to specify **guanciale**, also calls for **cream**: "Qualche cucchiaiata di
panna liquida freschissima e molto cremosa." Cesari dates the settling of the guanciale/egg/pecorino/no-cream
formula to the **end of the 1960s**. The rule is real and worth teaching — but as a modern convention, not an
ancient one.

### F39. "it is now the most cooked Italian dish in the world"
**VERDICT: UNVERIFIABLE — and the phrase is trade marketing. Cut it or attribute it.** No dataset measures this.
What exists: an **AstraRicerche** survey for **Unione Italiana Food** (the pasta makers' association), ~1,000
Italians, March 2026 — carbonara the favourite pasta of **46.1% of Italians**; #CarbonaraDay social metrics; Just
Eat delivery data ("quasi 23 mila kg" ordered in Italy in 2023); the Accademia Italiana della Cucina calling it
"la ricetta più interpretata all'estero" ("the most reinterpreted recipe abroad"). The superlative "il piatto più
amato e replicato al mondo" is **the headline of the pasta-makers' own press release** — the body that invented
Carbonara Day in 2017. **[promotional]**
https://www.welovepasta.it/press-room/carbonara-day-6-aprile-compie-70-anni-il-piatto-piu-amato-e-replicato-al-mondo-dal-1954-ad-oggi-ecco-la-lunga-storia-di-una-ricetta-sinonimo-di-liberta/

### F40. How to stage the disagreement honestly
Cesari and Grandi point the same way but are **not interchangeable** (F10): Cesari works dish by dish from dated
print and publishes his citations; Grandi is the polemicist, and his sharpest claim is off by a year (1952, not
1953) and undercut by the 1939 find that Cesari himself produced. And the pushback came from **a farm lobby, the
press and politicians — not from historians with counter-documents**. No named academic historian publishing a
scholarly rebuttal of the carbonara claim could be found. *The rewrite should stage that shape: an archive worker,
a polemicist who overstates him, and a trade body defending export value in its own words.*

# PART B — READING 4, "ROME: THE COLOSSEUM AND THE ETERNAL TABLE"

## Item 9 — Vespasian, Titus, Domitian

### F41. "The emperor Vespasian began it around AD 72"
**VERDICT: CORRECTED — defensible as a modern convention, but NO ancient source gives the year.** Suetonius names
the project without a date (*Vespasian* 9: "also an amphitheatre in the heart of the city, a plan which he learned
that Augustus had cherished"). The **Parco archeologico del Colosseo itself gives no year**: "It was built in the
1st century CE at the behest of the emperors of the Flavian dynasty." Modern handbooks vary between 70 and 72.
**Write "begun under Vespasian in the early 70s AD", not "around AD 72" as an attested date.**
https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Suetonius/12Caesars/Vespasian*.html ; https://colosseo.it/en/area/the-colosseum/

### F42. "on the drained lake of Nero's palace grounds"
**VERDICT: CONFIRMED, and the evidence is a line of poetry worth quoting.** Martial, *Liber spectaculorum* 2:
**"hic ubi conspicui uenerabilis Amphitheatri / erigitur moles, stagna Neronis erant"** ("here, where the mass of
the striking, venerable Amphitheatre rises, were Nero's pools"). The same poem closes **"Reddita Roma sibi est"**
("Rome has been given back to herself") — which is precisely the "deliberate gesture returning a private pleasure
park to the people" the reading asserts, and it can now be sourced instead of asserted.
https://www.thelatinlibrary.com/martial/mart.spec.shtml

### F43. "His son Titus opened it in AD 80 with a hundred days of games"
**VERDICT: CONFIRMED as Cassius Dio's figure — with a reliability caveat that should be visible in the prose.**
**Dio 66.25.4: "These were the spectacles that were offered, and they continued for a hundred days."** Also 66.25.1,
"animals both tame and wild were slain to the number of nine thousand".
**The caveat: Dio wrote in the early 3rd century, ~130–150 years later, AND Book 66 does not survive in his own
words** — books 61–80 reach us only through the 11th-century abridgement of the Byzantine monk John Xiphilinus. So
the hundred days is a number transmitted at two removes. (No modern scholar's explicit verdict on the figure was
found; do not claim scholars endorse or reject it.) Suetonius, *Titus* 7.3, gives the dedication and "quinque
milia omne genus ferarum" — five thousand beasts in a single day — but **no duration**.
https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Cassius_Dio/66*.html

### F44. "the younger son Domitian added the hypogeum"
**VERDICT: CONFIRMED, by the monument's own custodian — and the building was not finished in 80.**
The Parco: **"Built in wood for the Colosseum's inauguration, the underground levels were transformed into solid
masonry under Domitian."** The same page: the tunnels served "as docks for the naumachie (naval battles) which
took place in celebration of the monument's inauguration", and "when the wooden arena was substituted with
masonry, water shows were no longer held."
https://colosseo.it/en/marvels/the-underground-levels-of-the-colosseum/
Platner & Ashby on the phases: Vespasian "carried the structure to the top of the second arcade"; Titus "added the
third and fourth stories"; Domitian "completed the building ad clipea". **So "Titus opened it" and "the building
was finished" are not the same event.**
*The citable specialist:* **Heinz-Jürgen Beste, "The construction and phases of development of the wooden arena
flooring of the Colosseum", *Journal of Roman Archaeology* 13 (2000), 79–92, DOI 10.1017/S104775940001878X.** His

### F60. The line the rewrite must hold

## Item 17 — The Ghetto

### F66. "confined by papal decree from 1555 until 1870"
**VERDICT: CONFIRMED, and the bull is quotable.** ***Cum nimis absurdum*, Paul IV, 14 July 1555** — the dateline
reads "Datum Romae apud S. Marcum… **Pridie idus Julii**", i.e. 14 July. **(Italian Wikipedia's ghetto article says
12 July; that is inconsistent with the Latin. Use 14 July.)**
Incipit: **"Cum nimis absurdum et inconueniens existat ut iudaei, quos propria culpa perpetuae seruituti
submisit…"** ("Since it is exceedingly absurd and improper that the Jews, whom their own guilt has consigned to
perpetual servitude…").
What it imposed, in its own words: one quarter with **"ad quos unicus tantum ingressus pateat, et quibus solum
unicus exitus detur"** ("to which there is open only a single entrance, and from which only a single exit is
given"); forced sale of property, "bona immobilia… christianis uendere"; the distinguishing sign, "masculi
biretum, foeminae uero aliud signum patens… **glauci coloris**"; and restriction to rag-dealing, **"sola arte
strazzariae, seu cenciariae (ut uulgo dicitur) contenti"** ("content with the sole trade of *strazzaria*, or
rag-picking, as it is commonly called"). The bull also permitted only one synagogue, barred Christian servants,
and forbade Jewish physicians to "attend or take part in the care of Christians".
*Nuance worth a footnote: "glauci coloris" ordinarily means bluish-grey, yet the standard scholarly translations
render it "yellow", which is how the sign was applied in practice.*

**VERDICT: CONFIRMED, and separating them is the improvement.**
- **17 April 1848:** "papa Pio IX ordinò di abbattere il muro che circondava il ghetto" ("Pius IX ordered the
  demolition of the wall that surrounded the ghetto"). After the fall of the Roman Republic in 1849 the same pope
  compelled the Jews to return to the quarter, **"albeit now without gates and walls"**. So the physical wall went
  in 1848 and was never rebuilt; what returned in 1849 was the *legal obligation to live there*.
- **20 September 1870**, the breach of Porta Pia: "terminò il potere temporale dei papi, il ghetto fu
  definitivamente abolito e gli ebrei equiparati ai cittadini italiani".
- **The demolition of the quarter is a separate, later event**: planned under the 1883 master plan, **decided 1885**
  ("La demolizione del ghetto ebraico di Roma, decisa nel 1885"), **carried out from 1888** — "buona parte delle
  antiche stradine e dei vecchi edifici del ghetto, malsani e privi di servizi igienici, furono demoliti creando
  così tre nuove strade: via del Portico d'Ottavia…, via Catalana e via del Tempio".
  **The streets erased have names worth using:** via e piazzetta Rua, **via delle Azzimelle** (named for its matzo
  bakery), via della Fiumara, **piazzetta del Pancotto**, piazza Giudia. Ettore Roesler Franz photographed the
  demolition in 1890.
- The **Tempio Maggiore** followed: first stone 20 July 1901, inaugurated July 1904 (sources give 27 and 29 July —
  write "July 1904"), built "su uno dei quattro lotti di terreno ricavati demolendo le più fatiscenti aree del
  Ghetto", replacing the five Cinque Scole.
https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghetto_di_Roma ; https://romacentocinquanta.it/wp/percorso/ghetto-ebraico-trasformazioni-urbane/

### F68. "Rome's Jewish community is the oldest in Europe, older than Christianity in the city"
**VERDICT: CORRECTED — the strong form is the community's own; scholars hedge it. Use the hedged form.**
Three formulations, in ascending caution: the **Comunità Ebraica di Roma** says "la Comunità più antica d'Europa"
**[promotional, and legitimately so — it is their own claim about themselves]**; the **Museo Ebraico di Roma** says
"The Jewish community has been living **continuously** in Rome for 2,200 years, making it **one of** the oldest
communities **outside of Israel**"; and peer-reviewed work — **Sean Wyer, "Peeling back the artichoke leaves:
symbolism and origin stories in Jewish-Roman Cuisine", *Food, Culture & Society* 27:2 (2024), 537–554, DOI
10.1080/15528014.2023.2297484** — says "**one of Europe's longest-standing** Jewish communities".
**Recommended: "one of the oldest continuously settled Jewish communities in Europe."**
The evidence chain, and what each item does and does not prove:
- **The embassy of 161 BC**, 1 Maccabees 8:17, "So Judas chose Eupolemus son of John son of Accos, and Jason son of
  Eleazar, and sent them to Rome". **An embassy is a diplomatic visit, not settlement.** The community dates its
  presence from it.
- **Cicero, *Pro Flacco* 28.66–69 (59 BC)** — real, and usefully vivid, but **hostile in tone; flag that if quoted**:
  on the gold, "As gold, under pretence of being given to the Jews, was accustomed every year to be exported out of
  Italy and all the provinces to Jerusalem…"; on the crowd, **"You know how numerous that crowd is, how great is
  its unanimity, and of what weight it is in the popular assemblies"**; and the theatrical aside, **"I will speak
  in a low voice, just so as to let the judges hear me. For men are not wanting who would be glad to excite that
  people against me."** https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.02.0019:text%3DFlac.:chapter%3D28
- **The catacombs:** six Jewish catacombs are known in Rome, two open to the public; **Vigna Randanini** was in use
  "between the 2nd and 5th centuries" on radiocarbon evidence, with **1,737 tombs** counted in the recent study
  (Zimmermann et al., *MDAI(R)* 128, 2022, 360–431).
- **The synagogue at Ostia: the date is genuinely disputed — do not give one.** The older view put the first
  building "the middle of the first century AD", but "the latest stratigraphical research, by L. Michael White and
  his team, suggests that the building is not older than the Severan period". The two sides are in print against
  each other: Anders Runesson, *HTR* 92:4 (1999), 409–433, DOI 10.1017/s0017816000017752, and L. Michael White's
  reply, *HTR* 92:4 (1999), 435–464, DOI 10.1017/s0017816000017764. The **Mindius Faustus inscription**, opening
  "Pro Salute Aug(usti)" and recording the donation of the ark for the Torah scrolls at his own expense, is dated
  to the second half of the 2nd century AD. https://www.ostia-antica.org/regio4/17/17-1.htm

### F69. Named cooks and houses of the Ghetto

- **Piperno, founded 1860 — and note what that date means.** "Dobbiamo assegnare alla famiglia Piperno il merito di

- **Giggetto, 1923 — confirmed by the city, not only by the restaurant.** Rome's tourism board: **"Luigi Ceccarelli,

  https://www.turismoroma.it/en/places/trattoria-giggetto-al-portico-dottavia
- **Boccione (the ricotta e visciole bakery): the name and the tart are solid; the 1815/Limentani detail is NOT.**

- **UNVERIFIABLE as a causal statement:** that the demolition destroyed the old kitchens and produced the restaurant

  **Piperno (1860) predates the clearance; Giggetto (1923) follows it by a generation.**

## Item 18 — Carciofi alla giudia and the other Ghetto dishes

### F70. "the round, thornless Romanesco artichoke"
**VERDICT: CONFIRMED — and one terminological correction.** The IGP spec: "È ottenuto dalle cultivar
**Castellammare, Campagnano** e relativi cloni"; the heads are **"privo di spine"** (without thorns), which is why
the whole head can be eaten; and "Il capolino centrale, chiamato **'cimarolo' o 'mammola'**, è caratterizzato da
una forma sferica, compatta, con diametro superiore a 10 cm".
**CORRECTION: *cimarolo* and *mammola* are not varieties.** They are two names for the **apical/central head** —
the first and best, borne at the top — as against the smaller lateral heads (*braccioli*). "The mammola variety" is
a common error. https://www.qualigeo.eu/prodotto/carciofo-romanesco-del-lazio-igp/

### F71. "fried twice in olive oil, once slowly to cook it through and once at high heat"
**VERDICT: CONFIRMED in outline.** "First, the artichokes are fried at a low temperature to soften them. Then,
after their leaves are spread open, they are fried again at a higher temperature" (sourced to Giuliano Malizia,
*La Cucina Ebraico-Romanesca*, Newton Compton, 1995). Rome's tourism board independently: "twice fried in boiling
oil and with beautiful crispy leaves." Giggetto adds the shaping step — trimmed into a **rose shape** before frying.
**UNVERIFIED: the resting interval between the two fryings** — in every modern recipe, but no authoritative source
states it. Present as standard practice, not documented method.

### F72. The 2018 kashrut controversy — two corrections, and a better story
**VERDICT: CONFIRMED that a ruling was reported in 2018; the usual attribution is wrong twice.**
- **Correction 1, who ruled.** Not "the Chief Rabbinate" in a formal responsum, but **the Israeli rabbinate's
  central import division, headed by Rabbi Yitzhak Arazi**, as reported by *Haaretz* and relayed by ANSA on
  **5 April 2018**. Arazi's words: **"Il cuore del carciofo è pieno di vermi e non c'è modo di pulirlo. Non può
  essere casher. Non è la nostra politica, questa è la legge religiosa ebraica."** ("The heart of the artichoke is
  full of worms and there is no way to clean it. It cannot be kosher. This is not our policy, this is Jewish
  religious law.")
- **Correction 2, Di Segni did not issue a rebuttal.** What ANSA reports is (i) unnamed community sources — "per
  fonti della comunità ebraica romana… la 'guerra del carciofo'… semplicemente **'non esiste'**" — and (ii) **a
  gesture rather than a statement**: "in un recente video di saluti alla comunità per la Pasqua ebraica… da parte
  del rabbino capo di Roma **Riccardo Di Segni** e del presidente degli ebrei romani, **Ruth Dureghello**, si
  vedono **entrambi intenti a pulire i carciofi** e prepararli per il piatto tradizionale" ("both are seen busy
  cleaning artichokes and preparing them for the traditional dish"). **The chief rabbi of Rome answered the Israeli
  rabbinate by being filmed cleaning artichokes for Passover. That is the story.**
- *Note the mechanism differs between sources:* Arazi says the heart is full of worms and cannot be cleaned; the

  alla giudia non consente di sfogliare il carciofo in modo da eliminarvi i possibili insetti presenti fra le
  foglie". Rome's counter-argument is varietal: the romanesca's tight petals plus cleaning in lemon water.
https://www.ansa.it/canale_terraegusto/notizie/prodotti_tipici/2018/04/05/la-guerra-del-carciofo-alla-giudia-non-e-casher_5e987d78-bbe6-4bb0-bbe4-a2209413ab60.html ; https://moked.it/2018/04/10/carciofo/

## Item 19 — Testaccio

### F74. "the Mattatoio, which operated from 1890 to 1975"
**VERDICT: CORRECTED on the opening, CONFIRMED and sharpenable on the closing.**
- Designed by **Gioacchino Ersoch** "tra il 1881 e il 1891"; **built 1888–1891; in operation from 1891**, not 1890:
  "il mattatoio, progettato dall'architetto Ersoch, **entrava in funzione nel 1891**". (The literature is not
  unanimous — 1887–1892 and 1890 both appear in guidebooks — but **1890 is not the supported date**.)
- **Closed July 1975**: "Il complesso… rimane attivo fino al **luglio 1975** quando l'area viene dismessa."
https://www.sovraintendenzaroma.it/cosa_facciamo/attivita_sul_territorio/didattica/incontro_sul_tema_il_mattatoio_di_testaccio
- Now: the arts complex **Mattatoio** (including the former MACRO Testaccio and La Pelanda), the Roma Tre
  architecture department, the **Città dell'Altra Economia** (open since 2007, occupying 3,500 m² of the old
  Livestock Scales building), a music school and a social centre. **Prefer "Mattatoio" to "MACRO Testaccio" today.**

### F75. "Its workers were partly paid in what the butchers could not sell, the *quinto quarto*"
**VERDICT: UNVERIFIABLE as documented history — write it as a tradition, not a fact.** No wage agreement, contract,
guild rule, municipal record or labour-history study supporting it was found, and the municipal sources that would
carry such terms showed no such clause. The strongest attestation is a popular essay: Cesare Marchi, *Quando siamo
a tavola* — **"Pagati parte in denaro, parte in natura, cioè col quinto quarto"** ("Paid partly in money, partly in
kind, that is with the fifth quarter"). Italian Wikipedia repeats the claim sourced only to a food website.
**What IS documented and is safe:** Testaccio's cooking is the cooking of the slaughterhouse district and its
butchers. Payment in kind was ordinary in the trade — which makes the story plausible, and still undocumented.
https://openlibrary.org/search/inside?q=%22quinto+quarto%22+%22in+natura%22

### F76. The "four quarters" schema — DO NOT USE
**VERDICT: UNVERIFIABLE; almost certainly a modern folk schema.** The allocation to nobility, clergy, bourgeoisie
and soldiers returned **nothing** in two separate full-text searches. In the Italian book corpus "quattro quarti"
plus nobility/clergy belongs to a completely different, **heraldic** idiom — "i quattro quarti di nobiltà", the four
quarters of noble descent required for admission to an order — which is very likely where the food version was
retro-fitted from. **The real sense is butchery, not sociology:** a carcass splits into four quarters (two fore,
two hind); the fifth quarter is the remainder — head, tail, feet, offal. That definition is confirmed: "Il quinto
quarto è quel che rimane della bestia vaccina o ovina dopo che sono state vendute ai benestanti le parti pregiate."
https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cucina_romana

### F77. "coda alla vaccinara, oxtail braised with celery, tomato and a little cocoa"
**VERDICT: the cocoa is a documented VARIANT, not the base recipe — and the reading presents it as standard.**
- The base, from **Ada Boni, *Il talismano della felicità***: "CODA DI BUE ALLA VACCINARA — Per 6 persone: Coda e
  guance di bue, kg. 1,500 - Lardo, g. 100 - Strutto…" — **oxtail with cheek**, lardo, strutto, white wine, tomato,
  onion, carrot, **celery**. Celery is integral, to the point of entering the dish's name in some books.
- **FOR the cocoa:** Italian Wikipedia describes two principal versions, the richer with bitter cocoa, pine nuts and
  raisins served in Roman trattorias and restaurants, the home version without; Silvana Franconeri, *Italia in
  cucina*: "la ricetta prevede anche l'aggiunta di un po' di **cacao amaro** in polvere"; Diamanti & Soletti list
  "una combinazione d'uvetta, pinoli e cacao amaro".
- **AGAINST:** the standard reference works define the dish without them — John F. Mariani, *The Dictionary of
  Italian Food and Drink*: "Coda alla vaccinara is a stew of oxtail and red wine"; likewise Waverley Root, Anna
  Martini and Gennaro Contaldo.
- **NOT VERIFIED: that the cocoa version is "old-aristocratic" or a survival of Renaissance agrodolce.** No source
  says so. And the dish itself is dated to the **nineteenth century** by Ilaria Beltramme, *Magna Roma*: "le
  rinomate pajate e le code alla vaccinara sono ottocentesche".
- **Safe wording: "Some Roman kitchens finish it with bitter cocoa, pine nuts and raisins; others consider that a
  restaurant elaboration. Both are written down."**

### F78. "trippa alla romana with mint and pecorino"
**VERDICT: CONFIRMED for tomato, mint and pecorino; one detail to drop.** "La trippa alla romana, aromatizzata con
la **menta romana** e condita da abbondante pecorino romano" — the mint is *Mentha pulegium*, **mentuccia/menta

**UNVERIFIED: that the cut is specifically honeycomb tripe (cuffia).** No source consulted specifies the stomach

*A usable detail the reading lacks:* **"Sabato trippa"** is a genuine, long-attested Roman saying, part of
"Giovedì gnocchi, venerdì pesce, sabato trippa", in print well before the food-blog era — Alexander Lenard, *The
Fine Art of Roman Cooking*; Ignazio Silone, *And He Hid Himself*; Gillian Riley, *The Oxford Companion to Italian
Food*; John Hooper, *The Italians* — and **still displayed on signs in historic trattorias**. *Caveat:* the usual
explanation (Saturday was the butchers' slaughtering day) has **no scholarly or primary source**; give it as the
traditional explanation, not as established. https://openlibrary.org/search/inside?q=%22sabato+trippa%22

### F79. "pajata, the milk-filled intestines of a suckling calf"
**VERDICT: definition CONFIRMED; the BSE ban CONFIRMED in substance and CORRECTED in precision.**
- Definition: "rigatoni con la pajata, ossia con l'intestino tenue di vitello da latte… contenente ancora il

- **It was an EU-wide rule, not an Italian ban.** Before 2015, Annex V of **Regulation (EC) No 999/2001** listed as

  https://www.eunews.it/2015/03/23/ue-via-libera-ai-rigatoni-con-la-pajata-originale/

### F80. Monte Testaccio (not currently in the reading, and it should be)
**VERDICT: CONFIRMED, with the headline number needing a hedge.**
- **Size:** "Alto **54 metri** e con una circonferenza di circa **1 chilometro**" (Sovrintendenza); Remesal gives
  "a perimeter of almost 1 km and a height of 50 m"; English Wikipedia gives **c. 35 m above street level**, ~2 ha
  at the base, ~580,000 m³. The 54/50 figures are above sea level. **Safest: "about 35 m above the street, nearly a
  kilometre around."**
- **The number of amphorae: 53 million is an ESTIMATE from a general history, not a scholarly count.** Its source in
  the English chain is Bryan Ward-Perkins, *The Fall of Rome and the End of Civilization* — "Monte Testaccio
  contains the remains of some 53 million amphorae" — which is the footnote English Wikipedia hangs it on. Spanish
  specialist literature gives materially different figures (Blázquez: "unos **26 millones** de ánforas, que
  transportaron unos 1.875 millones de litros"; others 25, 40, "more than fifty"). **Remesal himself does not give
  53 million**, writing only of "fragments of millions of amphorae". **Write: "the estimates run from about 25 to 53
  million vessels, none of them a count."**
- **What they carried: Baetican olive oil in Dressel 20 amphorae** — "large globular 70-litre vessels from Baetica
  (the Guadalquivir region of modern Spain)" — plus smaller numbers from **Tripolitania (Libya) and Byzacena
  (Tunisia)**. **CORRECTION: the secondary sources are North African, not "eastern."**
- **Dates:** the excavated deposits run roughly **AD 140–250**; the Sovrintendenza puts the dumping as a whole
  "tra il periodo augusteo e la metà del III sec. d.C."
- **Why dumped rather than reused — two mechanisms, one clause each:** the vessels were "non riutilizzabili a causa
  della **rapida alterazione dei residui di olio**" and were "non smaltate all'interno" (unglazed inside), so rancid
  oil soaked into the fabric; and the Dressel 20 "broke into large curved fragments that could not readily be
  reduced to small shards".
- **The *tituli picti*:** "note scritte a pennello o a calamo con **il nome dell'esportatore, indicazioni sul
  contenuto**" — and, per English Wikipedia, the weight of the oil, the names of the people who weighed and
  documented it, and the district where it was bottled. *This is a mountain of broken jars that records its own
  paperwork.*
- **Citation note:** CEIPAC's site was unreachable throughout. Cite **José Remesal Rodríguez, "Monte Testaccio
  (Rome, Italy)", *Encyclopedia of Global Archaeology*, DOI 10.1007/978-3-030-30018-0_3331**, and the series
  *Estudios sobre el Monte Testaccio (Roma)* I, Barcelona, 1999. Remesal has run fourteen excavation campaigns there.
http://www.sovraintendenzaroma.it/i_luoghi/roma_antica/monumenti/monte_testaccio ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Testaccio

### F82. The vaccinari, and whether the dish is named for them
**VERDICT: the guild is CONFIRMED; the etymology is a traditional attribution, not a demonstrated one.**

### F83. **BEST OPENING FOR READING 3 — Belli's footnote, Rome, 1832.**
Giuseppe Gioachino Belli's sonnet **"Er roffiano onorato", dated 10 January 1832**, opens "È nnata e bbattezzata a
la Matriscia" ("She was born and baptised at la Matriscia"), and the edition's editorial footnote glosses the
community in a single paragraph that contains an entire migration:

> "*Matrice*: piccolo comune nel circondario di Campobasso. Ma col nome di *Matriciani* si designano tutti coloro
> che da quelle regioni vengono a Roma a far gli osti, i guatteri, gli erbivendoli, i granatai (*scopàri*), i
> facchini di caffè, di spezieria, ecc. Son gente buona, industriosa, laboriosa; ma molto attaccata all'interesse.
> Nell'inverno stanno volentieri a Roma; nell'estate, se possono, ritornano ai propri paesi, dove tutti hanno
> qualche pezzetto di terra propria da coltivare."

("*Matrice*: a small comune in the district of Campobasso. But by the name *Matriciani* are designated all those
who come to Rome from those regions to work as innkeepers, kitchen boys, greengrocers, broom-sellers, porters in
cafés and apothecaries, etc. They are good people, industrious, hard-working, but very attached to their own
interest. **In winter they are glad to stay in Rome; in summer, if they can, they return to their home villages,
where they all have some little patch of land of their own to cultivate.**")
https://it.wikisource.org/wiki/Er_roffiano_onorato
**Why it is the right opening:** it is a dated, contemporary, non-culinary document that shows the actual mechanism
the reading currently attributes to shepherds — mountain people wintering in Rome and going home in summer, working
the city's food trades. It replaces an undocumented shepherd's pantry with a documented seasonal migration, and it
is about *people*, not ingredients. **Handle the one complication honestly (F84).**

### F84. **The complication that makes the opening better, not worse.**
Belli's own footnote glosses *Matrice* as the Molise town near Campobasso, **not Amatrice** — and so does the other
19th-century witness. **Alessandro Rufini, *Notizie storiche intorno alla origine dei nomi di alcune osterie, caffè,
alberghi e locande esistenti nella città di Roma* (1855)**, on the **Osteria della Matriciana in via della Pilotta,
rione Trevi**: **"Fu detta della matriciana, perché venne sempre condotta da una donna nativa della Matrice nel
regno di Napoli"** ("It was called *della matriciana* because it was always run by a woman born at la Matrice in the
Kingdom of Naples"). *Mitigating fact: Amatrice was also in the Kingdom of Naples until 1860, so this does not
settle it.* [Rufini reaches us via Angelo Forgione, *Il Re di Napoli*, Magenes, 2019, p. 95 — an author arguing the
Neapolitan case; check against Rufini's own text before quoting as primary.]
**The dictionary-level claim is nevertheless confirmed**: Fernando Ravaro, *Dizionario romanesco* (Newton Compton,
2005, p. 395) — "A Roma, molti osti e i trattori della città erano originari di Amatrice, così che il termine
*matriciano* venne a significare **'gestore di locanda con cucina'**" ("so that the term *matriciano* came to mean
'keeper of an inn with a kitchen'").
**This is exactly the kind of doubt the rewrite is supposed to stage rather than hide:** a Roman word that meant
"innkeeper", attached to mountain migrants from the old Kingdom of Naples, with two towns claiming it.

### F85. **Second opening for reading 3 — the 1665 edict, and the Amatriciani's own quarter.**
In **rione Ponte** there was a **Vicolo dei Matriciani** (still there, renamed **Vicolo degli Amatriciani** after
1870), a piazza (today's Piazza Lancellotti) and a Locanda of the same name, documented **from the 17th century**;
Amatrice people lodged there and held a market for the produce they brought down. Their church was San Simeone in
posterula. **A primary document survives: an edict of the Maestri di Strade of 22 August 1665 ordering that the
piazza "delli Matrigiani" be cleaned of refuse within two days** (Bandi Casanatense, Editto 10-70, p. 284). The
vicolo still appears by name in Rome's 1883 master plan.
http://www.borgato.be/MISCELLANEA/ROMA_PONTE-M-R/html/matriciani.html ; https://bibliotecadiamatrice.wordpress.com/la-biblioteca-digitale/lamatriciana/vicolo-dei-matriciani-a-roma/
**Why it works:** a street, a market, a complaint about rubbish and a date. It is the least romantic and most
concrete evidence available that Amatrice people were a working presence in Rome long before the dish was named.

### F86. **Has anyone counted the Amatrice-born innkeepers in Rome? NO.**
**UNVERIFIABLE. Do not print a number.** Every source says only "numerosissimi" / "molti". No census figure, no
year, no archival tally surfaced in the Amatrice civic library's digital collection, the comune-adjacent sources, or
the reference works. Google Books — where a figure from Ravaro or Gnoli would most likely sit — was rate-limited,
so this is an unclosed gap rather than a proof of absence.
*The one quantified 19th-century datum found in this area is unrelated to Rome: an 1811 census reference to
guanciale curing among the Monti della Laga shepherds.*

### F87. **A named Amatrice-born restaurateur in Rome: an honest trade-off.**
- **Best documented, but anonymous:** the woman from la Matrice who kept the **Osteria della Matriciana in via della
  Pilotta**, "always run by a woman born at la Matrice", attested in print in **1855** by Rufini. A real, dated
  Roman osteria run by a migrant woman — no name recorded.
- **Named and dated, but 20th-century and not an innkeeper: Giovanni Palombini**, born at **Cornillo, a frazione of
  Amatrice** (c. 1899–1900), founder of the Palombini coffee roasting works near Borgo Pio and Bar Palombini at EUR,
  Rome city councillor in the 1960s, known as Rome's **"re del caffè"**; kidnapped 17 April 1981 aged 81 and
  murdered, his body found 28 October. A Rome school is named for him.
  https://bibliotecadiamatrice.wordpress.com/personalita-amatriciane/personalita-del-novecento/giovanni-palombini/
- **Weak, do not lean on:** *Il Matriciano* (Prati, Colasanti family, 1912 — the site names no founder and asserts
  no Amatrice origin); *La Matriciana* (via del Viminale, 1870, founder unnamed).
**Recommendation: open on the 1855 osteria or the 1665 edict. Do not manufacture a named 19th-century oste.**

### F88. **Opening for reading 4 — Seneca at the midday show.**
**Seneca, *Epistulae morales* 7.3–5**, a named man on a specific afternoon, arriving expecting light entertainment:
> **7.3: "Casu in meridianum spectaculum incidi, lusus exspectans et sales et aliquid laxamenti quo hominum oculi
> ab humano cruore acquiescant."** ("By chance I came upon the midday show, expecting fun, wit and some relaxation,
> something to let men's eyes rest from human blood.")
> **7.4: "Mane leonibus et ursis homines, meridie spectatoribus suis obiciuntur."** ("In the morning men are thrown
> to lions and bears; at midday, to their own spectators.")
> **7.5: "Occide, verbera, ure! Quare tam timide incurrit in ferrum?"** ("Kill him! Flog him! Burn him! Why does he
> meet the sword so timidly?")
*Note the construction in 7.4 — **obiciuntur**, "are thrown", used twice, so the spectators occupy the grammatical
slot of the beasts. A translation that loses that loses the line.*
https://www.thelatinlibrary.com/sen/seneca.ep1.shtml ; https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Moral_letters_to_Lucilius/Letter_7

### F89. **Documented food reaching ordinary people at the games — the wooden balls.**
**Cassius Dio 66.25.5**, on Titus' inaugural games — this is the single best sourced passage about food and the
crowd, and it is *distribution*, not vending:
> "He would throw down into the theatre from aloft **little wooden balls variously inscribed, one designating some
> article of food, another clothing, another a silver vessel or perhaps a gold one, or again horses, pack-animals,
> cattle or slaves**. Those who seized them were to carry them to the dispensers of the bounty, from whom they
> would receive the article named."
https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Cassius_Dio/66*.html
Compare **Suetonius, *Nero* 11**, the fuller *missilia* list: "singula cotidie milia avium cuiusque generis,
multiplex penus, **tesserae frumentariae**, vestis, aurum, argentum, gemmae, margaritae, tabulae pictae, mancipia,
iumenta atque etiam mansuetae ferae, novissime naves, **insulae**, agri" — a thousand birds of each kind daily,
food of many sorts, grain tickets, clothing, gold, silver, gems, pearls, paintings, slaves, beasts of burden, tamed
wild animals, and finally ships, **blocks of flats** and farms. *(Beware machine translations rendering* insulae *as
"islands"; Rolfe gives "blocks of houses".)*
And **Suetonius, *Domitian* 4**, the closest thing to catering: at the Feast of the Seven Hills he gave "large
baskets of victuals to the senate and knights, and smaller one to the commons; **and he himself was the first to
begin to eat**", and because most of the scattered gifts fell in the popular seats he had "five hundred tickets…
into each section occupied by the senatorial and equestrian orders."
*Terminology: use **missilia** for the thrown tokens; **sparsio** is the scattering, and can also mean a perfumed
spray (Lewis & Short, s.v. *sparsio*, citing Seneca and Statius).*

### F90. **The seating law, and the tickets — what can and cannot be said.**
- **Suetonius, *Divus Augustus* 44**, Rolfe's Loeb: "He put a stop by special regulations to the disorderly and
  indiscriminate fashion of viewing the games, through exasperation at **the insult to a senator, to whom no one
  offered a seat in a crowded house at some largely attended games in Puteoli**… **He separated the soldiery from
  the people.** He assigned special seats to the married men of the commons, to boys under age their own section
  and the adjoining one to their preceptors; and he decreed that **no one wearing a dark cloak should sit in the
  middle of the house**. **He would not allow women to view even the gladiators except from the upper seats**,
  though it had been the custom for men and women to sit together at such shows. Only the Vestal virgins were
  assigned a place to themselves, opposite the praetor's tribunal." *The whole reform starts with one senator who
  could not get a seat in Puteoli — a usable human hook.*
  https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Suetonius/12Caesars/Augustus*.html
- **TWO CAVEATS.** (i) **Suetonius never names a "lex Iulia theatralis"** — the label is modern shorthand; the law
  dates 20–17 BC and **predates the Colosseum by a century**. Write "the Augustan rules still governed how Romans
  were seated", not "the lex Iulia theatralis applied to the Colosseum". (ii) **The reading's "spectators found
  their seats by ticket number, as in a modern stadium" is CONTESTED.** The popular claim rests on **William Smith's
  *Dictionary of Greek and Roman Antiquities*, 2nd ed., 1859** — a 19th-century dictionary. Modern work reopens it:
  Roland Austin rejected the gaming reading on shape grounds; **Giulia Baratta (2019)** argues the *tesserae
  lusoriae* were "tickets in a **tombola or lottery**"; **Lindsay M. Holman's 2021 UNC dissertation** is devoted to
  the question. **What IS solid:** numbered entrances (arches XXIII–LIIII survive), and seats marked with cut lines
  and, later, carved with senators' names — most surviving fragments being **3rd–4th century**, i.e. late. The
  scholarly edition is **Silvia Orlandi, *Epigrafia anfiteatrale dell'Occidente romano VI: Roma*** (Quasar, 2004).
  **Safe formulation: "Spectators were assigned places — the arches were numbered, the seats were marked and later
  carved with owners' names. Whether the little inscribed bone tokens in museum cases are the tickets is another
  matter, and the argument is still open."**

### F91. **"Panem et circenses" — cite it correctly or not at all.**
**Juvenal, *Satire* 10.77–81**; the phrase falls in **line 81**:
> "iam pridem, ex quo suffragia nulli / vendimus, effudit curas; nam qui dabat olim / **imperium, fasces, legiones,
> omnia**, nunc se / continet atque duas tantum res anxius optat, / **panem et circenses**."
("Long since — from the time when we sell our votes to no one — the people has thrown off its cares; for the people
that once handed out military command, the fasces, legions, everything, now holds itself in check and anxiously
desires just two things: bread and circuses.")
**What it actually says:** the subject is the *populus* that once conferred office and command and no longer does.
The lines immediately following are about the fall of Sejanus — "sequitur fortunam, ut semper, et odit / damnatos"
("it follows Fortune, as always, and hates the condemned") — and Juvenal adds that the same people would this very
hour be hailing Sejanus as Augustus had Fortune gone the other way. **It is a satirist blaming the people for
abdicating, not a description of a state policy of pacification by food.** Modern usage smuggles in "causal links
(that panem et circenses **caused** this state)" that Juvenal never asserts.
https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A2007.01.0093%3Abook%3D4%3Apoem%3D10 ; https://acoup.blog/2024/12/20/collections-on-bread-and-circuses/

### F92. **Crowd texture, if a sensory passage is wanted.**
- **Martial, *Liber spectaculorum* 3**, the whole world in one building, ending: **"Vox diuersa sonat populorum,
  tum tamen una est, / cum uerus patriae diceris esse pater."** ("The peoples' speech sounds different, yet it is
  one when you are hailed as the true father of the fatherland.") *Translation is the researcher's own — no
  published English version could be retrieved; commission or check one before printing.*
  Also **Spect. 20**, "Cum peteret pars haec Myrinum, pars illa Triumphum" (half the crowd shouting for Myrinus,
  half for Triumphus), and **Spect. 29**, "missio saepe uiris magno clamore petita est" ("release for the men was
  often demanded with a great shout"). https://www.thelatinlibrary.com/martial/mart.spec.shtml
- **Ovid, *Ars amatoria* 1.135–62**, the best crowd-texture passage available — dust, cramped benches, cushions
  brought from home, a footstool, a fan against the heat: "Parva leves capiunt animos: fuit utile multis / **Pulvinum**
  facili composuisse manu. / Profuit et tenui ventos movisse **tabella**, / Et cava sub tenerum **scamna** dedisse
  pedem." ("Small attentions win light hearts: many have profited by arranging a *cushion* with a deft hand; it has
  helped, too, to stir a breeze with a little *fan*, and to put a hollow *footstool* under a dainty foot.")
  *Caveat: cite as "Ars am. 1.135–62" rather than pinning single lines. Note this is the CIRCUS, where men and women
  could sit together — which is exactly why Ovid recommends it and why it is not the Colosseum.*
  https://www.thelatinlibrary.com/ovid/ovid.artis1.shtml

### F93. **The vaccinari, one documented life detail for a Testaccio passage.**
A datable chain that needs no folklore: **in 1570 Pope Pius V stripped the church of San Bartolomeo of its parish
duties and handed it to the confraternity of the vaccinari, the hide-and-leather men of the Regola** — "Con Pio V
nel 1570 cessò le funzioni parrocchiali e fu affidata alla confraternita dei conciatori di pelli, detti a Roma
vaccinari" — **they rebuilt it at their own expense in 1723** and rededicated it to their patron, the apostle
Bartholomew; **the church was pulled down around 1885** to drive Via Arenula through the quarter, and its high altar
was shipped to a church in Eritrea. **The street still carries the trade's name.** Sourced to Armellini, *Le chiese
di Roma dal secolo IV al XIX* (1891), Hülsen (1927) and Quercioli (2000).
https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chiesa_di_San_Bartolomeo_dei_Vaccinari ; https://archive.org/details/lechiesediromada00arme
**Note the chronology honestly:** 1570 for the guild's church, but the dish is dated to the **nineteenth century**.
The dish is named for a trade that long predates it — it was not invented by the guild.

### F94. **The 1870–1888 destruction of the Ghetto and what it did to the cooking.**
**The causal claim is UNVERIFIABLE (F69) — but the physical facts are vivid and sufficient.** What can be written:
the wall came down in 1848 and the obligation to live there ended in 1870, but **the quarter itself was destroyed
between 1885 and the 1890s** for the new master plan and the Lungotevere; the lanes that disappeared included
**via delle Azzimelle, named for its matzo bakery**, and **piazzetta del Pancotto**; Roesler Franz photographed it
going in 1890; the Tempio Maggiore rose on one of four cleared lots from 1901, replacing the five Cinque Scole that
had been squeezed into one building since the sixteenth century. **Piperno (1860) cooks on from before the
clearance; Giggetto opens in 1923 into the rebuilt street.** That contrast carries the point without asserting a
causal history no source supports.
