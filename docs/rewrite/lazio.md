## Openings already heard, and the shapes that are used up

Immediately before Lazio the listener has heard, in this order:

- Umbria 1: a place described in the present (a three-floor cellar in Orvieto).
- Umbria 2: a recurring farm practice told as habit (grapes not pressed at the harvest).
- Umbria 3: an object in a city — **a shop sign in Rome**, the norcineria.
- Umbria 4: a situation with a quotation inside it (Christmas on a Friday).
- Marche 1: a document with a date (a notary's contract, 12 January 1579).
- Marche 2: a working habit seen at a place, past tense (the divers of Portonovo).
- Marche 3: a technique as a second-person instruction (stoning an olive in a spiral).
- Marche 4: a scene at a meal reported by a witness (Federico da Montefeltro at table).

So Lazio may not open on: a dated document, a habitual crew going out to work, a second-person instruction, a
witnessed meal, or a sign or object in a street. Across the eleven regions already rewritten the following shapes
are also spent: a sales figure with a year (ER 1), a person's age and trade with a year (ER 2, Piemonte 4,
Lombardia 1, Friuli 1), a person at work in the early morning (ER 4), two things poured side by side (Toscana 1),
a queue at a cart (Toscana 4), a vehicle climbing (Valle d'Aosta 1), an army crossing a pass (Valle d'Aosta 4),
"Until the 1830s X was…" (Piemonte 2), a man going out at night with a dog (Piemonte 3), a named grower who says
something (Liguria 2), a competition with a date (Liguria 3), a market word explained (Liguria 4, Friuli 4),
"Every spring/autumn, until…" (Lombardia 3, Friuli 3), a grant or ruling with a date (Lombardia 4), goods leaving
by rail (Trentino 1), people given a deadline to leave (Trentino 2), a figure in a wall painting (Trentino 3), a
traveller noticing a rock (Trentino 4), a cellar running low (Veneto 1), a quotation (Veneto 2), fishermen drying
fish (Veneto 3), objects on a table made of sugar (Veneto 4), hail destroying a crop (Friuli 2).

Shapes still free and suited to Lazio: **a quantity arriving somewhere** (the carts of Castelli wine entering
Rome), **a measure or a price fixed by rule**, **a refusal**, **a crowd**, **a dish being ordered and eaten in
sequence**, **a building being taken apart**.

## Material the neighbouring readings have already used, and must not be repeated

- **Umbria 3 opens in Rome**, on the norcineria sign and the Norcia pork butchers, and carries the pig, the
  curing and the *norcino* glossary term. Lazio 3 is a pork-fat reading (guanciale) and must not re-tell the
  pork butcher, the Roman shop, or the mountains-supply-the-city move. Guanciale here is a *cut* and a
  *technique*, not a trade.
- Three city landmark readings are already written: **Bologna** (ER 4: a woman rolling the sfoglia, the towers,
  the porticoes, the osteria that sells no food), **Genoa** (Liguria 4: the stockfish mark, the port, the old
  town), **Venice** (Veneto 4: the sugar banquet, the Arsenale, the malvasie, the coffee houses). Rome is the
  fourth. It must not use their moves: the craftsperson at dawn, the market-word etymology, the banquet
  inventory, or the "put the three walks together" summary ending, which is also what the old Lazio 4 does.
- **Marche 3** already carries a pig slaughter, a spreadable salami, a flatbread and four ports' fish stews;
  **Emilia-Romagna 3** carries the DOP mechanics of cheese and ham (the hammer, the needle, the panel), so
  Pecorino Romano's rules must not be told as a third inspection scene.
- **Toscana 4** is Florence's street food (the cart at the market, lampredotto). Rome's friggitoria and supplì
  must therefore not be written as another cart-queue scene.
- **Two collisions found by reading the neighbours, not by guessing:**
  1. **Porchetta di Ariccia is already told in Umbria 3**, and told as a comparison: "Umbria and Lazio both
     claim to have invented it, and the herb tells the two apart, because the rules for Porchetta di Ariccia,
     south of Rome, require rosemary." The old Lazio 1 introduces the same porchetta as a new thing, with its
     IGP. Lazio 1 must either drop it to the box or take it up where Umbria left it — the rosemary is spent.
  2. **The bring-your-own-food custom is already the closing image of Emilia-Romagna 4**: the Osteria del Sole,
     which "has served wine since 1465 and no food at all", where you buy from the stalls outside and spread it
     on the long tables. The old Lazio 1 gives the fraschetta the same move ("brought or bought your own food:
     bread, olives, pecorino…"). Lazio 1 must carry the fraschetta through the *measure* and the *branch* —
     the fojetta, the frasca, what a jug cost, who drank it — and not through a second bring-your-own scene.
- Endings already used by the landmark readings: an osteria table (ER 4), a Christmas loaf with two slices set
  aside (Liguria 4), a republic ending and a café that stayed open (Veneto 4), a cocktail made by mistake
  (Lombardia 4), an empty painted city (Marche 4), a wolf fed by a town (Umbria 4), a painted calendar of
  months (Trentino 4). Rome's ending must not be a summary of the walks, which is what the old reading does.
- **Abruzzo is the next region** and has not been rewritten yet. Amatrice, the transhumance from Abruzzo to the
  Roman Campagna, and Montepulciano d'Abruzzo all sit on the border between the two. Lazio 3 keeps Amatrice,
  because the dish is Amatrice's; the droving routes and the shepherds' own kitchen belong to Abruzzo 3, so
  Lazio 3 takes only what it needs of the transhumance and does not tell the tratturi.

## Checked before drafting — readings 3 and 4

From `lazio-research-food.md`, items F1–F94 plus a do-not-print list. This is the most damaging research return
of the project so far: **the spine of reading 3 does not survive it.**

**Errors and overclaims in the old text, reading 3:**

- **F1, F6, F7 — the reading's whole frame is wrong.** There is no documented family order, and gricia is **not**
  the oldest of the four. On the documentary record **amatriciana is**: the name in print in 1916, the first
  recipe in Ada Boni's *Il talismano della felicità* in the late 1920s. **Gricia is the worst-attested of the
  four** — no first printed appearance of the word could be found at all. The order by first securely dated
  appearance of the name is amatriciana (1916), cacio e pepe (1929/30), gricia (the dish attested 1929/30 under
  another name, *marchiciana*; the word undated), carbonara (1939). **All four are twentieth-century phenomena
  on paper.** The popular sources that call gricia the oldest also define it as cacio e pepe plus guanciale,
  which would put cacio e pepe first — both orderings circulate, which is what happens when a story is reasoned
  backwards from a modern menu. The honest formulation: gricia is plausibly the older *form* of amatriciana and
  the *latest-named* of the group.
- **F8, F9 — the shepherd's pantry is undocumented.** The transhumance is real, but the provisions list is the
  problem. What Italian accounts actually record transhumant shepherds eating is bread, oil, cheese, eggs,
  onion, garlic, herbs and wine, and dishes built on stale bread and whey — *pancotto*, *acqua sala*, ricotta —
  not dried pasta dressed with pepper. And pepper was an expensive import: Munro's price series has a London
  master mason in 1438–39 needing about 2.25 days' wages for a pound of it. The honest verdict is that the
  story is undocumented and the documented diet does not include it. Note the self-contradiction in the usual
  source: the single reference behind the shepherd origin of cacio e pepe also says pepper was probably added
  much later because it was expensive and scarce.
- **F12, F13 — guanciale.** "Cured with salt and black pepper for several weeks" is wrong twice. Pepper is
  typical, not definitional (the De.Co. sheet allows "pepe o peperoncino", and sea salt and/or sugar and/or
  garlic in the salting), and the cure is **at least thirty days, usually two or three months**. Do not
  distinguish it from pancetta by cure length — Pancetta Piacentina DOP matures at least four months, longer
  than guanciale's minimum. Distinguish by **cut and fat**: the STG says "with the fatty part predominating
  over the lean". Write "usually unsmoked", because smoking is permitted and traditional in parts of the
  Amatrice zone. **And there is no Guanciale Amatriciano IGP or DOP** — it is protected only as a defined
  ingredient inside the STG, plus a municipal De.Co.
- **F14, F15, F17, F18, F19 — Pecorino Romano.** Five months is the minimum for **table** cheese; **eight** is
  the minimum for cheese to be grated, which is the relevant number in a reading about pasta. Sardinia's share
  is quantifiable: **92% of 39,000 tonnes in the 2024/25 campaign**, across about 9,000 shepherds and 47
  dairies, with 60% of saleable production exported. The DOP zone is all of Sardinia, all of Lazio and **one**
  Tuscan province. Dry salting is tradition, not rule — the spec permits brine. Columella VII.8 is genuinely a
  chapter on cheesemaking inside the book on sheep, and its best line is «Verum optimus caseus est, qui exiguum
  medicaminis habet», the best cheese is the one that has least additive in it — **but it names no cheese at
  all**, so it cannot be cited for pecorino romano. **The 27-gram legionary ration is a consortium claim with
  no ancient citation and must not be printed**; Polybius VI.39 itemises grain only.
- **F20, F21, F22 — gricia's name.** Treccani gives **only** the *grici* derivation: nineteenth-century Roman
  dealers in bread and foodstuffs, held to come from the Swiss Grisons or the Valtellina. **Treccani does not
  mention Grisciano at all**, and it allows crumbled sausage as an alternative to guanciale. The Grisciano case
  is the weaker one and its Italian-language advocates are interested parties citing no document; its scholarly
  venue is Buccini's 2007 Oxford Symposium paper, which could not be opened — name it, do not quote it.
  *Amatriciana bianca* is real usage and appears inside the EU STG text itself, but it is the Amatrice side's
  word.
- **F24, F25, F26 — cacio e pepe has a peer-reviewed mechanism now.** Bartolucci et al., "Phase behavior of
  Cacio e Pepe sauce", *Physics of Fluids* 37, 044122 (2025), DOI 10.1063/5.0255841, which won the 2025 Ig
  Nobel in physics. Findings: starch below 1% of cheese mass gives system-wide clumps, which they name the
  "Mozzarella Phase"; the ideal range is 2–3%; and there is a **lower critical solution temperature**, so the
  sauce separates on **heating**, with the abrupt transition around 65 °C. Starch works by binding the cheese
  proteins and reducing their aggregation. **Do not print gram quantities** — two fetches of the paper gave two
  different sets. Fat emulsification is a kitchen explanation, not a finding of the paper.
- **F27 — tonnarelli.** "The traditional shape" is wrong: it is the commonest today, the format is **Abruzzese**
  in origin (cut on the *chitarra*, which is why they are also called spaghetti alla chitarra), and the oldest
  printed authority, Ada Boni 1930, lists **tonnarelli or spaghetti**. Rigatoni or mezze maniche as traditional
  for cacio e pepe is unverifiable — do not assert it.
- **F28 — Amatrice's transfer.** Confirmed, with precisions. The instrument is a Royal Decree-**Law**, R.D.L. 2
  January 1927 n. 1, in *Gazzetta Ufficiale* n. 7 of 11 January 1927, and **Amatrice is not named
  individually**: it moves as part of the suppressed circondario di Cittaducale into the new province of Rieti.
  Before that it was in the province of Aquila degli Abruzzi. **Italy's regions did not exist as administrative
  bodies until 1970**, so "moved from Abruzzo to Lazio" is fair shorthand but must not imply a regional
  government changed hands.
- **F29, F30 — the onion and garlic.** The explicit prohibition is in the **EU text, not the comune's**:
  "'Amatriciana Tradizionale' does not provide for the use of ingredients such as garlic, onion or pork belly".
  The comune's own De.Co. document simply omits them from its list. Do not attribute the ban to Amatrice. What
  the comune's document does specify is lovely and concrete: **«una padella rigorosamente di ferro»**, a
  strictly iron pan, with a derogation for steel in restaurants — and spaghetti, which confirms the old text's
  "in Amatrice itself they use spaghetti".
- **F31 — the STG.** Commission Implementing Regulation (EU) 2020/395 of **6 March 2020**, published 13 March.
  First pasta **sauce**, yes — but Italy's **third** STG after Mozzarella (1998) and Pizza Napoletana (2010),
  so the sentence must not imply first overall.
- **F32, F33 — the earthquake and the campaign.** 24 August 2016, 03:36 local, epicentre near Accumoli, Mw 6.0
  on INGV's figure. **299 dead in total**; the Amatrice-only figure is genuinely contested (237, 239, 228), so
  write "the great majority of them in Amatrice". **Paolo Campana is a blogger, not a chef**, and he launched
  #AMAtriciana on the day of the earthquake: €2 a plate, one euro from the diner and one from the restaurant,
  to the Croce Rossa Italiana, with over 600 restaurants in the first days. **No published total exists — do
  not state an amount raised.**
- **F34 — two origin stories to drop and one to add.** Leonardi's *L'Apicio moderno* (1790) contains no
  amatriciana, so far as can be shown (an OCR check of two volumes found *culì di pomodoro* repeatedly and no
  occurrence of the dish), and the 1816 Quirinale banquet "alla maniera dei matriciani" is unverifiable. **What
  is documented is the opposite of the old text's orthodoxy: the historic amatriciana contained onion**, and
  the no-onion rule is a late codification.
- **F35, F36, F37, F38, F39 — carbonara.** The 1944 American-rations story rests on **a single interview given
  in 2009 by Renato Gualandi at the age of 88**, about a dinner at Riccione on 22 September 1944, and the
  details drift between retellings — the venue is the Hotel Vienna, the Grand Hotel Des Bains or the Domus Mea
  depending on the account, and the guest list shifts. The Rome leg is unestablished. And **a 1939 mention of
  "spaghetti alla carbonara" in print predates it by five years** — in *De Koerier*, a Dutch-language daily of
  the Dutch East Indies, 23 August 1939, found by Edwin Winkels and Janneke Vreugdenhil and authenticated by
  Grandi and Cesari. **This is flagged as not seen at source (Gambero Rosso returns 403), so it must be
  attributed, not asserted.** The charcoal-burner etymology is undocumented. "No cream, ever" is a convention
  of the late 1960s: **Carnacina's 1960 recipe, the first in Italian print to specify guanciale, also calls for
  cream.** "The most cooked Italian dish in the world" is the headline of the pasta-makers' own press release.
- **F40 — how to stage the argument.** Cesari and Grandi are not interchangeable. Cesari works dish by dish
  from dated print, publishes his citations, produced the 1939 find himself, and says «nessuno ha mai creato un
  piatto dal nulla». Grandi is the polemicist, and his sharpest sentence is wrong by a year (the Chicago recipe
  is 1952, not 1953) and undercut by that 1939 mention. The pushback came from **a farm lobby, the press and
  politicians, not from historians with counter-documents** — Coldiretti, which states its own economic motive,
  and a deputy prime minister. No scholarly rebuttal was found.

**Errors and overclaims in the old text, reading 4:**

- **F41** "around AD 72" is a modern convention: no ancient source gives a year, and the Parco archeologico
  itself gives none. Write "begun under Vespasian in the early 70s AD".
- **F42** The "deliberate gesture returning a private pleasure park to the people" can now be sourced instead
  of asserted: Martial, *Liber spectaculorum* 2, «hic ubi conspicui uenerabilis Amphitheatri / erigitur moles,
  stagna Neronis erant», and the same poem's «Reddita Roma sibi est», Rome has been given back to herself.
- **F43** The hundred days is Cassius Dio 66.25.4, written 130–150 years later and surviving only through an
  eleventh-century Byzantine abridgement. Suetonius gives five thousand beasts in a day and no duration.
- **F44** The hypogeum was **wooden at the inauguration** and rebuilt in masonry under Domitian, so "Titus
  opened it" and "the building was finished" are different events. Beste's phasing gives, at peak, **60
  capstans, each two storeys tall and turned by four men a level** — 240 men working winches under the floor.
- **F45, F46, F47** 188 × 156 m and 48.5 m high; **never write 50 m**. 80 arches but **76 numbered for the
  public, 29 of those numbers still legible on the north side**. The fourth-century catalogue's 87,000 counts
  *loca*, feet of bench, not bodies; about 45,000 seated plus standing room is where the familiar 50,000 comes
  from.
- **F48, F49** About 100,000 cubic metres of travertine from the Barco quarries at Tivoli, hauled some 30 km by
  ox-cart, and **300 tons of iron clamps**. **Drop the purpose-built haul road.** The pitting in the facade is
  where those cramps were dug out for scrap.
- **F50** The velarium rests on **one sentence of the Historia Augusta** (Commodus 15.6) which **does not
  mention Misenum**, in a late-fourth-century compilation notorious for invention. The physical evidence is
  good — 240 mast corbels around the attic. Write "sailors of the imperial fleet, barracked nearby, are thought
  to have worked it".
- **F51** The name transfers only after AD 1000. The statue was Zenodorus's colossal bronze, 120 feet, moved
  nearer the amphitheatre by Hadrian in 128 (with, in the sources, 24 elephants), and **part of Hadrian's
  pedestal survives, seven metres square** — something a reader can go and stand beside. **The prophecy is not
  Bede's**: it survives in the pseudo-Bedan *Collectanea*, in a single printed edition of 1563, in the present
  tense, and its "Colyseus" probably means the statue. Do not give a date for the statue's fall.
- **F52, F53** 443 years from 80 to the last recorded beast-hunt in 523. Gladiatorial combat is last mentioned
  around 435 and was abolished by Valentinian III in 438; the 523 venatio comes from Cassiodorus, *Variae*
  V.42, **which does not name the Colosseum** — so write "the last recorded beast-hunt at Rome".
- **F54, F55** The Frangipani held it from the late eleventh or twelfth century. St Peter's and Palazzo Venezia
  stand as quarry destinations; **Palazzo della Cancelleria must be cut** (its travertine came from the Theatre
  of Pompey). Nicholas V authorised removal in 1451; the **1349 earthquake** brought down the outer south side,
  which was built on unstable alluvial ground — that is why that arc is the part that fell; and Cardinal
  Farnese had permission to take as much stone as he could remove in twelve hours.
- **F56** Benedict XIV installed the Stations in 1744 and consecrated the building in 1749. The flora: Domenico
  Panaroli made the first catalogue in **1643**; **Richard Deakin counted 420 species in 1855**, the number in
  his own title, including 56 grasses and 41 legumes; **the vegetation was eradicated in 1871** for fear for the
  masonry; 684 species have been recorded there in all, and about 250 grow there again now.
- **F57, F58, F59, F60, F61 — the drains, which the old text gets wrong in date, name and inference.** Two
  campaigns 45 years apart: the short-axis drains in **1974–80** under Claudio Mocchegiani Carpano, and the
  south collector in **2021–22**, presented publicly on 24 November 2022. **There was no 2024–25 excavation and
  Rossella Rea directed neither** — the recent work is Martina Almonte, Federica Rinaldi and Barbara Nazzaro,
  under Alfonsina Russo, with Alessandra Celant on the plant remains and Jacopo De Grossi Mazzorin and Claudia
  Minniti on the animal remains. Found: figs, grapes, olives, peaches, blackberries, cherries, melon and
  nutshells, plus boxwood and laurel; **53 bronze coins** of about AD 250–450 and one orichalcum sestertius of
  Marcus Aurelius struck 170–171 (**brass, not silver, and 53, not 50**); bone gaming dice, a bone pin,
  clothing studs, shoe nails and leather. Rinaldi's own words: "Besides lions and bears used in the shows,
  remains of small dogs, chickens and pigs were found." **But "Romans ate at the games as they eat at football
  today" is an inference, not an excavated fact** — a drain collects whatever washes into it over centuries,
  and the coin range alone proves a long mixed accumulation. **And "outside the arches there would have been
  stalls selling bread, chickpeas and wine" is unverifiable: no ancient evidence of food vendors at any
  spectacle venue was found.** Write around the gap.
- **F62, F63** "Dozens at Pompeii" is wrong in both directions: the park says about **80**, and Ellis's
  systematic count of masonry counters gives **158**. Ellis also attacks the word: *thermopolium* is a label
  casually attached on discovery in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries with no corroboration in the
  record. The Regio V counter (announced 26 December 2020) is painted with a **nereid on a hippocamp** — not
  "a nymph on a horse", which is how English outlets garbled it — two mallards hanging head-down, a rooster and
  a dog on a lead, with residues of duck, pig, goat or sheep, fish and snails, and **crushed fava beans in a
  wine container**, which Apicius I,5 used to lighten wine's colour.
- **F64, F65** **"Monti, the oldest neighbourhood in Rome" is not defensible**: rione I is a position in an
  administrative list, and the present series was completed only in 1921. Nor is it the largest. What is true
  and better is that the **Suburra** was here, and Juvenal 3.5 is the line: «ego vel Prochytam praepono
  Suburae». Supplì: Treccani's own etymology carries **"forse"**, perhaps, for French *surprise* — keep the
  hedge; the 1847 Trattoria della Lepre "soplis di riso" rests on Jannattoni alone and must be attributed; and
  **"al telefono" gets no date**.
- **F66, F67, F68** *Cum nimis absurdum*, **14 July 1555** (not 12 — the Latin dateline settles it), which
  ordered a quarter with a single entrance and a single exit, forced the sale of property, imposed a
  distinguishing sign, and confined Jews to the rag trade, «sola arte strazzariae». **Three dates the old text
  collapses into one:** the wall came down on 17 April 1848; the obligation to live there ended with the breach
  of Porta Pia on 20 September 1870; **the quarter itself was demolished from 1888**, decided in 1885. The
  erased lanes have usable names — **via delle Azzimelle**, named for its matzo bakery, and **piazzetta del
  Pancotto**. And "the oldest Jewish community in Europe" is the community's own phrase; scholarship says
  **"one of the oldest continuously settled"**. The 161 BC embassy is a diplomatic visit, not settlement; the
  Ostia synagogue's date is disputed in print, so give none.
- **F69** Piperno (1860) predates the clearance and Giggetto (1923, Luigi Ceccarelli and his wife Ines,
  confirmed by the city's own tourism board) opens into the rebuilt street. **Boccione's 1815 and the Limentani
  name are not confirmed** — attribute or omit. That the demolition destroyed the old kitchens and produced the
  restaurant trade is **unverifiable as a causal claim**; the two dates carry the point without it.
- **F70, F71, F72** The Romanesco IGP is the Castellammare and Campagnano cultivars, genuinely thornless —
  but ***cimarolo* and *mammola* are not varieties**: they are names for the apical head, the first and best,
  as against the smaller lateral *braccioli*. The two fryings are confirmed; the resting interval between them
  is standard practice, not documented method. The 2018 kashrut ruling came from **the Israeli rabbinate's
  import division under Rabbi Yitzhak Arazi**, not a formal responsum of the Chief Rabbinate, and **Di Segni
  issued no rebuttal**: he and Ruth Dureghello were filmed cleaning artichokes for Passover. That gesture is
  the story.
- **F73** Aliciotti con l'indivia has no datable origin — give it none. **Filetti di baccalà is not documented
  as a Jewish dish**; Rome's own tourism board files it under street food.
- **F74, F75, F76** The Mattatoio was designed by Gioacchino Ersoch, built 1888–91 and **in operation from
  1891, not 1890**; it closed in **July 1975**. **The quinto quarto wage story is undocumented** — no wage
  agreement, contract, guild rule or labour history supports it, and the strongest attestation is a popular
  essay. Write it as a tradition. **The four-quarters-to-nobility-clergy-bourgeoisie-soldiers schema must not
  be used at all**: it returns nothing, and it was almost certainly retro-fitted from the heraldic "quattro
  quarti di nobiltà". The real sense is butchery — four quarters to a carcass, and the fifth is what is left.
- **F77, F78, F79** **Cocoa is a documented variant, not the base recipe**, and the old text presents it as
  standard; Ada Boni's version is oxtail **and cheek** with celery integral, and the dish is dated to the

  "honeycomb"**, which no source specifies. "Sabato trippa" is genuinely long-attested in print and worth
  having, though the butchers'-slaughtering-day explanation is not sourced. Pajata is the **jejunum** with its
  chyme, and the ban was **EU-wide** (Regulation 999/2001), lifted by Regulation 2015/728 in force on 27 May
  2015 — so the old text's 2001–2015 is right, and for fourteen years the dish was made with lamb.
- **F80** Monte Testaccio is not in the old reading and should be: about **35 m above the street and nearly a
  kilometre around**; the amphora count is an estimate running from about **25 to 53 million, none of them a
  count**; it is **Dressel 20 Baetican olive-oil jars**, with smaller numbers from Tripolitania and Byzacena —
  **North African, not eastern**; the excavated deposits run about AD 140–250; and the reason they were dumped
  rather than reused is mechanical — unglazed inside, so rancid oil soaked into the fabric, and the Dressel 20
  broke into large curved fragments that could not be reduced to shards. The *tituli picti* record the
  exporter, the contents, the weight, the men who weighed it and the district it came from.

  being rebuilt in place. The **vaccinari were the hide-and-leather men as much as the beef butchers** — the
  word covers tanners and flayers — so do not call them simply butchers; write that the dish **takes its name
  from** them rather than is derived from them, because no source argues the derivation and the chronology is
  loose. **Do not print "Università dei Vaccinari" or "Santa Maria in Cacaberis"**, neither of which could be
  verified. The documented chain is: in **1570 Pius V** stripped San Bartolomeo of its parish duties and handed
  it to the vaccinari's confraternity; they **rebuilt it at their own expense in 1723**; it was **pulled down
  around 1885** to drive Via Arenula through, and its high altar was shipped to Eritrea. The street still
  carries the trade's name.

## 3. The four Roman pastas

- Summary → four dishes that are younger on paper than anyone says, and the mountain trade that gave Rome the
  word for an innkeeper.
- Lead: 22 August 1665, the Maestri di Strade order the piazza of the Matrigiani cleared of its refuse within
  two days. Who they were: Amatrice people lodged in rione Ponte, with a vicolo, a locanda, a market for what
  they brought down, and a church.
- The word: *matriciano* came to mean the keeper of an inn with a kitchen; Rufini's osteria of 1855 and the
  doubt about which Matrice.
- The paper trail (quiz 3.1 stands): amatriciana named in print in 1916 and given its first recipe by Ada Boni
  in the late 1920s; cacio e pepe in her *La cucina romana*; gricia the worst attested of the four, and the
  dish under the name *marchiciana* in the same book. So gricia is the older form and the later name.
- What the four are made of: guanciale as a cut, thirty days minimum and usually two or three months, fat over
  lean, usually unsmoked; Pecorino Romano, eight months for grating, 92% of it from Sardinia. Box.
- Cacio e pepe (quiz 3.2, re-founded on the paper): the starch, the Mozzarella Phase under 1%, the ideal 2–3%,
  and the sauce that breaks when it is heated rather than when it cools.
- Amatriciana: the iron pan of the comune's own document; the 1927 decree that moved the town as part of a
  suppressed circondario; the EU text that forbids onion, and the historic recipe that contained it.
- Carbonara (quiz 3.3, rewritten): the 1939 mention, attributed; Gualandi's single 2009 interview and its
  drifting details; the 1952 Chicago recipe with tagliarini and Parmesan; the 1954 Italian one with pancetta,
  gruyère and garlic. Tasting-adjacent pairing line kept short.
- Ending: Carnacina's 1960 recipe, the first in Italian print to insist on guanciale, which also calls for a
  few spoonfuls of very fresh cream.

  *pulvis puteolanus* of the Bay of Naples, from the Campi Flegrei, 200 km south. The Alban Hills supplied the
  **Pozzolane Rosse** (erupted 456 ± 3 ka), the aggregate of Rome's *standing* concrete — the Forum and the
  Markets of Trajan, the great vaults — used almost exclusively from about the time Augustus became emperor.
  The local claim survives; the marine half does not.
- **W1.** The recap's "extinct volcano" is wrong. Every institutional source calls the Colli Albani
  **quiescent**: last eruption from the Albano maar at 36 ± 1 ka, with seismic swarms, gas of magmatic
  affinity, and about **230 cm of uplift measured by levelling between 1950 and 1993**. INGV names soil H₂S
  and CO₂ as the district's main natural hazard. "Sleeping, not dead, and risen two metres under the vineyards
  in living memory" is both true and better.
- **W20.** "A few million bottles a year" for Cesanese is about ten times too generous. Cesanese del Piglio is

## 2. Cesanese: Lazio's forgotten red
