## A. Reading 3, "Ascoli olives and the Adriatic brodetto"

### F1. "from the Adriatic to the peaks of the Sibillini is barely eighty kilometres"; Monte Vettore
**Overstated (the other way: the distance is shorter).** Straight-line distances I computed from coordinates:
Monte Vettore to the coast at San Benedetto del Tronto about 52 km, to Porto San Giorgio about 59 km. In the
north, the Apennine crest at Bocca Trabaria to Fano is about 69 km. Safe wording: "from the Sibillini to the sea
is about sixty kilometres". Monte Vettore is **2,476 m**, the highest peak of the Sibillini.
- Calculation (haversine), no web source; Vettore height: https://www.sibilliniweb.it/citta/lago-di-pilato-1941-mt/ and https://fondoambiente.it/luoghi/parco-nazionale-dei-monti-sibillini

### F2. Oliva Ascolana del Piceno DOP (2005), cultivar, area, brined and stuffed
**Confirmed, with an area nuance.** Registered by **Reg. (CE) n. 1855/2005 of 14 November 2005** (OJ 15 Nov 2005);
Italian spec published in the Gazzetta Ufficiale of 24 February 2006. The name covers olives "in salamoia" and
"ripiene", from the variety **Ascolana Tenera**. The spec lists municipalities of the **provinces of Ascoli Piceno
and Teramo** (the Fermo municipalities were then in Ascoli Piceno province; Fermo province became operational in
2009, so "Ascoli Piceno, Fermo and part of Teramo" is correct in today's map). Harvest 10 September to
20 October, maximum 70 quintals per hectare. The spec gives the fruit as "circa 8 grammi".
- https://www.gazzettaufficiale.it/atto/serie_generale/caricaArticolo?art.progressivo=0&art.idArticolo=1&art.versione=1&art.codiceRedazionale=06A01813&art.dataPubblicazioneGazzetta=2006-02-24&art.idGruppo=0&art.idSottoArticolo1=10&art.idSottoArticolo=1&art.flagTipoArticolo=1
- https://olivaascolanadelpicenodop.it/finalita/

### F3. "the largest green olives in Italy"
**Overstated.** Ascolana tenera is a large table olive (about 8 g, per the spec), but **Bella di Cerignola**
(Puglia) is generally described as larger, and is often called the largest table olive in the world. Write
"large" or "among the largest".
- https://www.puglia.com/bella-cerignola-oliva/ (lead only; weights given vary)

### F4. The spiral stoning
**Tradition, not in the spec.** The spec says only that the olives are "preventivamente denocciolate" (pitted
beforehand) and does not name a method. The single spiral cut is the recognised home and artisan technique
(commonly described as "a spirale"), but it is not a DOP requirement. Safe wording: "the traditional way is one
spiral cut".
- Spec link as F2.

### F5. The filling
**Mostly confirmed; two details corrected.** Spec, art. 6(b): beef **40–70%**, pork **30–50%**, poultry
tolerated up to **10%**; grated **aged cheese** at least **100 g per kg** of filling (the spec says "formaggio
stagionato grattugiato", not specifically Parmigiano); eggs; **dry white wine**; onion, carrot, celery; **nutmeg**;
optional "piccole quantità di salsa di pomodoro" and "buccia di limone grattugiata". Then flour, beaten egg,
breadcrumbs. Stuffed olives must number **65–95 per kg**.
- So: "beef, pork and sometimes chicken, cooked with white wine" is confirmed; "Parmigiano" should be "aged
  cheese"; lemon zest is optional. The recipe's nutmeg is right.
- Spec link as F2.

### F6. WANTED: the documented history of the Ascoli olive
- **Pliny: confirmed.** *Naturalis historia* 15.16 (on table olives): "et in ipsa Italia ceteris **Picenae et
  Sidicinae**" — within Italy itself, the Picene and Sidicine olives are preferred to the others for eating.
  https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/L/Roman/Texts/Pliny_the_Elder/15*.html
- **Martial: confirmed.** *Epigrams* 13.36 (a Xenia label, "Olivae"): "Haec quae **Picenis** venit subducta
  trapetis / inchoat atque eadem finit oliva dapes" — this olive, taken from the Picene presses, both opens and
  closes the meal. https://thelatinlibrary.com/martial/mart13.shtml
- **Petronius: not verifiable.** Olives appear at Trimalchio's dinner in the *Satyricon*, but I found no passage
  calling them Picene; the claim circulates only in secondary sources and it.wikipedia. Do not use.
- **The spec's own history** (art. 4.6 and 5) names "Ulivae Picenae", the Benedictine monks, a letter of **Pope
  Sixtus V** (from the Ascoli area himself), and the admiration of **Rossini, Garibaldi and Puccini**, and dates
  the stuffed olive to "intorno alla costituzione del Regno d'Italia (1859/61)". These are the consortium's claims
  in a legal document, not independent evidence.
- **Origin of the stuffed version: two dates in circulation, neither documented.** The local researcher Benedetto
  Marini dates it to **about 1800**, invented by cooks of noble families to use up surplus meats (reported by
  it.wikipedia and food press, no document cited). The spec says **1859/61**. An earlier "olive giudee" stuffed
  with herbs is mentioned for the 1600s (it.wikipedia only). Verdict: **legend / not verifiable**. Safe wording:
  "the meat-stuffed olive is a nineteenth-century invention of Ascoli's household cooks".
- **Garibaldi: local tradition, dated.** He is said to have eaten them brined and stuffed on **25 January 1849**
  during a short stay in Ascoli, and to have tried in vain to grow the trees on Caprera from cuttings sent by
  Candido Augusto Vecchi. Source chain: it.wikipedia and local sites; the spec names Garibaldi. **Not verifiable**
  beyond that; if used, "Ascoli tells that…".
- **Rossini: see F41.**
- https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olive_ascolane (lead)

### F7. Fritto misto all'ascolana with lamb chops, courgettes and squares of custard
**Confirmed (secondary sources).** The Ascoli fritto misto is olives, lamb chops (costolette d'agnello),
vegetables such as courgettes and artichokes, and fried custard (*crema fritta*, locally *cremini*).
- https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olive_ascolane (lead); https://www.xn--oliv-oqa.it/olive-ascolane.html (producer)

### F8. Brodetto of Porto Recanati: "yellow with saffron", no tomato
**Corrected.** No tomato: **confirmed**. The yellow comes from **zafferanella** (also "zafferanone", "zafferano
selvatico del Conero"), which it.wikipedia identifies as *Carthamus tinctorius*, i.e. **safflower, not true saffron**
(*Crocus sativus*). The colour is called "occhiu de gallu". The version is credited to the cook **Giovanni Velluti**,
owner of one of the oldest beach chalets, in the **early 1900s**; a 1923 Touring Club Italiano guide calls it
"brodetto bianco". A "Settimana del brodetto" is held every June. I did not find a botanical source confirming which
*Carthamus* grows wild on the Conero; treat the species as unconfirmed.
- Safe wording: "coloured yellow with zafferanella, a wild relative of saffron's colour but not saffron".
- https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brodetto_di_Porto_Recanati (lead)

### F9. San Benedetto del Tronto: green tomato, vinegar, sweet peppers
**Confirmed.** No ripe tomato, **green tomatoes**, **sweet (green) peppers** and **wine vinegar**. The vinegar and
green tomato are explained as ways to soften strong or less fresh fish and to keep it during days at sea on the
*paranze* (sailing trawlers). That wine is "replaced" by vinegar is not stated in the sources; drop "the wine is
replaced".
- https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brodetto_alla_sambenedettese (lead)
- https://www.iscsud.edu.it/pagine/la-ricetta-originale-del-brodetto-alla-sambenedettese

### F10. Fano: tomato paste and vinegar; the festival
**Confirmed.** The Fano recipe is characterised by **wine vinegar and tomato concentrate**. **BrodettoFest**
(organised by Confesercenti Pesaro e Urbino with the Comune di Fano) held its **24th edition in 2026**; sources say
it has run "since 2002" (a gap year would make it 2003). Earlier it was billed as "Festival Internazionale del
Brodetto e delle Zuppe di Pesce" in September; now late May / early June.
- https://www.brodettofest.com/la-ricetta/
- https://www.brodettofest.com/fano-festeggia-il-piatto-simbolo-dell-adriatico/

### F11. Ancona's "thirteen kinds of fish" and "nobody can produce a document that says why"
**Confirmed as tradition; the explanations are legend.** Food press gives three explanations: the **thirteen at the
Last Supper** (the dish said to belong to Holy Week), thirteen as a lucky number for fishermen, and the **thirteen
spouts of Ancona's Fontana del Calamo** ("delle Tredici Cannelle"). None is documented. The current sentence is fair.
The municipal culture page on the brodetto returned 404 at the time of checking.
- https://www.scattidigusto.it/brodetto-di-pesce-alla-anconetana-ricetta-originale
- https://www.anconatoday.it/social/brodetto-anconetana-ancona-ricetta.html

### F12. "began as what the fishermen could not sell"; fish added in order of cooking time
**Not verifiable as a documented origin; fine as general description.** Sources say it was the fishermen's meal
cooked on board the *paranze* with the unsaleable part of the catch. The cooking order is technique, not history.
- https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brodetto_alla_sambenedettese (lead)

### F13. Vincisgrassi: Nebbia's *Il cuoco maceratese*, year and recipe
**Year confirmed; recipe description corrected.**
- **Antonio Nebbia** (1723 – Macerata, 6 November 1786). **First edition Macerata 1779** (it.wikipedia; I did not
  see a 1779 copy). The **1781 Macerata edition** (Luigi Chiappini and Antonio Cortesi) calls itself the
  **"Seconda edizione"**, which supports 1779 as the first. Later editions Venice 1783, Macerata 1784 and 1786,
  Bassano (Remondini) 1787–1820. The 1781 copy is digitised by the Comune di Parma.
  https://www.parmaelasuastoria.it/Nebbia-Il-cuoco-maceratese-1.aspx ;
  https://flippingbooks.comune.parma.it/Libri/NEBBIA-IL%20CUOCO%20MACERATESE-1781/ ;
  https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antonio_Nebbia
- **Recipe title.** Sources give "**Salza per il Princisgras**" and "lasagne di princisgras" (also a "Piatto d'uova
  in Princisgras"). The course's "*lasagna in princisgras*" is **not confirmed**; use "princisgras".
- **Ingredients** (from the transcription of the 1998 reprint, ed. Scafà and Nebbia): half a pound of **prosciutto**,
  sliced **truffles**, about one and a half litres of **milk thickened with three ounces of flour**, fresh **cream**,
  whole eggs and yolks, broth, butter, **Parmigiano**; layered with the pasta and baked until a crust forms. **No
  giblets.**
- **Correction:** the course says "without either béchamel or giblets". **No giblets: confirmed. No béchamel:
  wrong in substance**, because Nebbia's sauce is milk thickened with flour and enriched with cream, i.e. a
  béchamel-type sauce, even if he does not use the word.
- https://sybillapicena.com/2016/04/07/sua-maesta-i-princisgras/

### F14. The Windisch-Graetz legend and the 1799 siege
**Legend; the course's "twenty years before the general arrived" needs rewording, because he never arrived.**
- **Siege of Ancona, 14 October – 13 November 1799.** French garrison (about 1,400) under **Jean-Charles Monnier**.
  Besiegers: Austrians under **General Michael von Frölich** (8,000–9,000), a Russian naval squadron under
  **Admiral Voinovich**, Ottoman forces (**Patrona Beg**) and insurgents under **Giuseppe Lahoz Ortiz**.
  **No Windisch-Graetz** is named. https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assedio_di_Ancona_(1799) (lead)
- **Alfred I zu Windisch-Graetz** was born in Brussels in **1787** and entered Austrian service in **1804**: he was
  twelve in 1799.
- **Where the legend comes from:** the Marche cook **Cesare Tirabasso**, *La guida in cucina* (**1927**), first
  codified the dish and tied the name to the Windisch-Graetz family, "one of whose representatives might have been"
  in Ancona. So the legend is twentieth-century.
- **The 1849 variant:** Ancona, held for the Roman Republic, was besieged by about 11,000 Austrians under
  **Franz von Wimpffen** from **24 May 1849**; the surrender was signed on **19 June 1849**. A recent hypothesis moves
  the naming to this occupation, with Windisch-Graetz dragoons in the city. The Comune di Ancona's culture page repeats
  the 1849 version ("nel 1849 sarebbe stato di stanza ad Ancona"), citing nothing. **Not verifiable.**
  https://www.comuneancona.it/ankonline/cultura/vincisgrassi/ ;
  https://www.associazionemazziniana.it/gli-assedi-famosi-ancona-1849/
- **A third etymology** (a "Princisglasses" / "alla Principe di Galles" dish in an 18th-century Umbrian text) appears
  only on it.wikipedia and blogs. **Not verifiable.**
- Safe wording: "In 1927 a Marche cookbook said the name came from an Austrian prince of the Windisch-Graetz family.
  Nebbia had printed *princisgras* in 1779, and the Windisch-Graetz of the story was a boy of twelve when Ancona was
  besieged in 1799."
- https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vincisgrassi_alla_maceratese (lead)

### F15. Giblets, sweetbreads, cloves and nutmeg in the Macerata tradition. NEW: vincisgrassi is an STG
**Confirmed, and there is a new fact the course does not have.** **"Vincisgrassi alla maceratese" is an EU
Traditional Speciality Guaranteed (STG/TSG)**, registered by **Implementing Regulation (EU) 2022/509 of 24 March
2022**, OJ L 103 of **31 March 2022** (Italy's fourth STG). The spec requires **45–55% poultry or rabbit meat**
(chicken, duck, goose, rabbit), **10–15% giblets (stomachs and livers)**, onion **studded with cloves**, a béchamel
of whole milk with **nutmeg**, egg pasta (32–35% egg) optionally with **vino cotto or Marsala** in the dough, and
**7–8 layers**. The Macerata Chamber of Commerce version (with the local Accademia Italiana della Cucina) adds
**sweetbreads** as an option.
- For the Norwegian: "garantert tradisjonell spesialitet" (style rule 8).
- https://www.qualivita.it/news/vincisgrassi-alla-maceratese-stg-registrati-ufficialmente-salgono-316-le-ig-italiane-del-cibo/
- https://www.regione.marche.it/News-ed-Eventi/Post/83837/La-STG-Vincisgrassi-alla-maceratese-%C3%A8-stata-registrata-dall-Unione-Europea
- https://www.disciplinare.it/vincisgrassi-alla-maceratese-stg-la-ricetta.html
- The spec is a ragù of chopped poultry, not beef "rather than mince"; I did not check wording about chopping.

### F16. Ciauscolo IGP
**Mostly confirmed; two corrections.** Registered by **Reg. (CE) n. 729/2009 of 10 August 2009**; spec published
in the Gazzetta Ufficiale of 11 September 2009 (amended since). From the spec (read in full):
- Cuts, in decreasing order: **pancetta (belly) up to 70%**, **shoulder up to 40%**, trimmings of ham and loin up to
  30%. Fat **32–42%** in the 2009 text, **32–46%** in the current Regione Marche text.
- Added: "**sale, pepe nero macinato, vino, aglio**". The spec says **wine**, not "white wine": correct the course.
- Final mincing plate **2–3 mm**; natural pork or beef casing; drying **4–7 days**, optional smoking; curing
  **minimum 15 days** at 8–18 °C. "Cured only briefly": fine.
- Area: named municipalities in the **provinces of Ancona, Macerata, Fermo and Ascoli Piceno**, including the
  mountain towns **Visso, Ussita, Castelsantangelo sul Nera** and Montemonaco. Label may say "prodotto della montagna".
- **"The version from the upper Macerata hills has the highest fat content": not verifiable.** Nothing in the spec.
  Drop it.
- **Etymology:** the spec itself (art. 6) says "ciaùscolo, ciavuscolo" "deriverebbe da **ciabusculum** ossia piccolo
  cibo o piccolo pasto", a snack eaten between breakfast and lunch and between lunch and supper. This is the spec's
  own conditional ("deriverebbe"), with no philological source. Safe: "the name is said to come from…".
- Also in the spec, a usable food-and-society line: the pig slaughter was a winter social event and the occasion of
  **gifts from the sharecropper (mezzadro) to the landowner ("padrone")**.
- https://www.gazzettaufficiale.it/do/atto/serie_generale/caricaPdf?cdimg=09A1068000100010110001&dgu=2009-09-11&art.dataPubblicazioneGazzetta=2009-09-11&art.codiceRedazionale=09A10680&art.num=1&art.tiposerie=SG
- https://static.regione.marche.it/portals/0/Agricoltura/news/DISCIPLINARE%20CIAUSCOLO%20IGP.docx.pdf

### F17. Crescia; Urbino's crescia sfogliata; the Easter crescia
- **Crescia sfogliata di Urbino: confirmed as a PAT** (Prodotto agroalimentare tradizionale on the ministry list,
  category bread and pastry). Flour, **eggs**, water, **lard**, salt and pepper. Traditionally cooked on a **clay
  slab** or *testo*, not a "stone"; write "on a hot griddle stone or clay slab".
  https://www.vivigreen.eu/blog/pat-crescia-sfogliata-di-urbino-marche/ ;
  https://www.turismo.marche.it/en-us/What-to-see/Taste-and-Handicraft/Traditional-recipes-Crescia-sfogliata/9335
- **Easter crescia al formaggio** (Pesaro, Urbino, Ancona): tall leavened cheese bread with coarsely grated aged
  pecorino, eaten on Easter morning with hard-boiled eggs and ciauscolo. A manuscript "Memorie delle cresce di Pasqua
  fatte nel 1848" from the convent of **Santa Maria Maddalena, Serra de' Conti** is cited only by recipe blogs.
  **Not verifiable.** I found no PAT or other recognition for it in this pass.
  https://www.nonnapaperina.it/2014/04/la-crescia-di-pasqua/ (lead)

### F18. Casciotta d'Urbino DOP and Michelangelo
**DOP facts confirmed; "Michelangelo's favourite" is overstated but rests on real documents.**
- Italian denomination by **DPR of 30 March 1982** (as "Caciotta d'Urbino"); spec amended by decree of 4 August 1995;
  **EU DOP by Reg. (CE) n. 1107/96** (OJ L 148, 21 June 1996).
- Milk: **sheep 70–80%, cow 20–30%**, whole, from two daily milkings, from the **whole province of Pesaro and Urbino**.
  Semi-cooked curd, hand-pressed, ripened **20–30 days**, 800–1,200 g, fat on dry matter at least 45%. (Read in the
  spec.) https://static.regione.marche.it/Portals/0/Agricoltura/qualita/casciotta%20durbino%20dop.pdf
- **The documented Michelangelo link:**
  - **Francesco Amadori, called "l'Urbino"**, born at **Casteldurante (today Urbania)**, was Michelangelo's servant
    and assistant **from 1530 until his death in Rome on 3 December 1555** (Treccani). Treccani says nothing about
    land or cheese. https://www.treccani.it/enciclopedia/amadori-francesco-detto-l-urbino_(Dizionario-Biografico)/
  - A **notarial deed of 12 February 1554** records **three farms near Casteldurante** belonging to Michelangelo
    ("Collonello", "Camporesi", "Ca la Ricciola"), let through Amadori, with the tenants obliged to send to Rome
    "**venticinque libbre di cascio di guaimo**", Amadori paying the carriage. Reported in 2015 from research
    presented at Urbino (student newspaper of the university journalism school, and ANSA); I did not see the deed.
    https://ifg.uniurb.it/2015/05/06/ducato-online/cultura/michelangelo-mangiava-casciotta-mentre-dipingeva-la-cappella-sistina-i-suoi-terreni-erano-a-urbania/73530/ ;
    http://www.ansa.it/terraegusto/notizie/rubriche/inbreve/2015/05/05/ritrovati-i-terreni-di-michelangelo-per-produrre-caciotta_32cdfde3-2999-4d8a-ab9a-577c788ea911.html
  - **Letters with Amadori's widow, Cornelia Colonnelli** (from the Michelangelo correspondence, as quoted by the
    consortium): 1 January 1557, "un fardelletto de cascio de guaimo de peso de livere otto"; Michelangelo,
    28 March 1557, "Quando tu mi mandasti i caci, mi scrivesti che mi volevi mandar più altre cose"; 19 April 1558,
    "doi persciutti e doi para di casci di guaime"; 7 January 1561, "sei casciotti de guaime de peso octo libre e mezo".
    https://www.casciottadiurbino.it/storia/
  - **Nuance:** the documents say *cascio / casciotti di guaime* (cheese from the *guaime*, the grass that regrows after
    mowing), not "Casciotta d'Urbino"; they show cheese sent regularly from the Urbino countryside, not a declared
    favourite. Safe wording: "Michelangelo had farms near Urbania whose tenants paid part of their rent in cheese, and
    his servant's widow kept sending him small cheeses and hams from there".

### F19. Vino cotto
**Method confirmed; legal status not verifiable; Roman link general only.**
- Must is boiled in a **copper cauldron (caldaro) over a wood fire for about 10–12 hours** until reduced by **a third to
  a half**, then **fermented and aged for years in wooden casks** (unlike *mosto cotto*, which is not fermented). Areas:
  hills of Ascoli Piceno, Fermo, Macerata and Ancona provinces; **Loro Piceno** is the best-known town. Listed as a
  **PAT**. https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vino_cotto (lead) ; https://www.corriereproposte.it/cosa-sapere/il-vino-cotto-di-loro-piceno.html
- **Whether it may legally be sold as "wine": not verifiable** in this pass; I found no authoritative statement.
- **Roman references:** Pliny and Columella describe *caroenum, defrutum, sapa* (must reduced by a third, a half,
  two-thirds), but I found no source tying these to Picenum specifically. Do not claim a Picene Roman lineage.
- The course's "at the top of a meal there is often vino cotto … poured over ice or into a glass at the end" is
  **not verifiable** and internally odd; it is usually drunk at the end of a meal, with dry biscuits.

### F20. Stoccafisso all'anconetana
**Academy confirmed; consumption claim not verifiable.**
- **Accademia dello Stoccafisso all'Anconitana**: statute registered before the notary on **31 January 1997** at
  Portonovo by four founders (Terenzio Montesi, Roberto Farroni, Gualberto Compagnucci, Amleto Roscioni), to protect
  the "tipicità" of the dish. Its rules (as reported): *Gadus morhua*, preferably graded "ragno", air-dried in the country
  of origin; extra virgin olive oil, preferably from the Marche; yellow-fleshed potatoes; white wine, preferably
  **Verdicchio**. In **2003** Ancona, the Academy and **Vågan** (Lofoten) signed a twinning memorandum (secondary).
  https://www.accademiadellostoccafisso.com/laccademia/ ; https://www.gazzettadelgusto.it/ricette/secondi/stoccafisso-all-anconitana-la-ricetta-accademia/
- **"Ancona eats more stockfish than any other Italian city": not verifiable.** Norwegian Seafood Council material names
  Liguria, Veneto and Sicily as strong markets, not Ancona.
- **How it reached Ancona:** only the Academy's general story (Ancona ships trading with Hanseatic ports loading stockfish
  for the return). **No dated source found.**

### F21. WANTED: moscioli selvatici di Portonovo
**Confirmed, with a 2025 update that matters.**
- Wild *Mytilus galloprovincialis* growing on submerged rocks along the Conero from **Pietralacroce to the Sassi Neri of
  Sirolo**, centred on Portonovo; the best-known rock is **the Trave**, a reef about a kilometre long.
- **Slow Food Presidium since 2004.** Harvested by divers (often free-diving) at **5–10 m** who tear them from the rock
  with a hand rake (the *moscioliniera*); the season has traditionally run **April/May to October**.
- **2025: Slow Food suspended the Presidium** and asked for a fishing stop, citing warming sea, mucilage, over-harvesting
  and illegal picking; the 2025 season opening was delayed from 15 May to 1 July. Any text must not present the
  presidium as current.
- https://www.ansa.it/canale_terraegusto/notizie/dal_mare/2023/03/25/slow-food-a-rischio-mosciolo-selvatico-di-portonovo_69ef5149-73b8-4fb1-9900-c0b97ab46ee9.html
- https://thetuscany.net/2025/06/26/salviamo-il-mosciolo-selvatico-di-portonovo-stop-alla-pesca-per-almeno-tutto-il-2025/
- https://www.fondazioneslowfood.com/it/arca-del-gusto-slow-food/mosciolo-selvatico-di-portonovo-2/

## B. Reading 4, "Urbino and the ideal city"

### F22. Federico da Montefeltro ruled 1444–1482; count, then duke 1474
**Confirmed.** Born **Gubbio, 7 June 1422**. Entered Urbino as lord on **23 July 1444**, after the murder of his
half-brother Oddantonio. Made **duke by Sixtus IV in August 1474**. Died at **Ferrara, 10 September 1482**.
- https://www.treccani.it/enciclopedia/federico-da-montefeltro-duca-di-urbino_(Dizionario-Biografico)/

### F23. Condottiero for whoever paid; "rarely defeated"
**Confirmed; the source is flattering.** Treccani lists service with the Sforza, Alfonso of Aragon, Florence and the
Church, at up to about 120,000 ducats a year. Vespasiano writes that he "mai non fu rotto" (was never routed). Keep
"rarely defeated". Vespasiano also tells that he refused a Venetian offer of **80,000 ducats a year to stay at home**.

### F24. Lost his right eye and the bridge of his nose in a tournament
**Confirmed, c. 1450.** A lance through the visor in a joust around 1450 took his right eye; the bridge of the nose is
said to have been removed (by the injury or a later operation) to widen the view of the left eye. Hence the left
profiles. Do not confuse with his **fall at San Marino on 23 November 1477**, which lamed him (Treccani).
- https://doi.org/10.4137/OED.S40918 (Schwartz et al., *Ophthalmology and Eye Diseases*, 2016)

### F25. The library; "employed scholars to copy manuscripts because he disliked printed books"
**Overstated.** Vespasiano (below, F30) says that over **fourteen years or more** Federico kept "**trenta o quaranta
scrittori**" (thirty or forty scribes, i.e. copyists, not scholars) at Urbino, Florence and elsewhere; that the library
cost "**più di ducati trenta mila**"; and: "i libri tutti sono belli in superlativo grado, tutti iscritti a penna, e non
v'è ignuno a stampa, **che se ne sarebbe vergognato**" — none is printed, "for it would have been ashamed". That is a
bookseller's boast about the library, not a statement of the duke's dislike (and Vespasiano sold manuscripts).
Size: about **900 manuscripts** at Federico's death (first inventory, the *Indice vecchio*, Urb. lat. 1761, c. 1487); to
the Vatican in **1657**. "One of the largest in Europe" is fair; Vespasiano claims he compared inventories of the papal
library, San Marco, Pavia and Oxford and found Urbino the most complete.
- Vespasiano text: https://archive.org/details/vitediuominiill00bistgoog
- https://spotlight.vatlib.it/it/humanist-library/feature/indice-vecchio

### F26. *The Book of the Courtier* "set in these rooms"
**Confirmed.** Set in **1507** in the palace, over four evenings, while Duke Guidobaldo is ill in bed; Elisabetta Gonzaga
and Emilia Pio preside. Published **1528**. (It is set at Guidobaldo's court, not Federico's.)
- https://www.treccani.it/enciclopedia/baldassarre-castiglione_(Enciclopedia-Italiana)/

### F27. Palace: 1454, Maso di Bartolomeo, Laurana, Francesco di Giorgio, Bramante
- **1454 / Maso di Bartolomeo: confirmed at lead level.** About 1454 Florentine masons under **Maso di Bartolomeo** began
  joining the older buildings (the Palazzetto della Jole phase). https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palazzo_Ducale_(Urbino)
- **Laurana: overstated / corrected.** Born at Laurana (Vrana) near Zadar, **Dalmatia**, c. 1420–25; in Urbino from
  about **1466**; patent of **10 June 1468** making him "Ingegnero e Capo di tutti li maestri"; designed the great
  staircase, the wing with the library and apartments, and the **loggia of the torricini**; **the courtyard's authorship
  is disputed**; **left in July 1472**; later fortifications at Naples and the **Rocca Costanza at Pesaro (1476–79)**; died
  Pesaro, September 1479. "Better known for fortifications" is wrong: he is best known for Urbino. "Built the
  substructures and the courtyard" should be softened.
  https://www.treccani.it/enciclopedia/luciano-laurana_(Dizionario-Biografico)/
- **Francesco di Giorgio Martini:** finished much of the palace and designed the helical ramp and the hydraulics
  (see F32). Confirmed at secondary level.
- **Bramante: overstated.** Born **1444 at Monte Asdrualdo (Fermignano)**; trained in Urbino's circle, probably near
  Piero and Fra Carnevale; the intarsia perspectives and some palace work are attributed to him without documents;
  **first documented in Bergamo in 1477**. He was 24 when Laurana's main campaign began, so "the young Bramante grew up
  watching it go up" is a flourish. Safe: "Bramante, born nearby in 1444, trained in this circle".
  https://www.treccani.it/enciclopedia/donato-bramante_(Dizionario-Biografico)/

### F29. The studiolo: size, intarsia, portraits, chapels
- **Size: confirmed.** About **3.60 × 3.35 m**, just over 12 m², the smallest room in the palace.
  https://arttrip.it/studiolo-federico-montefeltro-urbino/ (secondary)
- **Date and intarsia: confirmed.** Traditionally **1476** (work 1473–76). Executed by the workshop of **Giuliano and
  Benedetto da Maiano** (also the coffered ceiling), with **Baccio Pontelli** credited for the perspective constructions;
  designs attributed variously to **Botticelli, Francesco di Giorgio and Bramante**. Musical instruments are the most
  frequent objects shown; I found **no food or drink objects** described.
  https://gndm.it/en/opere/the-dukes-studiolo/ ; https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Studiolo_di_Federico_da_Montefeltro
- **The 28 portraits: corrected.** Painted by **Justus of Ghent** and "Pietro Spagnolo" (**Pedro Berruguete**). After
  1631 they were removed and split: **14 are in the Louvre** (Colonna-Sciarra, then Campana, bought by Napoleon III,
  arrived 1863) and **14 are back in Urbino** (returned 1934, rehung in the studiolo 1983). The course's "Above the
  panelling hangs a row of twenty-eight portraits" must say that half are now copies or gaps / in Paris.
- **Chapels: confirmed.** The **Cappella del Perdono** and the **Tempietto delle Muse**, with identical plans, are on the
  floor below the studiolo; Federico's bath is lower still.
  https://gndm.it/en/palace/ ; https://www.electa.it/en/product/il-palazzo-ducale-di-urbino-lo-studiolo-the-ducal-palace-of-urbino-the-studiolo/

### F30. WANTED: how Federico ate, in Vespasiano da Bisticci (primary text, best find)
**Confirmed from the text** (*Commentario de la vita del signore Federigo duca d'Urbino*, in *Vite di uomini illustri
del secolo XV*, ed. Bartoli, Florence 1859, pp. 73–110; read in the archive.org scan). Verbatim:
- The day in Urbino: he rode out at dawn, heard Mass, gave audience in a garden "con tutti gli usci aperti" until dinner.
- "**Postosi a tavola, stavano aperti tutti gli usci; ognuno poteva venire dov'era il signore; che mai mangiava, che non
  fusse piena la sala. Secondo il tempo, si faceva leggere, come è detto; di quaresima, cose ispirituali; in altri tempi,
  le Istorie di Livio; tutti in latino. Li cibi sui erano cibi grossi; confetti non mangiava, non beveva vino per
  continenza, se non o di melagrane o di frutte, come di ceragie o di mele.**"
  (At table all the doors stood open and anyone could come in; he never ate without the hall full. Depending on the
  season he had reading aloud: in Lent, spiritual works; at other times Livy's histories, all in Latin. His food was
  plain; he ate no sugared sweets, and drank no wine out of temperance, except wine made from pomegranates or from fruit
  such as cherries or apples.)
- "Chi gli voleva favellare, mangiato ch'egli aveva o mentre che mangiava, poteva." After meals an appeals judge put cases
  to him in Latin and he decided them in Latin.
- **Household:** "avendo in casa sua alle spese sua **cinquecento bocche o più**", run like a religious house: no gambling,
  no swearing.
- **Alms:** "dava d'ordinario ogni dì la casa sua, **pane e vino** in buona somma".
- **Fasting:** he fasted every vigil and every Lent; in his last year Ottaviano (Ubaldini) obtained a **papal
  dispensation** from the Lenten fast, which was handed to him "sendo di quaresima a tavola"; he laughed, thanked him,
  asked what example he would set his household, "e seguitò a digiunarla".
- **Caution on "vino … di melagrane o di frutte":** the phrase can also be read as fruit juices / fruit drinks rather than
  fermented fruit wine; say "no wine, only drinks made from pomegranates, cherries or apples".
- https://archive.org/details/vitediuominiill00bistgoog

### F31. WANTED: the *Ordine et officij* (BAV, Urb. lat. 1248)
**Partly verifiable.** A household ordinance of **sixty-four short chapters**, written **c. 1490**, early in Guidobaldo's
reign (1482–1508), surviving in an early 16th-century copy of a lost original, moved to the Vatican in **1657**. It covers
the chancery, **etiquette at the ducal table**, guarding the silver from light-fingered staff, and keeping the stables
dry. Offices named include the **scalco** (steward/carver), **credenziere** (keeper of the sideboard, plate and cold food)
and **bottigliere** (wine). Editions: Ermini, 1932; **Sabine Eiche, Accademia Raffaello, Urbino 1999**, with Allen J.
Grieco's essay "**Conviviality in a Renaissance court: the *Ordine et officij* and the court of Urbino**". The chapter
texts on the kitchen were **not accessible online**; the number of people fed and the kitchen rules are **not verifiable**
in this pass. Getting Grieco's essay would be the single most useful next step for a food paragraph.
- http://www.caareviews.org/reviews/49 (review; blocked to fetch, summary via search)
- https://archive.org/details/ordineetofficijd0000unse
- https://warwick.ac.uk/fac/arts/ren/archive-research-old/journal/nine/eiche.doc

### F32. WANTED: the palace's underground kitchens, ice house, cellars, stables
**Confirmed at secondary level only.** The basements hold kitchens, an **ice house (neviera)**, laundries, cisterns,
stables, and the duke's **bath, heated partly with hot water and fumes from the kitchens below**; rainwater was filtered
through gravel and charcoal for the kitchens; Francesco di Giorgio's **helical ramp** linked the palace to the **Data**
stables. The gallery's own site only says "kitchens and well-organised services". No capacity figures or dates found.
- https://www.coopculture.it/it/poi/galleria-nazionale-delle-marche-palazzo-ducale-di-urbino/
- http://tuttomusei.blogspot.com/2010/04/i-sotterranei-di-palazzo-ducale-urbino.html (lead)

### F37. Loreto: "brought here according to tradition in the thirteenth century"
**Confirmed, with the date of the tradition.** Tradition dates the arrival to **10 December 1294**. The story was first
written down about **1472** in the *Translatio miraculosa ecclesie beate Marie virginis de Loreto* by **Pietro di Giorgio
Tolomei, "il Teramano"**, governor of the shrine. The basilica was begun in **1469** by Nicolò delle Aste, bishop of
Recanati. Pilgrim numbers: not checked.
- https://www.santuarioloreto.va/it/storia.html
- **Food link (sourced): the pharmacy of the Holy House.** A hospital for **sick pilgrims** existed from the **end of the
  14th century**, with a spezieria that made compound remedies for pilgrims, including foreigners. Its **majolica jars from
  Urbino workshops** once numbered about **550**: **378** given in the second half of the 16th century (donor either Cardinal
  Giulio Feltrio della Rovere or Duke Francesco Maria II della Rovere), and **178 bought in Urbino in 1631**; the earliest
  group is associated with **Orazio Fontana**'s workshop, the 1631 purchase with the Patanazzi. A pharmacy, not a kitchen:
  use only if a medicine-and-sugar angle is wanted.
  https://fondoambiente.it/luoghi/antica-farmacia-santa-casa

### F40. Recanati and Leopardi
- **L'Infinito 1819: confirmed** (probably autumn 1819).
- **"the hill at the end of his garden … still called L'Infinito": corrected.** The hill is **Monte Tabor**, and the spot is
  the **orto of the convent of Santo Stefano**, a few steps from Palazzo Leopardi, not his own garden. It has been called

- **"grew up in his father's library":** Monaldo's library is well known; not re-checked in this pass.
- **WANTED: the list of 49 dishes: confirmed at secondary level.** An autograph list of **49 dishes** in brown ink on a
  narrow strip of ivory paper, written in his last years in Naples (from 1833) for the cook **Pasquale Ignarra**; once in the
  **Carte Ranieri**, now with the **Carte Leopardi** at the **Biblioteca Nazionale di Napoli**. Items as transcribed:
  "Tortellini di magro. Maccheroni, o tagliolini. Capellini al burro. Bodin di capellini. Bodin di latte. Bodin di polenta.
  Bodin di riso. Riso al burro. Frittelle di riso. Frittelle di mele o pere", plus gnocchi, fried artichokes, fried ricotta,
  pan dorato, latte fritto. Published in *Leopardi a tavola* (Fausto Lupetti). I did not see the manuscript or a shelfmark.
  https://www.vesuviolive.it/ultime-notizie/275016-leopardi-poeta-buongustaio-la-lista-dei-49-cibi-che-voleva-mangiare-a-napoli/ ;
  https://www.affaritaliani.it/libri-editori/la-vera-storia-040914.html
- **WANTED: sweets and ices in Naples: confirmed from the primary text**, Antonio Ranieri, *Sette anni di sodalizio con Giacomo
  Leopardi* (1880), read in the archive.org scan:
  - Doctors forbade sweets and "assolutamente, i gelati"; he answered with excesses: "il caffè, sciroppo di caffè; la limonea,
    sciroppo di limone; il cioccolatte, sciroppo di cioccolatte … E quanto ai gelati, **era un furore**".
  - One evening at the café at the corner of Taverna Penta on Toledo, "allora delle Due Sicilie", the excess was such that a
    mocking crowd gathered round him.
  - The sugared **tarallini** had to be those of "**Vito Pinto, famoso sorbettaio alla Carità, divenuto ricchissimo e barone a
    furia di ottimi gelati**"; ices from a Torre del Greco maker would not do. Leopardi wrote a tercet in praise of ices with the

    leave Naples.
  - **13 June 1837**: Ranieri's father gave the nursing sister two paper cones of **confetti cannellini di Sulmona**, a pound and

  - **14 June 1837**, the day he died: over his broth he asked for "una abbondante limonea gelata, **che qui chiamano granita**";
    Paolina brought a double, and he drank it "con la consueta avidità".
  - https://archive.org/details/setteannidisodal00raniuoft
