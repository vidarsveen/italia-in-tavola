# Molise readings 3 and 4 — research and fact-check

Research pass for the rewrite of `content/molise.js` lesson 3 ("Caciocavallo and the transhumance
routes") and lesson 4 ("Campobasso and the hill villages"). Verdicts are CONFIRMED / CORRECTED /
UNSUPPORTED / CANNOT VERIFY. Every finding carries a URL and, where it matters, the source's own words.

Labels used: [producer] = a firm selling the thing; [promotional] = tourism or trade body;
[calculated] = arithmetic done here, not quoted from a source.

Note on method: two academic hosts refused automated fetching — judaism-and-rome.org returns 403 and
hal.science sits behind Anubis — so the Saepinum inscription's Latin was recovered from the Epigraphic
Database Roma record surfaced through search (EDR079028, via Europeana) and cross-read against
romanoimpero and arttrip. The EDR site itself (edr-edr.it) refused the connection. Gaps are listed in
section D.

## A. Reading 3 — the tratturi and the shepherds' kitchen

### F1. The tratturi are drove roads between mountain and the Puglian plain
**Claim (old text):** "grass highways along which flocks moved twice a year between the summer pastures
of Abruzzo and Molise and the winter plains of Puglia."
**Verdict: CONFIRMED.** Treccani defines them as tracks with a natural surface, formed by the periodic
passage of flocks; the seasonal rhythm is down in autumn, back in late spring.
https://www.treccani.it/enciclopedia/tratturo/ and https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tratturo

### F2. The width — 111 metres, sixty Neapolitan paces
**Claim (old text):** "A royal tratturo was fixed at 111 metres wide, sixty Neapolitan paces."
**Verdict: CONFIRMED, and the unit is *passi*, not *canne*.**
> "Di norma la misura della larghezza della sede del tracciato viario è di 111 metri, corrispondenti a
> sessanta passi napoletani"

https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tratturo
Treccani gives 111.6 m for the same 60 paces (https://www.treccani.it/enciclopedia/tratturo/); the
Molise tratturi site writes "erano dotati di una larghezza originaria pari a 111,6 metri"
(https://tratturidelmolise.com/i-tratturi/territorio-e-paesaggio/).
**Usable detail the old text lacks — the rest of the hierarchy is also measured in paces:**
*tratturelli* of 20, 15 or 10 paces (37 m, 27.75 m, 18.5 m), *bracci* linking one tratturo to another,
and *riposi*, the resting grounds. https://www.fidaf.it/transumanza-e-tratturi-patrimonio-dellumanita/
Under Fascism the protected width was cut from 60 paces to 30, which is how the edges were ploughed in
(it.wikipedia, Tratturo). Boundary stones marked **R.T.**, *Regio Tratturo*, with a number; they were
set between **1574 and 1884** (same source).

### F3. "The longest ran well over two hundred kilometres"
**Verdict: CONFIRMED.** L'Aquila–Foggia, the *Tratturo Magno*, is 243–244 km; Pescasseroli–Candela 211
km; Celano–Foggia 207 km; Castel di Sangro–Lucera 127 km; Centurelle–Montesecco 120 km. Network total
about 3,100 km. https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tratturo and
https://goodtrekking.it/percorsi/italia/trekking-molise/tratturi-molise

### F4. "Four of the five great tratturi cross it" — WRONG, it is all five
**Verdict: CORRECTED.** Every one of the five royal tratturi crosses Molise. The regional tratturi site
lists Pescasseroli–Candela, Castel di Sangro–Lucera, Celano–Foggia, L'Aquila–Foggia and
Centurelle–Montesecco as the five crossing the region.
https://tratturidelmolise.com/i-tratturi/i-tratturi-la-storia/
> "I principali Tratturi che attraversano il Molise sono il Pescasseroli-Candela … il Castel di
> Sangro-Lucera … il Celano-Foggia … il L'Aquila-Foggia … e la sua diramazione Centurelle-Montesecco"

https://goodtrekking.it/percorsi/italia/trekking-molise/tratturi-molise
**And the figure that makes the point better than "four of five":** about **450 km of tratturo inside
Molise, covering some 4,086 hectares** — a regional network in a region of 4,460 km².
https://tratturidelmolise.com/i-tratturi/territorio-e-paesaggio/
Sabatino Moscati, quoted on the same page: "I Tratturi hanno dettato qui la legge dell'insediamento e
del movimento."

### F5. The Dogana — the old text puts it in the wrong town
**Claim (old text):** "in 1447 Alfonso of Aragon reorganised the sheep customs, the *Dogana della Mena
delle Pecore*, and moved it to Foggia."
**Verdict: CORRECTED.** Alfonso founded it **at Lucera in 1447**. The seat moved to **Foggia in 1468**,
under his son Ferrante — not by Alfonso.
> "Fu istituita nella città di Lucera nel 1447 da re Alfonso I" … the seat moved to Foggia by 1468

https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dogana_delle_pecore
What it taxed: the *fida*, a grazing rent on wintering flocks, "uno dei principali cespiti dell'erario
del Regno di Napoli". Jurisdiction extended over Abruzzo from 1532; the Doganella d'Abruzzo separated
in 1590; the Dogana had its own tribunal for shepherds, with appeal to the Regia Camera della Sommaria
in Naples. Abolished by Giuseppe Bonaparte's law of **21 May 1806**. (same source)
So the honest sentence is: founded 1447 at Lucera, at Foggia from 1468, abolished 1806 — **359 years**,
not "four centuries", though "the best part of four centuries" is safe. [calculated]

### F6. The UNESCO status — right as far as it goes, and it omits the thing that happened
**Claim (old text):** "Since 2006 Italy has been pressing the case for the tratturi as a UNESCO World
Heritage site, so far without success."
**Verdict: CONFIRMED on the tentative list, but MISLEADING by omission.**
- **"La transumanza: i Regi Tratturi" went onto Italy's UNESCO Tentative List on 01/06/2006** and has
  never been inscribed on the World Heritage List. https://www.patrimoniounesco.it/la-tentative-list-italiana/
- But **transhumance itself was inscribed on the Representative List of the Intangible Cultural
  Heritage of Humanity in 2019**, on a joint Italian–Austrian–Greek nomination lodged in March 2018,
  with the Molise communities directly involved (Frosolone is named).
  https://www.unesco.it/it/iniziative-dellunesco/patrimonio-culturale-immateriale/la-transumanza/
  Extended in 2023 to ten countries (see the Abruzzo research, F13, for the UNESCO decision numbers:
  14.COM 10.b.2, then 18.COM 8.b.14 — https://ich.unesco.org/en/RL/transhumance-the-seasonal-droving-of-livestock-01470).
**Recommendation:** "the roads themselves are still only on Italy's tentative list, twenty years on;
the walking of them was inscribed in 2019." That is both true and a better sentence.

### F7. Caciocavallo — the cheese, the shape, the hanging
**Verdict: CONFIRMED on the method; the etymology must be hedged.**
Stretched curd, heated and pulled, shaped into a pear with a head, tied in pairs and hung astride a
beam. Agnone's is **cow's milk**, 18–22 cm tall, **1.5 to about 3 kg**, aged **three months to two
years**; area Agnone, Capracotta and Vastogirardi, province of Isernia. It is on the national **PAT**
list and in **Slow Food's Ark of Taste** — it is *not* a DOP and not a Presidium.
https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caciocavallo_di_Agnone and https://www.onaf.it/index.php?c=index&a=schedaformaggio&id=404
**The name:** Treccani lists the hanging-astride explanation as one of several, alongside the shape, a
mark stamped on the rind, and (per Devoto) the Turkish *kaşkaval*:
> "composto di cacio e cavallo, forse per la forma o per un marchio impresso, o, secondo altri, perché
> questi formaggi si conservano legati e appaiati a cavalcioni di un'asse"

https://www.treccani.it/vocabolario/caciocavallo/
The old text states the *a cavallo* derivation flat. Write "the usual explanation" or "the one the
cheesemakers give", not "which is where the name comes from".
**One more fact worth having:** **Caciocavallo Silano DOP** covers both Molise provinces, so the region
does have a DOP caciocavallo — it is just not the Agnone one.
https://www.disciplinare.it/caciocavallo-silano-dop-disciplinare-di-produzione.html

### F8. Scamorza
**Verdict: CONFIRMED, with an etymology worth using.** Same stretched curd, eaten within days or
smoked, traditionally over beech or oak. The name is from the southern verb *scamozzare*, to lop or
pollard — or from *capa mozza*, cut-off head, which is exactly what the shape looks like.
> "derivato meridionale del verbo 'scamozzare' … deriverebbe da 'capa mozza' cioè testa mozzata"

https://unaparolaalgiorno.it/significato/scamorza and https://milkcoop.it/scamorza-affumicata-origini-curiosita-e-tradizioni/

### F9. The Molise cheeses the reading does not name
**Verdict: CONFIRMED, all on the regional PAT list.** Three that would earn their place:
- **Stracciata**, a fresh pasta filata from Agnone, Capracotta and Vastogirardi, folded into flat strips
  4–5 cm wide — the name is from the tearing.
- **Pecorino di Capracotta**, peppery when aged.
- **Burrino** (*manteca*): a ball of butter wrapped inside a skin of stretched curd, invented to keep
  butter without a cold room. That is a shepherd's refrigerator, and it is the best single image in the
  Molise dairy.
https://www.cibotoday.it/storie/lista-formaggi-rari-sconosciuti-molise.html and
https://www.disciplinare.it/elenco-dei-prodotti-agroalimentari-tradizionali-pat-molise.html

### F10. Cavatelli
**Verdict: CONFIRMED on substance; one popular claim must NOT be repeated.** Durum semolina, water,
no egg; rope cut into pieces of about a centimetre and dragged; the name is from *cavare*, to hollow
out; dialect *cavatielle*; the same *strascinata* family as orecchiette.
https://enciclopediacucina.giallozafferano.it/cavatelli and https://valdigrano.com/parlando-di-pasta-i-cavatelli/
**UNSUPPORTED:** the widely repeated line that cavatelli were "first mentioned in a document by
Frederick II" (https://www.ilgiornale.it/news/over/cavatelli-storia-pi-antica-pasta-italiana-1968510.html).
No document is cited anywhere I could reach. Do not write it.

### F11. Pampanella
**Verdict: CONFIRMED, with two corrections and a date to drop.**
- Town, method and look are right: San Martino in Pensilis; pork rubbed with sweet and hot dried red
  pepper, garlic, vinegar and salt; baked; comes out "di un rosso vivo … molto morbida".
  https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pampanella_(gastronomia) and
  https://www.cibotoday.it/storie/territorio/pampanella-molisana-storia-e-origine-della-ricetta.html
- **The cuts are not only cheap ones.** The sources list "pancetta, capocollo, prosciutto, costatine" —
  belly, capocollo, leg and ribs. Capocollo and leg are not cheap cuts. The old text's "usually the
  cheaper cuts and often the ribs" overstates the poverty. (same sources)
- **The vine leaves wrapped the meat, they were not a lining.** "I contadini e i pastori presero
  l'abitudine di cuocere la carne dopo averla avvolta nei pampini della vite" — wrapped in them. The
  recipe headnote in `content/recipes/molise.js` says the meat "was laid on them in the oven", which is
  a different claim; fix it there too.
- **Status, which is better than a legend:** a **De.Co.** granted by the comune of San Martino in
  Pensilis, with its own disciplinare, and a **Molise traditional product since 1999**; the *sagra* is
  in August. https://www.pampanelladelmolise.it/contenuti/pampanella-deco/ and
  https://www.infodeco.it/portfolio-item/pampanella-di-san-martino-in-pensilis-campobasso/
- **UNSUPPORTED: the "sixteenth century" origin and the Puglian-import story.** Every source frames it
  as "si narra" / "le origini si perdono nella tradizione popolare". No document. Write it as the story
  the town tells, or leave it out.

### F12. Ventricina — the old text has it exactly backwards
**Claim (old text):** "the version around Montenero is softer and more spreadable than the Abruzzese one."
**Verdict: CORRECTED, and inverted.** The **Montenero di Bisaccia** ventricina is the firm, sliceable,
long-aged one; the **spreadable** ventricina is the Abruzzese **teramana**.
- Montenero: lean cuts (thigh, loin, shoulder), lard, salt, chilli, paprika and fennel seed, stuffed
  into the bladder, dried by a fire then aged; Slow Food's Ark entry gives three months, and comparative
  sources give at least a year, with "una percentuale di grasso molto bassa".
  https://www.fondazioneslowfood.com/it/arca-del-gusto-slow-food/ventricina-di-montenero-di-bisaccia/
  and https://www.ricettegourmet.com/enogastronomia/prodotti-tipici-italiani/prodotti-tipici-molise/ventricina-di-montenero/
- Teramana: 60–70% fat, aged only weeks, "è spalmabile"; vastese is 70–80% lean, knife-cut, aged at
  least 100 days. https://www.viverelabruzzo.it/salumi/ventricina/ and
  https://www.spaghettiemandolino.it/blog/315-ventricina-vastese-abruzzese-teramana-spalmabile.html
**So the Molisan ventricina is the leanest and longest-aged of the three**, which is a better and truer
sentence than the one in the reading. (The Abruzzo reading already covers the vastese; see the Abruzzo
research, F24.)

### F13. The salume the reading misses — Signora di Conca Casale
**Verdict: CONFIRMED and it is the best food story in the region.** A **Slow Food Presidium** from Conca
Casale near Venafro, province of Isernia. Made in the coldest days of winter, knife-minced from the best
cuts — lean thigh, loin, shoulder, plus belly and back fat — seasoned with whole black peppercorns,
coriander, ground chilli and wild fennel, stuffed into the caecum, known locally as *la zia*, "the
aunt", which gives it the look of a beehive. 800 g to 5 kg. Aged at least six months. The women kept the
recipe. And the point: **the household that made it rarely ate it** — it was given as a tribute to the
doctor, the notary, the mayor. Hence *signora*.
https://www.fondazioneslowfood.com/en/slow-food-presidia/conca-casale-signora/

### F14. San Pietro Avellana and the truffles
**Verdict: CONFIRMED, with sharper figures and one caution.**
- Two fairs, and the old text has them the right way round: the **Mostra Mercato del Tartufo Bianco** in
  early November (1–3 November in recent years) and the **Fiera Nazionale del Tartufo Nero Estivo** on
  the second Sunday of August. https://www.teleaesse.it/eventi/mostra-mercato-del-tartufo-bianco-a-san-pietro-avellana-1-3-novembre/
  and https://eventimolise.it/event/san-pietro-avellana/cibo/xxxvi-fiera-del-tartufo-nero-estivo/
- **Caution: the edition numbers in circulation contradict each other** (a "37th" white-truffle fair
  reported in 2022 against a "29th" in 2024). Do not put an edition number in the text.
- The village has **just over 400 inhabitants**. https://www.ansa.it/molise/notizie/2023/10/27/mostra-mercato-del-tartufo-bianco-a-san-pietro-avellana_bc642a4c-9bc4-491e-9e99-6cdca98ef5cd.html
- **The scale, which the old text only gestures at: Molise supplies about 40% of Italy's truffle
  harvest**, and about 80% of it leaves the region.
  https://www.dissapore.com/notizie/molise-tartufo-in-crescita-con-il-40-della-raccolta-nazionale/
  and https://www.agi.it/lifestyle/news/2021-01-11/molise-diventa-patria-tartufo-10978317/

### F15. "Sold under other regions' names" — SUPPORTED, and the reason is documentary
**Verdict: CONFIRMED in substance.** Molise truffle has **no DOP or IGP**, "ed è proprio per questo
motivo che questo prezioso tubero viene confuso con quello di Alba o di Acqualagna" — with no
designation of origin, the tuber is sold as somebody else's.
https://www.molisenetwork.net/2021/01/14/molise-tartufo-raccolto-nazionale/
That is a stronger, checkable version of "a complaint you will hear from anyone in the trade there".

### F16. The olive oil
**Claim (old text):** "The region also presses good olive oil from the hills behind the coast."
**Verdict: CONFIRMED and far too vague.** There is a **Molise DOP extra virgin olive oil, registered in
2003**, from Aurina, **Gentile di Larino**, Oliva Nera di Colletorto and Leccino, which must make up at
least 80% of the grove. https://www.treccani.it/enciclopedia/molise-dop-olio-evo_(Dizionario-dei-prodotti-DOP-e-IGP)/
and https://www.qualigeo.eu/prodotto-qualigeo/molise-dop-olio-evo/

### F17. "The peppers that end up dried and ground into everything"
**Verdict: CANNOT VERIFY as a Molise product.** No named Molisan pepper, cultivar, PAT entry or
production area turned up. The famous dried southern peppers are Senise IGP (Basilicata) and the
Abruzzese *peperone dolce di Altino*. The paprika in pampanella is real; a Molisan pepper *crop* is not
sourced. Write around it — say the rub is dried sweet and hot pepper, without claiming the region grows
it — or attribute it to the pampanella disciplinare, which I could not open (see D3).

### F18. Winter food, mutton, and the dish the reading should have
**Verdict: the generalities are safe; here is the documented dish.**
**Pezzata di Capracotta**: sheep (not lamb) cut into pieces — *depezzata*, hence the name — and boiled
long with tomato, potato, chilli and wild herbs. Its origin is stated plainly as transhumance food: the
animals that did not survive the move between lowland and mountain pasture, cooked by the shepherds on
the road with what they had. The *sagra* is on the first Sunday of August on the Prato Gentile plateau
at about 1,420 m; the 2026 edition is the 60th.
https://www.ilgiornaledelcibo.it/pezzata-di-capracotta/ and https://capracotta.com/2-agosto-60-edizione-de-la-pezzata/
This is Molise's exact counterpart to Abruzzo's *pecora alla callara* (Abruzzo research F56), and it is
the honest way to put mutton on the page: the meat you eat is the animal that could not finish the walk.

## B. Reading 4 — Campobasso, Saepinum, Pietrabbondante, Agnone, Termoli

### F19. Campobasso's population, and "Italy's smallest regional capital" — WRONG
**Claim (old text):** "a town of about 45,000 people"; caption and recap: "the smallest of any Italian
region" / "Campobasso is Italy's smallest regional capital".
**Verdict: CORRECTED twice.**
- Population: **48,080 residents at 31 December 2025** (Istat, reported by ANSA);
  **47,359 at 31 May 2026** (it.wikipedia). Write "about 47,000", not 45,000.
  https://www.ansa.it/molise/notizie/2026/08/03/ancora-in-calo-la-popolazione-di-campobasso-48.080-residenti-nel-2025_f163993e-39c4-4051-9314-7785534c7622.html
- **Aosta is smaller: 33,098 inhabitants.** Aosta and Campobasso are the only two regional capitals
  under 50,000, and Campobasso is the **second** smallest.
  https://www.tuttitalia.it/valle-d-aosta/35-comuni/popolazione/ and
  https://www.immobiliare.it/news/vivere-a/italia/qual-e-la-regione-meno-popolosa-ditalia-240315/
  **This error appears twice (figure caption and recap bullet) and must be fixed in both.**

### F20. Castello Monforte
**Verdict: CONFIRMED, and the earthquake can be named.** Nicola II Monforte, called Cola, rebuilt it in
**1458** after the **Sannio earthquake of 1456** had left the citadel in rubble:
> "Dopo il disastroso terremoto del Sannio del 1456, la cittadella di Campobasso era ridotta in
> macerie" … "il feudatario Nicola II Monforte (o Cola) si occupò personalmente della ricostruzione
> integrale del castello"

A parchment of **1375** attests an earlier castle; traces of cyclopean walls suggest a Samnite
fortification on the same hill. The castle stands at about **790 m**, roughly 100 m above the town, with
the Monforte arms — a cross between four roses — over the drawbridge.
https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Castello_Monforte

### F21. The Misteri
**Verdict: CONFIRMED, with one wording fix.** Conceived by the sculptor **Paolo Saverio Di Zinno
(1718–1781) around 1740**; a wooden base carrying a wrought-iron structure that branches upward, with a
harness at each end for a child; the carriers' rhythm makes the iron sway, so the children appear to
fly. **Thirteen** go out, at **Corpus Domini**.
https://www.misterietradizioni.com/misteri/ and https://www.meravigliaitaliana.it/meraviglie/misteri-di-campobasso/
**Fix:** the old text says "each June". Corpus Christi is a moveable feast and can fall in late May.
Write "at Corpus Christi" and the problem disappears.
The blacksmiths matter here: the machines are the joint work of a sculptor and Campobasso's ironworkers,
which ties the procession to F22.

### F22. The cutlers — right about Campobasso, but the knife town is Frosolone
**Verdict: CONFIRMED with an addition the reading badly wants.**
- Campobasso: steel working "originated in the 15th century", and **Nicola di Monforte brought
  blacksmiths from France** to make weapons; under Ferrante Gonzaga in the sixteenth century the town
  became a major arms centre; scissors, paper knives and blades are still made there.
  https://molise.guideslow.it/la-nobile-arte-del-traforo-campobasso/
- **Frosolone**, in the province of Isernia, is the region's knife and scissors town: civil knife-making
  from the early 1800s, hundreds employed in the nineteenth century, **80 workshops still at mid-
  twentieth century**, and a **Museo dei Ferri Taglienti opened in 1997**. In the Spanish period
  Frosolone blades were said to rival Toledo's.
  https://turismoinmolise.com/frosolone/ and https://comune.frosolone.is.it/musei-di-frosolone/
  Frosolone also sits on the tratturo and is one of the communities named in Italy's UNESCO
  transhumance nomination (F6) — a town that made knives *and* walked sheep.

### F23. Saepinum — the walls, and the towers are not all round
**Claim (old text):** "walled in the years around the birth of Christ with a circuit of thirty-five
round towers and four gates"; facts box: "about 12 hectares with 35 towers and four gates".
**Verdict: 12 hectares CONFIRMED, four gates CONFIRMED, "thirty-five round towers" CORRECTED.**
- Circuit **1,270 m**, enclosing **about 12 hectares**; **35 towers originally**, spaced at 100 Roman
  feet (29.60 m); **27 survive**, of which **25 circular and two octagonal**. So the towers are mostly
  but not all round, and only 27 are there to see.
  https://www.romanoimpero.com/2010/07/saepinum-sepino-molise.html and
  https://www.sepino.net/turismo/c38-pacchetti-turistici-itinerari-a-sepino-ed-in-molise/la-zona-archeologica-di-altilia-saepinum-sepino-romana/
- **The date is documented, not approximate.** The dedicatory inscription on the gates — complete only
  on Porta Bojano — names Tiberius and Drusus, who paid for the work themselves:
  > "TI(berius) CLAUDIUS TI(beri) F(ilius) NERO … NERO CLAUDIUS TI(beri) F(ilius) DRUSUS … MURUM PORTAS
  > TURRIS D(e) S(ua) P(ecunia) F(eciundum) C(uraverunt)"

  dated **2 BC – AD 4**. Flanking it are two carved German prisoners, half-naked and in chains.
  (romanoimpero, as above; the prisoners also at sepino.net)
- The public buildings are all there: forum, capitolium, basilica with an internal peristyle of twenty
  columns, *macellum* with a hexagonal centre, baths, theatre. (same)

### F24. Saepinum's survival — the nuance the old text flattens
**Claim (old text):** "it was never worth destroying … from the eighteenth century farmers moved back
in and built their houses against the Roman walls, using the ruins as barns rather than as a quarry."
**Verdict: CORRECTED in detail.** Two things are off.
- The town did not simply fade where it stood: **in the ninth century the population moved uphill to
  Castellum Sepini**, modern Sepino, for safety. (romanoimpero, as above)
- The eighteenth-century farmhouses were built **with stone robbed from the ruins** — *spolia* — and
  they were built **on the theatre**, following the curve of the cavea so exactly that they repeat its
  plan. They are not barns leaning on the wall; they are houses standing in the auditorium, and the
  Antiquarium is inside them today.
  https://localitaitaliane.it/molise/campobasso/antiquarium-saepinum-altilia/ and
  https://www.paginedipoggio.com/?p=4159
  The reading's own point survives — the town was inhabited rather than erased — but the true image is
  better: a farmhouse whose front wall is the arc of a Roman theatre, with sink, hearth and bread oven
  inside it.
- **Excavations: from 1950**, with the first systematic work in the Porta Bojano sector under the
  superintendent **Valerio Cianfarani** in the mid-1950s. New campaigns 2023–2025; an imperial-period
  domus was announced in March 2026.
  https://localitaitaliane.it/molise/campobasso/antiquarium-saepinum-altilia/ and
  https://cultura.gov.it/comunicato/28824

### F25. The Porta Bojano inscription — the single best thing in either reading, and the old text gets the author wrong
**Claim (old text):** "Carved beside it is an imperial ruling that settled a dispute … The reply,
inscribed on the gate so that everybody could read it, told the magistrates to leave the flocks alone."
**Verdict: CONFIRMED in substance, CORRECTED in attribution, and enormously improvable.**
- **It is CIL IX 2438**, carved **on the southern pilaster of Porta Bojano, four metres above the
  tratturo that runs under the gate**, and dated **AD 169–172**, under Marcus Aurelius.
  https://arttrip.it/saepinum-altilia-sepino-citta-romana/ (location and height) and
  https://www.europeana.eu/en/item/2058806/EDR__0a004c23d0cce36a546bc6fda3eb5530__artifact__cho
  (EDR079028: "Date: 169-172 AD", findspot Saepinum)
- **It is not an emperor's ruling.** It is a dossier of three letters, posted in reverse order: a report
  from the imperial freedman **Septimianus** to his colleague **Cosmus**, the freedman *a rationibus*;
  Cosmus's request to the two praetorian prefects; and the prefects' warning letter to the magistrates,
  placed at the head. The prefects are **Bassaeus Rufus** and **Macrinius Vindex**.
  https://www.judaism-and-rome.org/infringement-imperial-rights-municipal-magistrates-cil-ix-2438
  (page returns 403 to automated fetching; content read through search extracts)
- **The Latin, as transcribed in EDR079028:**
  > "Bassaeus Rufus et Macṛịṇịụs Vindex m(agistratibus) Saepinat(ibus) salutem. Exemplum epistulae
  > scriptae nobis a Cosmo Aug(usti) lib(erto) a rationibus cum his, quae iuncta erant, subiecimus et
  > admonemus abstineatis iniuris faciendis conductoribus gregum oviaricorum, cum magna fisci iniuria,
  > ne necesse sit ṛecognosci de hoc et in factum, si ita res fuerit, vindicari."

  and Cosmus's own letter:
  > "…peto tanti faciatis scribere m(agistratibus) Saepin(atibus) et Bovian(ensibus), uti desinant
  > iniuriam conductoribus gregum oviaricoṛụm, qui sunt sub cura mea, facere, ut beneficio ṿẹstro ratio
  > fisci indemnis sit."

  (Europeana/EDR, as above)
- **What was actually happening:** when the flocks passed, the magistrates of **Saepinum and Bovianum**
  and the *stationarii* — the road police — stopped them, "affermando che i cavalli e i pastori da essi
  appaltati sono rispettivamente animali rubati e schiavi fuggiti": claiming the pack animals were
  stolen and the shepherds were runaway slaves. Septimianus had already tried to settle it locally with
  the magistrates responsible for the stationarii, and failed, before going up the chain.
  https://www.romanoimpero.com/2010/07/saepinum-sepino-molise.html and the Judaism-and-Rome commentary
- **The reason given is money, not justice.** Both letters turn on "magna fisci iniuria" and "ratio
  fisci indemnis" — grave damage to the treasury. The flocks were imperial; the shepherds were slaves;
  what the prefects protect is the revenue. The reading currently makes it sound like a ruling for the
  shepherds, which is exactly what it is not, and the truth is a better sentence.
- Chronology check: AD 169–172 is **about 1,850 years ago**, not two thousand. [calculated]

### F26. The Samnite wars
**Verdict: CONFIRMED.** Three wars: **343–341, 326–304 and 298–290 BC**; the Caudine Forks humiliation
in **321 BC**; peace in 290 leaving the Samnites as Roman allies.
https://www.skuola.net/storia-antica/roma-guerre-sannitiche.html and
https://www.prometheus-studio.it/filosofia_e_storia/2019/12/07/le-guerre-sannitiche-343-291-a-c/
"Came closer to breaking it than anyone before Hannibal" is a judgement, not a fact; the Caudine Forks
is the concrete thing to put in its place.

### F27. Pietrabbondante — the theatre and its seats
**Verdict: CONFIRMED, and richer than the old text.**
- **Theatre and the great temple (Temple B) built in the last decades of the 2nd century BC**, about
  100 BC. https://www.sitiarcheologiciditalia.it/santuario-di-pietrabbondante/
- The seats: **anatomical, each cut from a single block, with a backrest tilted slightly back**. Franco
  Valente stresses they are "sedili totalmente autonomi rispetto al resto del teatro" — three rows of
  free-standing seats in the *ima cavea*, with backs following the line of the spine, which he calls
  unprecedented in Mediterranean theatre architecture. The reason is that this was **an assembly
  building in the shape of a theatre**: the people who sat there sat for hours.
  https://www.francovalente.it/2008/02/24/il-teatro-ellenistico-sannitico-di-pietrabbondante/
  and https://arttrip.it/pietrabbondante-santuario-sanniti-teatro/
- Scale: orchestra about 52 m across, an analemma of polygonal megalithic blocks, roughly **6,000
  places**, six wedges and seven stairs (Valente). The seat-dividers are carved as **eagles** with wings
  and talons.
- **Temple B was paid for by a named Samnite magistrate, L. Statiis Klar.**
  https://www.sitiarcheologiciditalia.it/santuario-di-pietrabbondante/
- The "you will not find it in a Roman theatre" line is Valente's judgement and defensible as
  "unprecedented", but attribute it rather than state it as fact.

### F28. Pietrabbondante — the abandonment date is WRONG
**Claim (old text):** "The sanctuary was abandoned after the Social War, around 95 BC, and never reused."
**Verdict: CORRECTED on both halves.**
- **The Social War is 91–88 BC**, not 95. The "95" comes from the Italian Wikipedia formula that the
  site was the Samnites' chief sanctuary "tra il II secolo a.C. e il 95 a.C."
  (https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pietrabbondante), which is a terminus, not the war.
- **What ended it was Sulla, not the Social War.** The sanctuary was **confiscated immediately after
  Sulla's victory in the civil war**, the Samnites having backed the defeated Marian side, and it fell
  out of use with no restoration in the imperial period — that is **c. 82–80 BC**.
  https://www.sitiarcheologiciditalia.it/santuario-di-pietrabbondante/
  Why the buildings survived at all is openly unexplained in the literature; Valente offers guesses and
  labels them as such. That honest gap is worth keeping.
- **"Never reused" needs qualifying:** excavation found a late ritual of desacralisation, with deposits
  of lamps and coins of the **later 4th and early 5th century AD**, matching the imperial programme of
  closing pagan sites. (same source)
- Oscan inscriptions **CONFIRMED**, including fragments built into the village church of Santa Maria
  Assunta, and the medieval village built from stone off the site.
  https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pietrabbondante

### F29. Agnone and the Marinelli foundry
**Verdict: CONFIRMED, with three refinements.**
- **1339, Nicodemo Marinelli**, called *Campanarus*, cast a bell of about two quintals for a church in
  the Frosinone area; the record is kept in the foundry's museum. Origins "around the year 1000" are
  tradition, as the old text already admits. Twenty-seventh generation today.
  https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pontificia_fonderia_di_campane_Marinelli and
  https://museocampanemarinelli.it/%EF%BF%BCcampane-da-1000-anni/
- **Pius XI, 1924, the papal arms — CONFIRMED.**
  > "Nel 1924 il papa Pio XI conferì alla famiglia Marinelli l'onore di avvalersi dello stemma pontificio"
- **The bells: correct, but Pisa needs a caveat.** Pompeii 1923; Montecassino 1950; Buenos Aires
  cathedral; the Jubilee bell presented to John Paul II in 2000; the UN building in New York and FAO in
  Rome. The **Leaning Tower of Pisa** bell is a **600 kg replica cast to replace one damaged in the
  Second World War, installed at Easter 2004** — not a medieval Agnone bell.
  https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pontificia_Fonderia_Marinelli
- **The museum has a date:** the Museo storico della campana "Giovanni Paolo II", **founded 1999**.
- **"One of the oldest family firms in the world"** is safest as written. The claims in circulation are
  inconsistent — oldest, second oldest, third oldest — depending on the criteria.
  https://familybiz.it/1000-anni-di-attivita-per-26-generazioni-continuita-del-business-e-della-tradizione/

### F30. Termoli — castle, cathedral, coast
**Verdict: CONFIRMED, with datable detail.**
- **Castello Svevo**: an eleventh-century structure fortified a century later under Frederick II.
- **Cattedrale di Santa Maria della Purificazione**: built between the twelfth and thirteenth centuries,
  Apulian Romanesque, attributed to a local master, Alfano of Termoli. **The upper half of the facade is
  a rebuild after the 1456 earthquake** — the same earthquake that levelled Campobasso's citadel (F20),
  which links the two halves of the reading. The relics of **San Basso** were found in the church in
  **1760**, those of **San Timoteo** in **1945**; the crypt has a Romanesque mosaic floor and a marble
  slab dated **1238**.
  https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cattedrale_di_Santa_Maria_della_Purificazione and
  https://arttrip.it/cattedrale-di-termoli-romanico-pugliese/

### F31. The trabucchi — the old text is wrong about their age and their number
**Claim (old text):** "They are centuries old, they look like something washed up from a shipwreck, and
several still work."
**Verdict: CORRECTED on both counts.**
- **They are nineteenth-century machines.** The first Molisan trabucco is traditionally credited to
  **Felice Marinucci**, a Termoli fisherman who saw one near Ancona **around 1850** and built his own at
  Marina di San Pietro. A **notarial deed of 1793** for coastal land is argued by Giovanni De Fanis to
  push the Termoli presence back some decades — but that is late eighteenth century, not "centuries".
  https://termolionline.it/2026/06/attualita/quasi-150-anni-di-storia-i-trabucchi-a-termoli-e-si-guarda-anche-al-futuro/
  and https://www.primonumero.it/2026/06/i-trabucchi-di-termoli-una-storia-ricostruita-per-la-prima-volta-da-giovanni-de-fanis-in-un-libro/1530902218
- **"Several still work" is wrong. Two survive at Termoli**, one at the port and one at Marina di
  Sant'Antonio, and fishing from them has all but ceased. They are protected by **regional law 12/2020,
  amended by law 7/2022**, which defines them as heritage rather than as gear.
  https://termolionline.it/2025/09/attualita/termoli-e-il-piano-comunale-dei-trabucchi-storia-identita-e-futuro-di-un-patrimonio-che-torna-a-vivere/
**Recommendation:** "two survive at Termoli, and a regional law now protects them" is both true and a
sharper ending than "several still work".

### F32. The Tremiti
**Verdict: CONFIRMED.** The islands are in the **province of Foggia**, Puglia; ferries and hydrofoils run

https://www.traghettiper-tremiti.it/traghetti-termoli-tremiti/ and

### F33. The fish soup the landmark reading does not mention
**Verdict: CONFIRMED, and it belongs in reading 3 or 4.** Termoli's **u' bredette**: the evening soup of

https://www.dimoramonsignore.it/portfolio/zuppa-di-pesce-termoli/?lang=en and

## C. Material for the rewrite, and candidate openings

### F35. Isernia La Pineta — a Molise site the course does not mention at all
**Verdict: CONFIRMED and remarkable.** A Palaeolithic camp of about **700,000 years** ago, found by
chance by the researcher **Alberto Solinas in May 1979** during roadworks for a bypass of the SS 85;
four occupation phases sealed under alluvium and volcanic ash, over some 30,000 m².
https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isernia_La_Pineta
In **2014** a team from the University of Ferrara found a **milk tooth — an upper incisor, 7 mm — of a
child of five or six, dated about 586,000 years**. It is the **oldest human remain found in Italy**,
attributed to *Homo heidelbergensis*. It is displayed in a touchable case with a 3-D scan beside it, in
the Museo nazionale del Paleolitico, opened 1999.
https://www.unife.it/scienze/beni.culturali/notizie/il-piu-antico-resto-umano-ditalia-rinvenuto-nel-sito-paleolitico-di-la-pineta-di-isernia
and https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Museo_nazionale_del_paleolitico_di_Isernia

### F36. The Dogana registers name Molisan men and count their sheep
**Verdict: CONFIRMED — this is Molise's own material, not Abruzzo's.** **Capracotta is in Molise**
(province of Isernia), on the L'Aquila–Foggia tratturo, and the Foggia Dogana registers list its
*locati* by name, year and head of stock. From the transcribed table:
- **Mattia Pizzella: 9,005 head in 1740, 10,000 in 1750**; Giovanni Pizzella and brothers 3,850 in 1760;
  Giovanni Pizzella 2,510 in 1770 and 2,610 in 1780 — a family at ten thousand sheep and back down to
  two and a half thousand within forty years.
- **The Cappella della Madonna di Loreto** — a chapel — held **9,500 head in 1600, 21,210 in 1700**,
  17,980 in 1750, 13,024 in 1790, 13,334 in 1800.
- **The Duke**, 6,000–14,900 across 1690–1760; dr. Filippo del Baccaro 7,500 in 1600; Leone d'Andrea
  6,500 in 1600.
https://www.amicidicapracotta.com/2014/02/27/i-maggiori-locati-capracottesi-della-dogana-di-foggia-dal-1600-al-1800/
and https://www.letteraturacapracottese.com/post/dogana-foggia-maggiori-locati-capracotta
Scale of the classification, from the same sources: a small owner held up to 200 sheep, a medium one 200
to 2,000, a large one more. The registers survive at the **Archivio di Stato di Foggia**, in the Dogana
delle pecore fonds at Palazzo Dogana — the sheep were written down because they were taxed.
https://archiviodistatofoggia.cultura.gov.it/fileadmin/risorse/materiale_archivio/patrimonio_palazzodogana/Dogana_delle_pecore_Serie_I__bb.44-73_.pdf
(For the Foggia end of the same system — 8 ducats per hundred sheep, and 4,471,496 sheep counted in
1592 — see the Abruzzo research, F10 and F11.)

### F37. Detfri and Amica — two enslaved women who signed a roof tile at Pietrabbondante
**Verdict: CONFIRMED, and it is the most human documented object in Molise.** A clay roof tile,
**0.67 × 0.94 m**, from the sanctuary at Pietrabbondante, **about 100 BC**, probably made when a portico
beside a temple was re-roofed. Pressed into the wet clay: **four shoe prints, two pairs**, and two
inscriptions in two hands and two languages.
> Oscan, in the Central Oscan alphabet: **"hn. sattiieis. detfri"**
> Latin: **"segnatted. plavtad / herennis. amica / signauit. qando. a-/ponebamus. tegila(m)"**

"Detfri of Hn. Sattis signed with a footprint"; "Amica of Herens signed when we were laying out the
tile." Both are almost certainly enslaved women. *Amica* is a common slave name — "friend" — and she
declines her owner's name with an **Oscan genitive while writing in Latin**, so she was bilingual.
*Detfri* is a name found nowhere else; she may have been trafficked from elsewhere and kept it. They
wrote to each other, on the roof, in two scripts, and their feet are still in the clay.
https://katherinemcdonald.net/2016/01/14/four-footprints-two-languages-one-tile/
Corroborated as a bilingual tile with shoe impressions at
https://www.researchgate.net/figure/Terracotta-tile-from-Pietrabbondante-bearing-the-names-and-shoe-impressions-of-the_fig5_231903822
**What is NOT documented:** that they roofed the theatre, that they were the same women in any other
record, or anything about their lives. Everything beyond the tile is invention.

### F38. Jelsi, 26 July 1805
**Verdict: CONFIRMED.** The **Festa del Grano** at Jelsi dates from the earthquake of **26 July 1805**,
whose epicentre was near Baranello and which killed **more than 5,500 people across the Kingdom of
Naples**. At Jelsi **thirty died**, twenty-seven found on the day of the main shock and three after, and
the village has carried the statue of Sant'Anna in procession every 26 July since, in thanks. Villagers
weave kilometres of wheat plaits and lay them along the streets; ox-drawn *traglie* — wooden drags —
come through loaded with grain, worked ear by ear. About two hectares of wheat a year go into it.
https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Festa_del_grano_(Jelsi) and https://www.festadelgranojelsi.it/index.php/la-festa
Listed by the Istituto centrale per il patrimonio immateriale: https://icpi.cultura.gov.it/santanna-a-jelsi/
**Note for the rewrite:** reading 2's hero image is already Jelsi, so this connects.

### F39. The Carrese, and the ox-change at the tratturo
**Verdict: CONFIRMED.** At **San Martino in Pensilis — the pampanella town — on 30 April**, ox-carts race
a course of a little over **9 km** for the feast of San Leo, **changing oxen halfway, in the place called
Tratturo**. The legend behind it, from about the twelfth century, is that four noblemen found the
saint's body, quarrelled over the relics, and settled it by putting the body on an ox-cart and letting
it stop where it would; it stopped in San Martino's territory.
https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corsa_dei_carri_(San_Martino_in_Pensilis) and
https://turismoinmolise.com/carrese-corsa-buoi/
Documented in the national demo-ethno-anthropological catalogue:
https://catalogo.beniculturali.it/detail/DemoEthnoAnthropologicalHeritage/14iccd_modi_5416551306441
The reading's pork dish and the reading's drove road meet at the halfway point of this race. That is a
real link, not a manufactured one.

### F40. The 'Ndocciata of Agnone
**Verdict: CONFIRMED as practice; the Samnite origin is tradition, not evidence.** On **24 December**
(and the first Saturday of December) the quarters of Agnone — Capammonde e Capaballe, Colle Sente,
Guastra, San Quirico, Sant'Onofrio — carry fir-wood torches over **three metres** high, fanned out and
packed with dry broom that crackles as it burns, under the sound of the town's bells. A 'Ndocciata was
carried in St Peter's Square on **8 December 1996** for the fiftieth anniversary of John Paul II's
ordination, before about 30,000 people.
https://www.regione.molise.it/flex/cm/pages/ServeBLOB.php/L/IT/IDPagina/2459 and
https://turismoinmolise.com/ndocciata/
The claim that the Samnites carried torches on night movements is repeated everywhere with no source;
the documented layer is the eighteenth century onwards, when farmers lit the way to the churches on
Christmas Eve. Write it that way.
The bells and the fire belong to the same town, which is worth an economy of words.

### F41. San Giuliano di Puglia, 31 October 2002
**Verdict: CONFIRMED.** At **11:32 on 31 October 2002**, a magnitude **5.7** earthquake struck lower
Molise. At San Giuliano di Puglia the roof slab of the **Francesco Jovine school** — nursery, primary
and middle school in one building — came down on 57 children, 8 teachers and a caretaker. **Twenty-seven
children and one teacher died**; two more people died in other circumstances. Rescuers dug for thirty
hours, largely by hand; the last survivor, Angelo, was brought out at dawn on 1 November. The school was
rebuilt to seismic standards, and the disaster forced a new national seismic map in which **no part of
Italy is classed as risk-free**.
https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terremoto_del_Molise_del_2002,
https://emergenze.protezionecivile.gov.it/it/sismiche/terremoto-molise-2002/,
https://www.vigilfuoco.it/chi-siamo/memoria-storica/notizie-storiche/il-terremoto-del-molise-del-2002-e-la-tragedia-di-san-giuliano-di-puglia
**Handle with care.** An entire school year of one village died. It is not colour, and it should not open
a reading about cheese. If it is used at all, it belongs where the readings already talk about
earthquakes — 1456 at Campobasso and Termoli, 1805 at Jelsi — as the last term of a series, stated
plainly and briefly.

### F42. Larino, the region's other Roman town
**Verdict: CONFIRMED, with one figure to distrust.** Larino has a Roman **amphitheatre of the Flavian
period, about AD 81**, built at the expense of a wealthy citizen, **Quintus Capito**; baths; and
polychrome mosaics of the 2nd–3rd century, among them a she-wolf with Romulus and Remus as a house
floor. https://www.sitiarcheologiciditalia.it/larino/ and https://arttrip.it/larino-anfiteatro-cattedrale-mosaici/
**Distrust the capacity:** "up to 16,000 spectators" is repeated in tourism copy and is implausible for
a town of Larinum's size; if the figure is used at all, attribute it. Larino also gives its name to the
**Gentile di Larino** olive in the Molise DOP (F16), so it can carry the oil paragraph.

### F43. Candidate openings, ranked

1. **The letter four metres above the road (F25).** Saepinum, AD 169–172. A gate with a drove road
   running under it, and carved on the pilaster above, where anyone passing could see it, a chain of
   three letters ending with two praetorian prefects telling the magistrates of Saepinum and Bovianum to
   stop seizing the flocks — *cum magna fisci iniuria*, because it was costing the treasury. Named
   people, an exact document, a still-visible stone, and a twist: the shepherds are protected because
   they are property. **Best opening for reading 4, and arguably for reading 3.**
2. **Detfri and Amica (F37).** Two enslaved women, about 100 BC, pressing their shoes into a wet roof
   tile at Pietrabbondante and writing their names beside the prints — one in Oscan, one in Latin with
   an Oscan genitive. It opens on two people doing something, in a language dying out, at the sanctuary
   the reading is about, and it is carried by an object you can point at.
3. **Mattia Pizzella of Capracotta, 1740 (F36).** Nine thousand and five head entered against his name
   in the Foggia register, ten thousand by 1750, two thousand six hundred by 1780. A Molisan man in a
   Molisan village on the Molisan tratturo, counted because he was taxed. Opens reading 3 on the economy
   without a single adjective. (See also the chapel of the Madonna di Loreto running 21,210 sheep in
   1700 — a chapel, with twenty-one thousand sheep.)
4. **The pezzata at Capracotta (F18).** A cauldron at 1,420 m on the first Sunday of August, and the
   dish's own documented origin: the sheep that did not survive the move, cut into pieces and boiled all
   day by the men walking it. The exact counterpart of Abruzzo's callara, and the honest way to put
   mutton and the tratturo on the same page.
5. **The Signora of Conca Casale (F13).** The best cuts of the pig, knife-minced in the coldest days of
   winter by the women of the house, stuffed into the caecum they call *la zia*, aged six months — and
   then given away to the doctor, the notary and the mayor. A Slow Food Presidium, so the practice is
   documented, and the social fact is inside the name.
6. **The farmhouse in the theatre at Saepinum (F24).** Eighteenth-century peasants building along the
   curve of the cavea, so exactly that the houses repeat its plan, with hearth and bread oven inside;
   the Antiquarium is in them now. A concrete image of the reading's actual argument — that nothing
   happened here, and that is why it is still standing.
7. **Nicodemo Marinelli, 1339 (F29).** A bell of about two quintals cast for a church near Frosinone,
   the record still in the museum at Agnone, the twenty-seventh generation of the same family at the
   same furnace. A dated act by a named man, and the oldest thing in the region that never stopped.
8. **The child's tooth at Isernia (F35).** A seven-millimetre milk incisor, a child of five or six,
   586,000 years old, the oldest human remain in Italy, found in 1979 roadworks and displayed today in a
   case you can touch. For a region whose joke is that it does not exist.
