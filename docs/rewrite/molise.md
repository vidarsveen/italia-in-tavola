## The problem this region has that no other has had: the neighbour just used the material

Abruzzo 3 and 4 were drafted in the same session, immediately before these. Between them they have already
spent, in full:

- the *tratturi* as a system: the legal width of sixty Neapolitan paces and 111.6 metres, the stone markers,
  the 243 km of the Tratturo Magno, the ~3,100 km network, the fortnight on the road, the seven to ten
  shepherds per thousand sheep, and the cheesemaker who walked with them;
- the **Dogana della Mena delle Pecore** in full: 1447, the eight ducats a hundred, the 1592 count of
  4,471,496 sheep and 622,173 ducats, the abolition on 21 May 1806, and a customs post on the road at Rocca
  Calascio;
- **transhumance at UNESCO in 2019**, extended in 2023;
- **the animal that could not finish the walk, boiled all day in a cauldron** — Abruzzo 3 opens on *pecora
  alla callara*;
- **ventricina**, both the vastese and the spreadable teramana.

Molise reading 3 is titled "Caciocavallo and the transhumance routes". It cannot re-tell any of that.

**The division of labour, decided here.** Abruzzo owns the road and the tax. **Molise owns the people on the
register and what they made to keep.** What is left, and is specifically Molisan rather than generic:

- **450 kilometres of tratturo inside Molise, covering about 4,086 hectares**, in a region of 4,460 km² — all
  five royal tratturi cross it (F4; the old text's "four of the five" is wrong). Abruzzo described the road;
  Molise is the crossroads, and that is a different sentence.
- **The Foggia registers name Molisan men** (F36): Mattia Pizzella of **Capracotta**, 9,005 head in 1740,
  10,000 by 1750, 2,610 by 1780; and the **Cappella della Madonna di Loreto**, a chapel, running 9,500 head
  in 1600 and **21,210 in 1700**. Abruzzo 4 used the tax; Molise 3 uses the names inside it.
- **What a shepherd's dairy invented to keep**: the *burrino* or *manteca*, a ball of butter wrapped in a skin
  of stretched curd so it would keep without a cold room (F9). That is the single best image in the Molise
  dairy and Abruzzo has nothing like it.
- **The Signora di Conca Casale** (F13): the best cuts of the pig, knife-minced in the coldest days of winter,
  stuffed into the caecum the village calls *la zia*, aged six months — and then **given away** to the doctor,
  the notary and the mayor by a household that rarely ate it. Nothing in Abruzzo touches this.
- **Pezzata di Capracotta** (F18) is Molise's exact counterpart to the callara, and **must not be given the
  callara's treatment or its position.** Abruzzo 3 opens on a sheep that died on the road, jointed into a
  cauldron. Molise may name the pezzata, and should, but as a festival on a plateau at 1,420 m on the first
  Sunday of August, not as a second discovery of the same idea. One clause on the shared origin, no more.

## Openings already heard, and the shapes now spent

The listener reaches Molise 1 having just heard, in order: Lazio 3 a dated municipal order; Lazio 4 an
excavation and what was in the drain; **Abruzzo 1 an absence** ("In 2007 the Valentini estate made no red wine
at all"); **Abruzzo 2 a mechanism under a clock** ("Montepulciano's skins carry so much pigment…");
**Abruzzo 3 a pot on a tripod, on the road** ("A sheep that could not finish the walk was not left behind");
**Abruzzo 4 a tunnel under the snow** ("In winter there was no way onto Campo Imperatore except the cable car").

Shapes spent across the fourteen regions rewritten so far, not to be reused: a sales figure with a year; a
person's age and trade with a year; a person at work in the early morning; two things poured side by side; a
queue at a cart; a vehicle climbing; an army crossing a pass; a man going out at night with a dog; a named
grower who says something; a competition with a date; a market word explained; a grant or ruling with a date;
goods leaving by rail; people given a deadline to leave; a figure in a wall painting; a traveller noticing a
rock; a cellar running low; a quotation; fishermen drying fish; objects on a table made of sugar; hail
destroying a crop; a dated document; a habitual work crew going out; a shop sign; a cargo moving at night;
one plot across two generations; an absence; a mechanism under a clock; a pot on the road; a tunnel.

**Chosen shapes, the first words of each lead, and the grammatical opening move.** This last column is new,
and it is the lesson the Abruzzo reviews forced (see `abruzzo.md`, "Second pass"): a reader reviewer heard
Lazio 2, Lazio 3 and Abruzzo 1 as a formula not because their subjects were alike — they were not — but
because **all three fronted a prepositional or adverbial phrase before the subject**. Changing the entrance to
a scene does not change the shape of the sentence, and the shape is what carries across four chapters. So
every Molise lead below opens **subject-first**, and the variety has to come from what kind of subject it is.

| # | Shape | First words | Opening move | Subject |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | A name taken away by a court | "A region, a province and a wine consortium…" | subject-first, plural institutions | three bodies |
| 2 | A man writing a request | "Raffaele Pepe, an agronomist at Civitacampomarano, wrote down…" | subject-first, named person, past | one person |
| 3 | A thing made to be given away | "The best cuts of the pig go into it…" | subject-first, a thing, habitual present | an object |
| 4 | Two people signing a roof tile | "Two women pressed their shoes into wet clay…" | subject-first, two people, past | two people |

**No lead begins with a year, a date or a preposition.** Reading 1 and reading 2 both turn on a document, so
reading 1's must be an institution acting and reading 2's a person writing — and reading 2 must not open on
the year 1810, nor on the quotation, which a listener cannot hear the marks around (the Umbria rule).

## What the readings must keep answering

Quiz (`content/quiz.js` / `.no.js`, `window.QUIZ['IT-67']`). Six of the twelve rest on claims the research has
overturned or left unsupported:

- **1.1** how Molise became a region — **stands**, and the `why` gains the nuance: constitutional law of
  December 1963, region in being January 1964, one province until Isernia was created in 1970 (W2).
- **1.2** why a Norwegian reader has never drunk it — the answer stands, but the **`why` must be rewritten**:
  it rests on "a few dozen bottling estates" (unverifiable, W17) and "Vinmonopolet currently lists a single
  Molise wine" (unverifiable and perishable, W15). Re-found it on the sourced arithmetic: Biferno DOC is
  **48 hectares** and Molise DOC **177**, together about 7,690 hl against a regional 319,000 hl — **under 3%**
  (W10, W11, W16).
- **1.3** why Montepulciano was removed in 2000 — **stands**, and improves by naming the three challengers.
- **2.1** what the 200-metre rule achieves — **must be rewritten.** The `why` cites "between 200 and 600
  metres" (the ceiling is unsourced, W29) and "fast and flabby" (unsourced, and it rests on the false Naples
  latitude, W28). Re-found on what the rule actually says — hillside land, not below 200 m — and on the
  Biferno contrast.
- **2.2** what is documented about Tintilia — **stands** and sharpens: name Raffaele Pepe and 1810.
- **2.3** which description fits Tintilia — the answer stands; the **`why` must drop the northern Rhône Syrah
  comparison**, which no source makes (W30). The disciplinare's own «speziato», for the Riserva, replaces it.
- **3.1** the width of a royal tratturo — **stands**.
- **3.2** where *caciocavallo* gets its name — the answer stands but the **`why` must be hedged**: Treccani
  gives the hanging-astride derivation as one of several, alongside the shape, a stamped mark and Turkish
  *kaşkaval* (F7).
- **3.3** what pampanella is — the answer stands; the **`why` must drop "cheaper cuts, often the ribs"**: the
  sources list belly, capocollo, leg and ribs, and capocollo and leg are not cheap (F11).
- **4.1** why Saepinum is so complete — **must be rewritten.** "Farmers reoccupied it and used the ruins as
  barns" is wrong twice: the population left uphill to Castellum Sepini in the ninth century, and the
  eighteenth-century houses were built **of** robbed stone **inside the theatre**, following the curve of the
  cavea (F24).
- **4.2** what the Porta Bojano inscription records — **must be rewritten.** It is not "an imperial order": it
  is a dossier of three letters headed by the praetorian prefects **Bassaeus Rufus** and **Macrinius Vindex**,
  AD 169–172, and the stated motive is **damage to the treasury**, not the shepherds' welfare (F25).
- **4.3** the seats at Pietrabbondante — the answer stands; the **`why` must correct "abandoned around 95 BC"**
  to confiscation after Sulla's victory, c. 82–80 BC (F28).

## Checked before drafting — the errors in the old text

**Readings 1 and 2** (from `molise-research-wine.md`):

- **W11, the hardest error: the Molise DOC is 1998, not 1988.** Three independent sources. The recap bullet
  repeats it.
- **W28: "Molise is a southern region, on the same latitude as Naples" is false.** Campobasso is at 41.56° N
  and Naples at about 40.85°; **the whole region lies north of Naples**, Campobasso by some eighty kilometres.
  The reading uses the claim to argue Molise is too hot and needs altitude, so the argument has to be rebuilt
  on what is true: **no plain at all**, 55.3% mountain and 44.7% hill, and two appellations dividing the same
  slope — Biferno capped **above** at 500 m, Tintilia floored **below** at 200 m (W5, W10, W24).
- **W14: the region has four DOCs and the reading knows three.** **Pentro di Isernia DOC, 18 May 1983**,
  seventeen comuni in the province of Isernia — and its red and rosato **require Tintilia at 20–25%**. So the
  state wrote Tintilia into an appellation **twenty-eight years before the grape got its own**, which
  complicates reading 2's rescue-from-nothing story. Say both.
- **W19/W20: the Tintilia origin story is the wrong way round.** Every wine page prefers Spanish *tinto*, with
  hedges — *presumibilmente*, *potrebbe*. But the single oldest document, Raffaele Pepe's manuscript of 1810,
  calls the vine **«Le Tenturier d'Espagne = Tintiglia»**, which is a **French** varietal name, says it came
  **from France**, and asks for **«marze forastiere»**, cuttings from outside. And the DNA matches nothing in
  Spain (W21). Three answers, none proven. Stage it; do not choose.
- **W25: the Riserva yield is misstated.** The disciplinare sets **8 t/ha for all three types alike**. What
  differs is the grape-to-wine conversion, **70% for Rosso and Rosato against 55% for the Riserva**. The
  reading's instinct (a Riserva yields less wine) is right; the rule it cites is not.
- **W31: "Cantine Cipressi" and "Claudio Cipressi" are one estate**, listed as two. Delete one. Claudio
  Cipressi, San Felice del Molise: 16 ha of which 11 Tintilia, about 50,000 bottles, vines at 450 m, and
  president of the Consorzio.
- **W8: Campobasso is not 45,000.** 47,519 at the end of 2024, 48,080 at the end of 2025, 47,359 in May 2026.
  Write "about forty-seven thousand". The figure appears in reading 1 **and** in reading 4's prose, caption
  and recap.
- **W24: "which is unusual in an appellation's rules" is unsupported** — minimum-altitude clauses are not rare
  — and **"is there to keep the wine fresh" is the growers' argument, not a recorded legislative purpose.**
- **W9: "falling by about seven per cent in the decade to 2021" is unsupported.** What is documented is a peak
  of about **331,000 in 1951** against **284,970 in 2026**.
- **W6**: the coast is 36 km, not "about thirty-five" stated as exact. **W15**: the Vinmonopolet sentence is
  unverifiable and perishable — replace it with the 3% calculation. **W16**: "sold as bulk wine to blenders
  elsewhere" is supported as to bulk but **not as to the destination** — that finding belongs to Abruzzo and
  must not be carried across the border. **W31**: Di Majo Norante's labels are largely Molise DOC; do not say
  they make Biferno without checking. **B6: Alessio Di Majo Norante died on 19 April 2026**, so the reading's
  present tense is out of date exactly as Abruzzo's was about Emidio Pepe.

**Readings 3 and 4** (from `molise-research-food.md`):

- **F4: all five royal tratturi cross Molise**, not four.
- **F5: Alfonso founded the Dogana at Lucera in 1447; the seat moved to Foggia in 1468 under his son
  Ferrante.** The old text has Alfonso moving it. (Abruzzo 4 says "founded at Lucera by Alfonso of Aragon and
  later moved to Foggia", which is right and stays.)
- **F6: the UNESCO sentence is misleading by omission.** The roads are still only on Italy's **tentative
  list**, where they went in 2006; **the walking of them was inscribed in 2019**.
- **F12: the ventricina is exactly backwards.** Montenero di Bisaccia's is the **lean, long-aged, sliceable**
  one — the leanest and longest-aged of the three — and the spreadable one is the Abruzzese *teramana*.
- **F19: Campobasso is not Italy's smallest regional capital.** Aosta has about 33,100. Campobasso is the
  **second** smallest. The error appears in a caption and in a recap bullet.
- **F23: Saepinum's towers.** Thirty-five originally, **twenty-seven survive, twenty-five circular and two
  octagonal**. And the date is documented rather than approximate: the dedication on Porta Bojano names
  **Tiberius and Drusus**, who paid for the walls themselves, **2 BC – AD 4**.
- **F24: the survival story is better than the one told.** The population moved uphill to Castellum Sepini in
  the ninth century; the eighteenth-century farmhouses were built **of** stone robbed from the ruins and stand
  **in the theatre**, following the curve of the cavea so exactly that they repeat its plan, with hearth and
  bread oven inside. The Antiquarium is in them now.
- **F25: the Porta Bojano text is not an imperial ruling**, and the motive is not kindness. See the quiz note
  above. *Cum magna fisci iniuria* — grave damage to the treasury. The flocks were imperial and the shepherds
  were slaves; what the prefects protect is the revenue. **That is a better sentence than the one the reading
  has**, and it is the reading's hinge.
- **F28: Pietrabbondante.** The Social War is **91–88 BC**, not 95; what ended the sanctuary was **Sulla**,
  c. 82–80 BC, the Samnites having backed the losing side; and "never reused" needs qualifying against a
  desacralisation deposit of lamps and coins of the later fourth and early fifth century AD.
- **F31: the trabucchi are nineteenth-century and there are two.** The first Molisan one is credited to
  **Felice Marinucci** of Termoli, about **1850**, after he saw one near Ancona; a notarial deed of 1793 is
  argued to push the presence back some decades, which is still not "centuries old". **Two survive at
  Termoli**, and fishing from them has all but ceased; **regional law 12/2020** protects them as heritage.
- **F29: the Pisa bell is a 600 kg replica installed at Easter 2004**, not a historic Agnone casting.
- **F11: pampanella's cuts** include capocollo and leg; the vine leaves **wrapped** the meat; the sixteenth
  century is undocumented. What is documented and better: a **De.Co.** from the comune with its own
  disciplinare, and a place on the regional traditional-products list since **1999**.
- **F7: caciocavallo's *a cavallo* etymology is one of four** in Treccani. **F10:** cavatelli "first mentioned
  by Frederick II" is unsupported — do not write it. **F17:** a Molisan **pepper crop** is unsupported; the
  paprika in pampanella is real, the growing of it here is not. **F21:** the Misteri go out at **Corpus
  Christi**, a moveable feast that can fall in late May, so "each June" is wrong. **F26:** "came closer to
  breaking Rome than anyone before Hannibal" is a judgement — the **Caudine Forks of 321 BC** is the concrete
  thing to put in its place.

## 1. A region rediscovered

- Summary → a region that was told what it could not call its own wine, and what it kept.
- Lead (a name taken away by a court): in 1998 Molise was given a denomination that let it put *Montepulciano*
  on a label; within two years the Regione Toscana, the Provincia di Siena and the Consorzio del Vino Nobile
  went to the TAR del Lazio, and a decree of 10 October 2000 struck the words out. The growers kept the grape
  and lost the word. **One clause of cross-reference only** to the fact, established two chapters earlier,
  that the Tuscan wine is Sangiovese and has no connection with the grape at all — and no repetition of
  Abruzzo 1's explanation.
- What the region is, entered through that defeat rather than through a definition: the constitutional law of
  December 1963, in being January 1964, one province until Isernia in 1970, **the only Italian region ever
  formed by detaching from another**; 4,460 km², 285,000 people, under half a per cent of the country;
  55.3% mountain, 44.7% hill, **no plain at all**, and the vineyard sharing exactly that split. The meme goes
  here, dated to the early-2000s forums and 2013–14, and **not** at the top.
- The shape of the land as the shape of the wine: the Biferno from Pietrecadute at 500 m to the estuary
  between Termoli and Campomarino, the Guardialfiera reservoir of the 1960s and 70s; and the fact that gives
  the region its geometry — **Biferno capped above at 500 m, Tintilia floored below at 200 m**, two
  appellations stacked on one hillside. Drop "the warmest, broadest and most planted land", which is
  unsourced.
- **Four names on the label, not three** (the old title of the section must change): Biferno 1983, Pentro di
  Isernia 1983 with its compulsory 20–25% Tintilia, Molise **1998**, Tintilia del Molise 2011.
- Why you have never drunk it, re-founded on arithmetic (quiz 1.2): **Biferno is forty-eight hectares** and
  Molise DOC a hundred and seventy-seven; together about 7,690 hl against a regional 319,000 — under three per
  cent of what Molise makes carries either of its two main names. Then the co-operatives with their dates and
  their member counts, and San Zenone's own description of what it sells: bottle, bag-in-box and bulk. **Do
  not import Abruzzo's finding that the bulk goes to blenders elsewhere; it is not documented here.**
- The emigration, with the sourced numbers only: about 331,000 in 1951 against 284,970 now.
- Ending: on the word rather than the wine — a region that makes three per cent of its wine under its own
  names, and had one of those names taken off the label by a court in another region.

## 2. Tintilia: a grape saved from extinction

- Summary → the grape's own founding document disagrees with the story told about it.
- Lead (a man writing a request, name before date): Raffaele Pepe, an agronomist at Civitacampomarano, writing
  in 1810, calling the vine «Le Tenturier d'Espagne = Tintiglia» and asking for **«marze forastiere»**, foreign
  cuttings, to give body and colour to wines that had neither. **Do not open on the year**, and do not open on
  the quotation.
- The name (quiz 2.2): three answers and none proven. The folk etymology says Spanish *tinto*, and the sources
  that give it hedge it — *presumibilmente*, *potrebbe*. The oldest document gives it a **French** name and
  says it came from France, and asks to import it. The DNA matches nothing in Spain. Note the unresolved
  puzzle: a *teinturier* is properly a red-fleshed dyer grape, and no source says Tintilia is one.
- The catalogue was wrong (the strongest sourcing in either reading): Reale, Pilla and Angiolillo, 2006,
  thirteen SSR loci across thirty-seven accessions; the Molise samples identical to each other and different
  from every other 'Tintilia' and from Spain; **Tintilia and Bovale grande shown to be different varieties
  "even though they have been reported in the *Vitis International Variety Catalogue* as being identical"**;
  Pilla's own caveat kept. And the error still in circulation, with reference sites listing Bovale names as
  synonyms twenty years on.
- Who saved it, with the names the old text leaves out and the dispute staged (not resolved): Michele Tanno
  walking the region in the early 1990s collecting material from surviving vines; Claudio Cipressi replanting
  at 450 m at San Felice del Molise; Giuseppe Mogavero, five terms on the regional council, planting it at
  Petrella Tifernina in 1975 and called its discoverer by his obituarist. Two candidates, both in circulation.
- **The rescue was not from nothing** (the correction the research forces): Pentro di Isernia, 1983, requires
  Tintilia at 20–25% of its red — the state named the grape twenty-eight years before it had a DOC of its own.
  Then 0 hectares at the 2000 census, 111 by 2010, about a hundred now with about thirty wineries, and the
  Consorzio founded in 2017 by seven producers.
- The rules (quiz 2.1, rewritten): 2011; **95% Tintilia**; 11.5% and 13% for the Riserva, which ages two years
  from 1 November; **8 t/ha for all three types**, with the Riserva's press yield cut from 70% to 55%; and the
  altitude clause in its own words, a floor of 200 m **on hillside land**, both conditions — set against
  Biferno's ceiling of 500. Not "unusual"; specific.
- Tasting (quiz 2.3, rewritten): the disciplinare's own «rosso rubino intenso, con riflessi violacei» and,
  for the Riserva, **«speziato»**. Drop the northern Rhône comparison, or mark it as what drinkers reach for.
  **The Rosato is «rosato più o meno intenso»**, which permits pale, so the "deep pink, not Provençal" claim
  goes. Tasting table and box.
- The growers: Di Majo Norante's 123 hectares, organic since 19 October 1996, Cotarella from the mid-1990s,
  and **Alessio's death on 19 April 2026** — he went to Vinitaly to the end. **Cipressi is one estate, not
  two.** Grieco's 4.5 hectares at 300 m.
- Ending (the limit of the claim): the 200 metres is not settled. On 9 August 2026 at San Martino in Pensilis
  the denomination's own president said the DOC was not to be touched, while a farmers' union and the San
  Zenone co-operative argued the line is starving the growers who have to obey it. Nothing was decided. And a
  grower down at Portocannone has printed the rule on his label and called the wine **«200 Metri»**.

## 4. Campobasso and the hill villages

- Summary → what survives here survives because nothing came after it.
- Lead (two people signing a roof tile): about a hundred years before Christ, at the sanctuary of
  Pietrabbondante, two women pressed their shoes into a wet clay tile — four prints, two pairs — and wrote
  their names beside them. **Detfri** wrote in Oscan. **Amica** wrote in Latin, and declined her owner's name
  with an Oscan genitive, so she had both languages. Both were almost certainly slaves; *Amica* is a slave's
  name, and *Detfri* is found nowhere else. Nothing else about them is known, and nothing else is to be
  claimed.
- Pietrabbondante: the sanctuary and the theatre of the late second century BC; the anatomical seats, each
  from a single block, backs following the line of the spine, dividers carved as eagles, about six thousand
  places — and Valente's explanation, attributed, that this was an assembly building in the shape of a
  theatre, where people sat for hours. Temple B paid for by L. Statiis Klar. The Samnite wars and the Caudine
  Forks of 321 BC instead of the Hannibal judgement. Confiscated after Sulla, c. 82–80 BC; a desacralisation
  deposit of lamps and coins in the later fourth and early fifth century AD; Oscan fragments built into the
  village church.
- **Saepinum, and the letter four metres above the road**: the walls paid for by Tiberius and Drusus, 2 BC to
  AD 4, 1,270 metres of circuit round twelve hectares, thirty-five towers of which twenty-seven survive,
  twenty-five round and two octagonal, two carved German prisoners flanking the gate. Then CIL IX 2438 on the
  southern pilaster of Porta Bojano, over the drove road that still runs under the arch: three letters posted
  in reverse order, the praetorian prefects Bassaeus Rufus and Macrinius Vindex at the head, telling the
  magistrates of Saepinum and Bovianum to stop injuring the contractors of the sheep flocks — **because it was
  costing the treasury.** The magistrates had been stopping the flocks and calling the animals stolen and the
  shepherds runaway slaves. **The shepherds are protected because they are property**, and that is the
  sentence, not the one the old text has.
- Why it is still standing (quiz 4.1, rewritten): the town did not simply fade where it stood — the people
  went uphill to Castellum Sepini in the ninth century; and the eighteenth-century farmhouses were built **of**
  stone robbed from the ruins, **inside the theatre**, following the curve of the cavea so exactly that they
  repeat its plan, with hearth and bread oven in them. The Antiquarium is in those houses now. Excavation from
  1950 under Cianfarani.
- Campobasso: about forty-seven thousand people, **the second smallest regional capital after Aosta**;
  Castello Monforte rebuilt in 1458 after the 1456 earthquake, at 790 m, the arms over the drawbridge; the
  Misteri at **Corpus Christi**, thirteen of them, Di Zinno's frames of about 1740 and the town's ironworkers
  who made them. The cutlers kept general, and **Frosolone** given the weight, because that is where the
  museum and the workshop count are — a town that made knives and walked sheep.
- Agnone: Nicodemo Marinelli's bell of 1339, the record in the museum, the twenty-seventh generation, the
  papal arms in 1924, the bells at Pompeii and Montecassino and Buenos Aires — **and Pisa's a replica of
  2004.**
- Termoli, and the coast as the way out: the Swabian castle, the cathedral whose upper facade was rebuilt
  after **the same 1456 earthquake** that levelled Campobasso's citadel — which is what ties the two ends of
  the reading together — and *u' bredette*, the evening soup of what did not sell, with the sweet green pepper
  that runs up this coast from Vasto. The trabucchi corrected: about 1850, not centuries; **two left**, barely
  fished from, protected by a regional law of 2020. The Tremiti, in Puglia, reached from Molise.
- Ending: an image, not a summary. The two trabucchi, or the farmhouse standing in the Roman theatre, or the
  tile — a callback to Detfri and Amica, whose feet are still in the clay.
