**Checked before drafting** (a research pass on twenty doubtful or wanted claims, sources below). Errors found in
the old text: Turin's café culture does not predate Vienna's (Vienna licensed its first coffee house in 1685; Al
Bicerin is 1763); Lombardy, not Piemonte, has the most DOP cheeses (14 against Piemonte's 10); "tar and roses" is
English wine-writing, not what locals say; Nebbiolo is about 9% of Piedmont's vineyard (ISTAT 2010: 4,480 ha), not
6%; the truffle season is 1 October–31 January since 2024; a white truffle at several thousand euros a kilo is not
"worth more than gold by weight"; Piazza San Carlo is not where vermouth was born (Carpano worked in Piazza Castello).

## 1. Nebbiolo and the Langhe hills
- Summary → one sentence: Nebbiolo buds first and is picked last, in the October fog, and on the marl of these hills
  gives a pale wine built to last for decades.
- Lead: first to bud, last picked → frost risk and October fog → the name probably from *nebbia*, with the two other
  explanations given and left open → the ridges, villages and castles.
- Chain: late ripening → the best south-facing slopes → so under a tenth of Piedmont's vines, Barbera nearly a
  third → particular about marl from the old sea → rarely the same wine elsewhere (Valtellina, Donnas, experiments)
  → at home Langhe, Roero, Alto Piemonte. The wine: pale and misleading, roses, tar and dried cherry, "tar and
  roses" in English after a 1990 book, tannin and acidity → decades. Every hill named → 1961 first single-vineyard
  bottlings → Ratti's map 1976 → 181 MGA in 2010 → Cannubi as Chambertin. The slopes Nebbiolo leaves: Barbera,
  Dolcetto, and Cortese at Gavi. Ending: the view from La Morra, the October fog in the bowl, and the harvest.
- Box: UNESCO, DOCG 1980, soils kept; the 6% line corrected. Tasting table unchanged.
- Ledger: all old facts kept except as noted. **Changed, sourced**: "Locals describe it as tar and roses" → English
  wine writers, after the 1990 book; "about 6%" → under a tenth (ISTAT 2010); the name "from the fog" → most likely,
  with the bloom and *nobile* as alternatives. **New, sourced**: Prunotto and Vietti 1961; Ratti 1976; that Barolo was
  usually a blend of slopes before. Donnas added to the list of places outside Piemonte (the course's own Valle
  d'Aosta 2). Cut: "and the next reading goes through them one by one" (page reference); "remember the principle"
  (a summary); "sulks" (personification) → "rarely gives the same wine"; "Italy's answer to Burgundy" (summary line only).

## 2. Barolo and Barbaresco, village by village
- Summary → Barolo was sweet until the 1830s and Barbaresco had no wine of its own name until 1894, and since then
  growers have argued over both village by village and cask by cask.
- Lead: sweet and fizzy Barolo, why (late harvest, cold cellars, yeast stops, starts again in spring) → Cavour hires
  Staglieno at Grinzane in 1836 → fermented dry → the Oudart story and why the documents point elsewhere → the
  court, "king of wines". The Marchesa's 325 casks told as legend.
- Barolo villages: two marls, five villages, ten years to open, forty to live; ageing in the box.
- Barbaresco: grapes sold to Barolo makers → Cavazza, Alba's school, 1894 cooperative, first wine under the name →
  closed in the 1920s → Don Fiorino Marengo, 1958 → so "the queen" was lesser for reasons outside the glass → lower
  hills near the Tanaro → ripens earlier, released a year sooner, rounder. Three crus in prose, the fourth in the box.
- The wars: the old way (weeks on skins, years in botti) → Burgundy and export → barriques → Altare's chainsaw and
  his father → Mascarello's label → faded, some of each, new oak out of fashion.
- Tuesday: Langhe Nebbiolo, Roero and Nebbiolo d'Alba, Barbera. Ending: brasato al Barolo, the same wine in the pot
  and on the table.
- Box: label box kept, plus both ageing rules (Riserva 50 for Barbaresco new, sourced), the four Barbaresco communes
  and Santo Stefano. Tasting table unchanged.
- Ledger: all old facts kept. **New, sourced**: sweet Barolo and its cause; Staglieno and Cavour at Grinzane
  1836; the Oudart attribution and its weakness; the Marchesa legend; Cavazza 1881/1894; closure in the 1920s;
  Produttori 1958; Altare; Mascarello. Cut: "is the heart of any Piemonte wine education" (announcing); "which is the
  Piedmontese way of saying that food and wine here were never meant to be separated" (a summary with a verdict).

## 3. Truffles, tajarin and bagna cauda
- Summary → autumn is truffle season, when a dog finds what nobody can farm, and the rest of the year the kitchen

  at night, in secret → what comes out of the ground.
- Truffle: hosts, cannot be farmed, season, fair, price, smell, shaved over hot food because heat releases it (quiz);

- Butter, yolks and veal (old lead moved here): Alpine winters, French manners at the Savoy court → the Sunday lunch
  in the order it arrives, each dish with its wine where the old text gave one.
- Bagna cauda: the dish → why a landlocked region eats anchovy: the salt roads from Liguria and Nice → the acciugai
  of the Valle Maira → the smuggling legend and why it does not hold → the fish poor kitchens could reach. Ending: the
  wine and the nights that last until the pot is empty.
- Box: fair dates and cheese line corrected; season and dog law added.
- Ledger: all old facts kept. **Changed, sourced**: season "late September to December" / "October and November" →
