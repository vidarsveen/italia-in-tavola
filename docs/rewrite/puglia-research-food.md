# Puglia readings 3 and 4 — research and fact-check

Research pass for the rewrite of `content/puglia.js` lesson 3 ("Orecchiette, burrata and the bread of
Altamura") and lesson 4 ("Trulli, castles and the Adriatic"). Verdicts are CONFIRMED / CORRECTED /
DISPUTED / UNVERIFIABLE. Every finding carries a URL and, where it matters, the source's own words.

Labels used: [producer] = a firm selling the thing; [promotional] = a consortium, trade body or tourist
board; [trade press] = the food/restaurant trade papers; [popular history] = an unfootnoted local-history
site; [calculated] = arithmetic done here, not quoted from a source.

**Note on method and on one hard limit.** The bread section below was worked from the primary document:
the full annex of Commission Regulation (EC) No 1291/2003 was read directly on EUR-Lex and is quoted
verbatim throughout. **The session's WebSearch budget (200 calls) was exhausted** during the parallel
passes; a handful of confirmations named in section H could not be closed and are left explicitly
unverified rather than guessed. Three hosts refused automated fetching: nytimes.com, loebclassics.com
(403) and eur-lex's Italian HTML rendering of the annex (thin extraction only).

## A. Reading 3 — the bread of Altamura

The single richest vein in this reading, and the one where the old text is closest to repeating
consortium copy. Everything in A1–A8 is from the registered specification unless marked otherwise.

### F1. "The first bread in Europe with a protected designation" — true only if you say *designation of origin*
**Claim (old text):** "**Pane di Altamura DOP** (2003), the first bread in Europe with a protected
designation". Also in the body: "It was the first bread in Europe to receive a protected designation, in
2003."
**Verdict: CORRECTED — and the correction is one word wide.**
- **Altamura's registration — CONFIRMED in every particular.** Commission Regulation (EC) **No 1291/2003
  of 18 July 2003**, published **OJ L 181 of 19 July 2003, pp. 12–19**, class **2.4, "Prodotti della
  panetteria, della pasticceria, della confetteria, della biscotteria"**.
  https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32003R1291 (primary)
- **But a bread was protected in Europe six years earlier.** ***Pane casareccio di Genzano*** was
  registered as a **PGI/IGP** — EU registration **25 November 1997**, class **"2.3 Bread, pastry, cakes,
  confectionery, biscuits and other baker's wares"**, Italy.
  https://www.gov.uk/protected-food-drink-names/pane-casareccio-di-genzano (the UK mirror of the EU
  register) · registration date given as **24/11/1997** by https://www.qualigeo.eu/prodotto-qualigeo/pane-casareccio-di-genzano-igp/
  The instrument is **Commission Regulation (EC) No 2325/97 of 24 November 1997**, *"supplementing the
  Annex to Regulation (EC) No 1107/96 on the registration of geographical indications and designations of
  origin"*, **OJ L 322, 25/11/1997, pp. 33–35**.
  https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:31997R2325 (the annex itself renders as
  `>TABLE>` placeholders, so the Genzano line could not be read there; the two register entries above
  carry the date).
- **The defensible sentence:** *the first bread in Europe to get a protected designation of **origin** —
  a PDO, the stricter of the two marks. A Roman bread, Genzano's, had taken the weaker geographical
  indication in 1997.* Italian Wikipedia and the consortium's own history page, to their credit, **make
  no "first in Europe" claim at all**; it is the English-language sources that do.
  https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pane_di_Altamura · https://www.panealtamuradop.it/pane-dop/storia/ [promotional]
⚠️ **Not exhaustively checked:** that no *other* bread PDO predates 2003. Genzano is the one that breaks
the claim as usually worded; the narrower claim was not tested against the whole register (search budget
gone). See H1.

### F2. Horace — the passage is real, the bread is real, and Altamura is not in it
**Claim (old text):** "The town claims Horace, passing through in 37 BC, called it the best bread in the
world."
**Verdict: CONFIRMED as a claim the town makes, CORRECTED as history — and the old text's "the town
claims" hedge is doing exactly the right work. Keep the hedge; sharpen the facts.**

**What Horace actually wrote.** *Satires* I.5, the journey to Brundisium, lines 86–91:

> *mansuri oppidulo, quod versu dicere non est,*
> *signis perfacile est: venit vilissima rerum*
> *hic aqua; sed panis longe pulcherrimus, ultra*
> *callidus ut soleat umeris portare viator:*
> *nam Canusi lapidosus, aquae non ditior urna*
> *qui locus a forti Diomede est conditus olim.*

https://www.thelatinlibrary.com/horace/serm1.shtml · https://dco.dickinson.edu/horatius-carmina/sermonum-1-5
A published translation: *"to spend the night in a little town I can't fit in the verse, though here's a
clue: they sell what's commonly free there, water: but the bread's the best by far, so wise travellers
carry a load on their shoulders for later, 'cos it's gritty at Canusium."*
https://www.poetryintranslation.com/PITBR/Latin/HoraceSatiresBkISatV.php

**Three things follow, and all three cut against the town's version.**
1. **The town is not named — that is the joke.** *"oppidulo, quod versu dicere non est"* — a little town
   whose name will not go into a hexameter. Horace is making a metrical joke, not withholding a secret.
2. **It is not Altamura, on anybody's reading.** The Dickinson commentary identifies it as **Venusia**,
   Horace's own birthplace, *"whose name contains four short syllables incompatible with dactylic
   hexameter verse"* — and Venusia is modern **Venosa, province of Potenza, in BASILICATA**, a different
   region. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venosa **The identification is not settled** (see H2), but no
   candidate in circulation is Altamura, and Altamura sits well off the road: the route runs Beneventum →
   Trivicum → the little town → **Canusium** (modern **Canosa di Puglia**, province of Barletta-Andria-
   Trani) → Rubi → Barium → Gnatia → Brundisium. https://topostext.org/work/680 ·
   https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canosa_di_Puglia
3. **"Best bread in the world" is not in the Latin.** *panis longe pulchermus* is "by far the finest
   bread"; the superlative is local and comparative, and its foil is Canusium's *lapidosus* — gritty,
   stony — bread three lines later.

**What the town says, and where it says it.** The consortium: in the spring of **37 BC** Horace noted
*"pane migliore del mondo, tanto che il viaggiatore diligente se ne porta una provvista per il prosieguo
del viaggio"*. https://www.panealtamuradop.it/pane-dop/storia/ [promotional] Italian Wikipedia repeats it
with a conspicuous hedge of its own: *"Il primo riferimento al luogo di origine del prodotto, **se non
proprio riconducibile ad Altamura ma sicuramente al territorio murgiano**…"*
https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pane_di_Altamura ⚠️ Even that retreat is generous: Venosa and Canosa are
not on the Murgia either.

**And the claim is inside the European registration.** This is the detail worth the paragraph. Regulation
1291/2003's own "Link" section, section 4.6, prints it:
> *"Although it concerns the Murgia region rather than Altamura, the first reference to the product's
> place of origin is found in Book I, V of Horace's Satires. Visiting the countryside where he had spent
> his youth in the spring of 37 BC, the Roman poet spoke of the bread, 'far the best bread to be had, so
> good that the wise traveller takes a supply of it for his onward journey'."*
**The Commission registered a bread on a history that concedes in its own first clause that it is about
somewhere else.** Compare Campania F10, where the pizza specification dates the marinara to 1734 without
saying how it knows: the same habit, in the same series of documents.

### F3. The specification — the old text's list is right in outline and thin in every particular
**Claim (old text):** "durum-wheat semolina from the Murge, sourdough, a wood-fired oven, and a loaf that
keeps for two weeks"; and in the body, "made only from durum semolina, a sourdough starter kept alive
from the previous batch, water and salt, and baked in a wood oven".
**Verdict: CORRECTED on the oven and the keeping (F4, F5); CONFIRMED and much enriched on the rest.**
All quotations are from the annex to Reg. (EC) No 1291/2003.
https://eur-lex.europa.eu/legal-content/EN/TXT/HTML/?uri=CELEX:32003R1291

**The flour.** *"flour of durum-wheat semolina"*, remilled, from the varieties **"appulo", "arcangelo",
"duilio" and "simeto"**, which must be **"at least 80 % of the total"**. The remaining 20% may be other
durum wheat from the area. **Four named cultivars and a percentage is a far better sentence than "durum
semolina".**

**The batch, per 100 kg of flour** — and these are the numbers to use:
> *"20 kg (20 %) leaven, 2 kg (2 %) sea salt and approximately 60 litres (60 %) water at a temperature of
> 18 °C"*
**The leaven** must be built by *"adding ingredients at least three times to increase the fermenting
dough, i.e. water and durum-wheat meal (20 % of the durum-wheat flour used)"* — refreshed three times, not
merely "kept alive from the previous batch".

**The sequence, in the regulation's own order:**
1. mixing **20 minutes**, *"using a kneading machine with mechanical arms"*;
2. first rising **at least 90 minutes**, the dough *"covered with a cotton cloth"*;
3. weighed and shaped by hand, then **30 minutes**;
4. shaped again, then **at least 15 minutes**;
5. *"each loaf (pagnotta) is turned over and pushed gently by hand into the oven"*;
6. oven at **250 °C**; *"the first phase of baking takes place with the oven door open. After 15 minutes,
   the door is closed"*;
7. **a further 45 minutes**;
8. then *"the oven door is left open for at least five minutes to allow the steam to escape, so the bread
   can dry and become crusty"*.
**Turned over on the way in, and the door left open at both ends of the bake** — the two most physical
details in the document, and neither is in the old text.

**The finished loaf.** *"characteristic aroma and weighing not less than 0,5 kg"*; *"crust must not be
less than 3 mm thick"*; crumb straw-yellow with *"air-bubbles of uniform size"*; *"moisture content must
not exceed 33 %"*.
**Minimum weight only — the annex sets no maximum**, so the old text's "a kilo or two and sometimes more"
is not contradicted by the spec, but it is not authorised by it either. The number that is in the
document is **half a kilo, and 3 mm of crust**.

**The two shapes, with their dialect names** — the old text mentions the fold but not the names:
- ***"U sckuanète"*** — the tall one, *"a tall loaf formed by folding the dough from either side into the
  centre"*;
- ***"a cappidde de prèvete"*** — the priest's hat, the lower one.
In both, *"the edges of the loaves do not touch each other"* in the oven.
**Inspection body at registration: ARSSA**, the Agenzia Regionale per lo Sviluppo e l'Assestamento
Agricolo.

### F4. "Baked in a wood oven" — the specification does not require one
**Verdict: CORRECTED. This is the clearest factual error in the reading, and it is in the Key facts box
as well as the body.**
The annex, verbatim:
> *"The oven should **preferably** be wood- or gas-fired and heated indirectly and it must reach a
> temperature of 250 °C. Ovens heated directly and used for baking 'Pane di Altamura' must be fired with
> **oakwood**."*
So: **gas is permitted outright**; wood is a preference, not a rule; and the only place wood is
*mandatory* is the narrow case of a **directly heated** oven, where it must specifically be **oak**.
**"Baked in a wood oven" is wrong twice over** — as a requirement, and as a description of what most DOP
production actually uses. The photograph captioned "Loaves by the wood-fired oven in Altamura. Only durum
semolina, sourdough, water and salt are allowed" carries the same error in its second clause too: sea
salt is specified, and the flour rule is 80% of four named varieties.
**The salvageable detail is better than the error: a direct-fired oven must burn oak.**

### F5. "Keeps for a fortnight" — the fortnight is in the document, but it is not a shelf life
**Claim (old text):** "a loaf that keeps for two weeks"; "a yellow, dense, sweet crumb that stays fresh
for a fortnight, which is why shepherds and farm workers who spent a week away from home carried it."
**Verdict: CORRECTED, and the correction is subtle enough to be worth getting exactly right.**
**The specification sets no shelf life at all.** What its Link section says is:
> *"The main characteristic of the bread, which still applies today, was its long-lasting quality, a
> necessary criterion for food to sustain peasants and shepherds during **the week or, more often, the
> fortnight** they spent in isolated farms"*
**The fortnight is the length of the absence, not a guaranteed freshness window** — the old text has
inverted the two halves of its own sentence, giving the fortnight to the bread and the week to the men.
The *Enciclopedia* line in English Wikipedia gives the bread *"a long shelf life of a week"*, citing
Sinha (2007), pp. 367–368. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pane_di_Altamura
**Write it the way the document does: it was made to last as long as a shepherd stayed out, a week and
often two.** Do not print a shelf-life figure as a fact about the loaf.

### F6. The communal ovens and the family stamp — CONFIRMED, and there is an archival citation
**Claim (old text):** "in the old days each family brought its loaves to the communal oven with the
family's initial stamped on the crust to tell them apart."
**Verdict: CONFIRMED, and considerably stronger than the old text makes it: home baking was not merely
customary but *prohibited*.**
The regulation's Link section:
> *"The traditional status of breadmaking in Altamura is confirmed in the city's municipal statutes of
> **1527** (Statuti Municipali della città fatti nell'anno 1527)"*
> *"The custom of baking in public ovens derived from the **prohibition** on citizens 'of any station or
> condition' (di ogni stato o condizione) baking any type of bread or other baker's wares in their own
> homes, under penalty of a fine"*
> *"The baker marked the initials of the head of the family on the loaves with a **wooden or iron stamp**
> before putting them in the oven."*
**The fine is quantified in the Italian sources**, and this is the detail to use: *"pena il pagamento di
un'ammenda rilevante, pari ad **un terzo del costo complessivo della panificazione**"* — a third of the
whole cost of the baking. https://www.panealtamuradop.it/pane-dop/storia/ [promotional] ·
https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pane_di_Altamura
**There is a real archival reference behind it**, which is rare in this dossier: the consortium states
that the articles of the 1527 statutes concerning the oven tax were transcribed by **G. De Gemmis** in the
*Bollettino dell'Archivio-Biblioteca-Museo Civico*, **1954, pp. 5–49**. ⚠️ The transcription itself was
not consulted (see H3), so attribute it — "the town's statutes of 1527, transcribed in 1954" — rather
than quoting the statutes directly.
**Note the mechanism, because it is the point:** the stamp exists *because* the oven was shared and the
dough was not. Women mixed and raised the dough at home; the loaf went out of the house to be baked and
had to come back to the right family. It is a property mark, exactly like the Herculaneum loaf stamped
*Celeris Q. Grani Veri ser.* in the Campania dossier — worth noticing, not worth reusing.
⚠️ **Dates in circulation that are NOT in any cited document:** a **1420** exemption of the clergy from
the bread tax, the **Antico Forno Santa Caterina** trading since **1391**, and a notarial deed of **1300**
behind the Antico Forno Forte. These come from bakery and local-history pages with no archival reference
and, in two cases, from the businesses themselves. https://www.fornosantacaterina.it/en/blogs/news/il-pane-di-altamura-dop
[producer] · https://www.museodelpaneforte.it/storia/storia-del-forno/ [producer] **Do not print them.**

### F7. Where the bread may be made — five comuni, not one town
**Verdict: CORRECTED, quietly but definitely.**
The annex:
> *"The defined geographical area of production of the grain and the ground semolina used comprises the
> municipalities of **Altamura, Gravina di Puglia, Poggiorsini, Spinazzola and Minervino Murge** in the
> province of Bari"*, and *"The area of production of 'Pane di Altamura' coincides with the territory
> defined above"*.
So the grain **and the baking** are both spread across **five comuni**, not confined to the town whose
name the bread carries. (Two of the five, Spinazzola and Minervino Murge, now sit in the province of
Barletta-Andria-Trani, created in 2004 — the 2003 text predates the split and still says "province of
Bari".)
**Useful for the reading only if the sentence is doing work:** the name is a town's, the appellation is a
plateau's.

### F8. The 2025 drought amendment — the best live detail in this reading
**Verdict: CONFIRMED, and it is new enough that nothing in the old text could have known it.**
- **Ministerial decree of 7 February 2025**, published in the **Gazzetta Ufficiale of 18 February 2025**
  (atto **25A00992**), a **temporary** modification of the disciplinare, in force **until 31 July 2025**.
  https://www.gazzettaufficiale.it/atto/serie_generale/caricaDettaglioAtto/originario?atto.dataPubblicazioneGazzetta=2025-02-18&atto.codiceRedazionale=25A00992
  (primary) · https://www.disciplinare.it/pane-di-altamura-dop-modifica-disciplinare-di-produzione-2025.html
- **What it changed:** Article 4's first paragraph became *"La materia prima impiegata consiste in un
  rimacinato di semola di grano duro ricavato dalla macinazione di grani duri prodotte nel territorio
  delimitato nell'allegato K."* — **the four named varieties and the 80% floor were deleted**. Any durum
  wheat grown in the delimited territory would do.
- **Why:** the **2024 drought**. The stated grounds are that *"la siccità ne abbia dimezzato la
  produzione"* — drought had halved the harvest — and that there was not enough qualifying grain to
  certify the usual volume.
- **The row.** The amendment was requested by the Consorzio di Tutela, whose president **Lucia Forte**
  runs **Oropan**, an industrial bakery which the report sizes at **47 million euro**; critics argued a
  handmade product's *availability* should not be allowed to rewrite its standard, and raised a
  conflict-of-interest objection about an industrial producer presiding over the consortium.
  **DOP production in 2023: 561 tonnes.**
  https://winenews.it/it/pane-di-altamura-dop-o-non-dop-con-la-modifica-del-disciplinare-questo-e-il-dilemma_560320/
  [trade press] — ⚠️ the conflict-of-interest characterisation is that outlet's, and the consortium's
  reply was not found. Stage it as a reported dispute, name the drought as the agreed fact.
- **Why it belongs in the reading:** it is the DOP system caught doing the thing the reading is about.
  A bread defined by four cultivars grown on one plateau met a year in which the plateau could not grow
  them, and the definition moved. **561 tonnes** is also the only production figure anywhere in this
  dossier, and it is small.

### F9. Altamura, the town — the figures, and the two things it is otherwise famous for
**Verdict: CONFIRMED.** Population **70,419 (31 May 2026)**, area **431.38 km²**, altitude **467 m**.
https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Altamura
Two finds, neither of them food, both offered here only so the writer knows they exist and can decline
them: the **Uomo di Altamura**, a *Homo neanderthalensis* skeleton found by CARS speleologists in the
**Lamalunga** cave in **October 1993**; and about **twenty thousand dinosaur footprints over 12,000 m²**
in the abandoned **Cava Pontrelli** quarry, found in **1999**. ⚠️ The course has already spent
"archaeological feature described in the present tense" elsewhere and the brief rules it out as an
opening. Neither reaches a table. **Leave both out.**

### F10. The McDonald's that closed — a real, dated, well-reported scene, and the best opening candidate
**Verdict: CONFIRMED in outline; one figure needs care.**
- A **McDonald's opened in Altamura in 2001**. *"Pochi mesi dopo, il panettiere **Luca Di Gesù**, con il
  fratello **Giuseppe**, apre poco lontano una piccola focacceria"* — a few months later the baker Luca Di
  Gesù and his brother Giuseppe opened a small focaccia shop nearby. **After about a year the McDonald's
  closed** for want of custom, the focacceria being part of the reason.
- **It was reported internationally in 2002** — *Panorama* in Italy, *Libération* in France, the *New York
  Times* in the United States — and the *New York Times* returned to it with **"The Bread Is Famously
  Good, but It Killed McDonald's", 12 January 2006**.
- The story became **Nico Cirasola's film *Focaccia Blues* (2009)**, described as *"a documentary about a
  successful focaccia shop in Altamura which forced the local McDonald's shop to close due to lack of
  customers"*.
  https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Focaccia_blues · https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nico_Cirasola
⚠️ **The NYT article itself could not be fetched** (the host refuses automated requests), so the headline
and date are as cited by the Italian Wikipedia article, which lists it among its references alongside
*La Gazzetta del Mezzogiorno* and a piece by Giuseppe Salvaggiulo. **Attribute the closure as reported,
and do not claim the focaccia alone closed it** — the sources say the shop was a cause, not the cause,
and *Focaccia Blues* is a film with a thesis, not an audit.
**Why it is the opening:** a named man, a date, a shopfront, a crowd voting with its lunch money, and a
prejudice overturned inside the sentence — the small town that is supposed to lose. And it lands on the
reading's actual subject, which is that this is a place where bread is the local industry, not the local
colour. See section G1.

## B. Reading 3 — the frame, the orecchiette, and the street

### F11. *Cucina povera* — the frame the reading opens on is the one the field has stopped accepting
**Claim (old text):** "The cooking of Puglia is *cucina povera* in the strict sense: what a landless
peasant could grow, gather or afford."
**Verdict: DISPUTED. Defensible as description of a repertoire; not defensible as history, and the
phrase "in the strict sense" is the part that cannot stand.**
- **Massimo Montanari** argues the poor/rich split is an *ideological* construction rather than a
  separation of practice: elite recipe books from the *Liber de coquina* (early 14th c., Angevin Naples)
  onward are full of peasant foods. Verbatim: *«La rigidità degli schemi simbolici, che oppongono
  drasticamente lo stile di vita dei contadini a quello dei signori … convive tranquillamente con la
  presenza di prodotti e sapori "contadini" nella cucina di élite»*, and *«i modelli di opposizione e di
  esclusione rappresentano per così dire l'utopia delle classi dominanti, tanto più organica e coerente
  quanto più disattesa nella pratica.»*
  Montanari, *Cucina povera, cucina ricca*, in *Quaderni medievali* LII (dicembre 2001), pp. 95–105:
  http://www.rmoa.unina.it/921/1/RM-Montanari-Cucina.pdf (academic, peer-reviewed, digitised by Reti
  Medievali) — **the single best citation in this dossier for the frame.**
- **Carol Helstosky**, *Garlic and Oil: Food and Politics in Italy* (Berg, 2004), dates the *valorisation*
  of scarcity to the interwar period: Fascism *«contributed to transforming the necessity of austerity
  into "attributes of italianità"»* and to a *«pride in regional cooking»* which *«became the foundation
  of Italian cooking in the 1920s and 1930s»* (p. 125). ⚠️ Quoted here from Magaudda's academic review,
  not from the book: https://www.paomag.net/university/pdfuni/magaudda_garlicoil_review.pdf
- **Alberto Grandi** (Parma), the loudest version: *«The truth is that we were starving. We ate little and
  badly. Then we started eating a lot and badly. Finally we told ourselves we had always eaten well.»*
  And: *«From 1876 to 1915, 20 million Italians went abroad seeking food. One Venetian farmer in three
  suffered from pellagra.»* https://it.wikiquote.org/wiki/Alberto_Grandi (quotation database with
  per-quote sourcing; the underlying *Corriere* interview of 2 April 2022 was not fetched). Grandi is a
  provocateur and should be named as one, not used as consensus.
**How to write it:** the repertoire is real and the poverty was real (F12); what is not real is the tidy
opposition. The honest sentence says what the kitchen *uses* and lets the reader infer, rather than
announcing a category. **⚠️ "Cucina povera" as a term could not be dated** — no dictionary, no academic
source; the only dating found was an unsourced blog giving "the 1960s". **Do not date the phrase.** (H7)

### F12. "A landless peasant" — CONFIRMED, and the documentation is far better than the old text's
**Verdict: CONFIRMED in substance, with exact dates. This is where the reading's frame can be anchored
in fact instead of in category.**
- The southern *latifondo* was worked by day labourers living in towns, not by families on the land:
  *«Il latifondo, organizzato in massima parte attraverso masserie, prevedeva manodopera bracciantile,
  che risiedeva nei paesi arroccati sulle colline o in montagna.»*
- The reform: *decreti Gullo* (six decrees, **1944–1945**); **legge "Sila" n. 230 of 12 May 1950**;
  **legge "stralcio" n. 841 of 21 October 1950**. Puglia's reform body was created by **DPR n. 67 of
  7 February 1951**. The Puglia–Lucania–Molise district was the largest in Italy: **c. 1,502,000 ha**,
  of which **just over 1,453,000 ha** agro-forestal, across **129 comuni** in 8 provinces. *Poderi* up to
  **15 ha**; *quote* usually **2–4 ha**.
  ALSIA (Regione Basilicata public agency), *Riforma fondiaria: per saperne di più*:
  https://www.alsia.it/opencms/export/sites/alsia/.allegati/temi/Riforma-fondiaria_per-saperne-di-piu_finale.pdf
- **The Arneo occupation, and it is a scene.** Mobilisations in **November–December 1949 and January
  1950** drew "up to 10 or 15,000" smallholders and labourers; the occupation proper began **28 December
  1950**, c. 1,200 labourers growing to c. 2,000, **walking 15–20 km** from towns such as Copertino and
  Carmiano. And the reason: *«La legge stralcio prevedeva interventi di esproprio … per quanto riguarda
  la Puglia, nelle province di Bari e Foggia, **ma non in quella di Lecce**»* — **the Salento had been
  left out of the land reform, and the walk was to force it in.**
  https://www.fondazioneterradotranto.it/2020/05/17/a-settantanni-dalle-lotte-dellarneo-una-riflessione-sulla-riforma-agraria-nel-salento-1950-1960-terza-parte/
  [local history foundation, citing archival and parliamentary material]
⚠️ **Do not print** the circulating totals (750,000 ha expropriated nationally, 196,937 ha in Puglia and
Lucania, 121,000 families settled) — they appeared only in search summaries and were never fetched (H8).

### F16. The making — the thumb is the wrong gesture for the street the reading names
**Claim (old text):** "each is dragged across the board with a knife so that it curls, and then turned
inside out over the thumb to make a rough-surfaced cup". Repeated in `content/recipes/puglia.js` (the
orecchiette headnote and steps 4–5).
**Verdict: CORRECTED — and the error is specifically a Bari error.**
The drag-then-invert sequence is the general and Valle d'Itria technique. **In Bari the dome comes from
the drag alone**, with two index fingers, *without* the thumb inversion — the pasta is stretched as it is
*«pulled with the knife»*, creating *«a natural sort of dome, without turning it on the finger»*.
Elsewhere there is *«an additional step: turning the pasta on the finger to obtain that hollow»*.
La Cucina Italiana, *The Fresh Pasta in Puglia: Orecchiette, Stràscenàte & Co.*:
https://www.lacucinaitaliana.com/italian-food/italian-dishes/fresh-pasta-in-puglia-orecchiette-strascenate-and-co
[food magazine] · corroborated by https://pugliosita.it/2022/08/04/orecchiette-e-strascenate-due-nomi-per-un-unico-formato-di-pasta/
[regional culture blog]
**The knife has a name: the *sfèrre***, a small round- or smooth-tipped iron knife, *«a knife that our
grandmothers used to use, made of iron»*. The verb *strascinare*, to drag, gives the dialect name
*strascenàte*. **The tool's name is worth more to the reading than the thumb is.**

### F17. "The same shape is called *strascinati* in the hills" — backwards
**Verdict: DISPUTED, and the old text states only one half of a genuine split.**
- **In the hills (Murgia, Basilicata), strascinati are a different format** — larger, flatter, markedly
  less concave, dragged with **three fingers** (four- and eight-finger variants in Basilicata), and
  *«non prevedono l'utilizzo del pollice»*. Italian Wikipedia: *«Ne esiste anche una versione realizzata
  senza la forma di cupola, meglio conosciuta come strascinati.»*
- **In Bari the two words coincide**, because *strascinat'* names the *method*, not the outcome — and in
  Bari, per F16, there is no separate inverted shape to distinguish. The source is explicit: *«in Bari,
  orecchiette and strascinati coincide because you don't make the low ones.»*
So the old sentence has the geography inverted: the words merge **in the city**, and separate **in the
hills**. https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strascinati · La Cucina Italiana as above.

### F18. The dialect names — all real, and size- and town-specific

### F22. Cime di rapa — not the turnip you eat the root of, and there is no IGP
**Verdict: CORRECTED on both counts.**
- Botanically ***Brassica rapa* L. subsp. *sylvestris* L. Janch. var. *esculenta* Hort.** — the same
  species as the turnip, a different subspecies and variety, **bred and grown for the tops, not the
  root**. The edible part is *«l'infiorescenza, i cui fiori non sono ancora aperti, da una piccola
  porzione di stelo e dalle foglie più giovani»*; each plant gives a main head and then secondary ones
  picked in succession, September to May.
- **There is no IGP and no DOP.** A register search returns *«Nessun risultato»*
  (https://www.qualigeo.eu/?s=cima+di+rapa). What exists is **PAT status, entered in the national
  register of Prodotti Agroalimentari Tradizionali in 2006** — a regional list, not an EU mark.
- Puglia grows **c. 3,500 ha, about one third of Italian production.**
https://www.patpuglia.it/it/12/Cima_di_rapa/5_100 (Regione Puglia, institutional)
**The old text's "flowering tops of the turnip" is loose but not absurd; "a brassica grown for its tops"
is the accurate phrase.**

### F23. The dressing — anchovy is traditional, cheese is forbidden, breadcrumbs are not Barese

The PAT Puglia sheet's method: greens boiled in salted water, the pasta added part-way through the
greens' cooking, **anchovies melted separately in warm oil** and tossed through, and **no cheese** —
citing Giovanni Panza: *«condisci i cavatelli e rape senza aggiungere formaggio»*.
https://www.patpuglia.it/it/18/Ce_se_mange_i%C3%B2sce_Le_orecchiette_con_le_cime_di_rapa/58 (Regione
Puglia, institutional, citing a named local authority) — **this is a genuinely good source and confirms
the one-pot method the recipe file already teaches.**
⚠️ **One recipe correction.** `content/recipes/puglia.js` ends the dish with *"toasted breadcrumbs are
the only thing that goes on top"* and gives a *mollica* variation. Breadcrumbs are real but are **not the
Barese dressing** — they belong *«in altre zone, soprattutto in Alta Murgia»*, and the PAT sheet records
Panza as finding the fried-breadcrumb option one that *«non piace eccessivamente»*. The absolute part is
the cheese prohibition, not the crumbs. **Soften the "only thing" claim; keep the variation.**

### F24. *Braciole* and horse meat — the old text offers a false alternative
**Claim (old text):** "The second classic is ragù of horse meat **or** of *braciole*, rolled slices of
meat, cooked on Sunday."
**Verdict: CORRECTED. The *brasciole* **are** the horse meat — it is not an either/or.**
*Brasciole* (Barese spelling) are rolls of **10–15 cm** made *«con fettine di cavallo (o di vitello,
contravvenendo alla ricetta tradizionale**) ripiene di lardo e pecorino»* — **veal is explicitly named as
the deviation.** Browned with oil and onion, splashed with red wine, then **at least four hours** in
tomato. *«Le orecchiette al ragù di brasciole rappresentano il piatto della domenica per eccellenza in
molte famiglie pugliesi.»* The meat is then served as the second course — **the same one-pot-two-courses
logic as the Neapolitan ragù**, which the Campania dossier already has, so do not present it as novel.
https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brasciole [encyclopaedic, thinly sourced — no primary or academic source
for *ragù barese* was found]
**The word itself is worth a line:** in most of Italy *braciola* is a **chop**; in Puglia, Basilicata,
Sicily and among Italian-Americans it is the **stuffed roll**.

**Horse meat — CONFIRMED as real, CORRECTED as "widespread".** It is a minority food, and declining.
- Of Italians who eat meat, **17% eat horse meat** at least monthly — **c. 7 million people** — and the
  trend is *«in leggero calo»*. By region, Lombardia **24%**, **Puglia 11%**, Emilia-Romagna 9%.
- Production: at 31/12/2024, **11,394 horse farms and 46,616 horses** raised for slaughter; **Puglia
  holds 9.85% of farms and 14.03% of head.**
- Italy is the world's largest importer of horse meat: **25,191 tonnes in 2022**.
- ⚠️ **Data caveat to respect:** ISTAT gave 28,181 equids slaughtered in 2017 where the health ministry's
  BDN recorded 46,053 — a near-2× divergence. **Attribute any single national figure to its source.**

https://animalequality.it/app/uploads/2025/05/Report-ipsos-cavalli-2025.pdf [advocacy NGO commissioning a

### F26. What the disciplinare actually protects — cow's milk, and cream from anywhere
**Claim (old text):** "a pouch of mozzarella filled with shredded mozzarella and cream".
**Verdict: CORRECTED on the terminology, and on one thing readers will care about.**
The consolidated specification (decree **14 September 2023**, GU n. 224 of 25.9.2023), Art. 2:
> *"La 'Burrata di Andria' I.G.P. è un formaggio prodotto con **latte vaccino**, ottenuto dall'unione di
> panna e formaggio a pasta filata. L'involucro è costituito **esclusivamente da pasta filata** che
> racchiude, al suo interno, una miscela di panna e pasta filata sfilacciata."*
- **The spec never says "mozzarella"** — it says *pasta filata*. Small, but the reading is about precision.
- **Cow's milk only; buffalo is not permitted.** Raw or pasteurised at 72 °C for 15 seconds.
- **The cream need not be local, or even fresh.** Art. 2.1 expressly allows *"panna fresca pastorizzata
  e/o UHT confezionata e/o loro miscele"*. **Bought-in UHT cream is legal inside the IGP** — the single
  most deflating fact in the cheese paragraph, and worth one clause.
- **Weight 100 g to 1000 g**; skin *"≥ 2 mm circa"*; humidity **50–70%** in the 2023 text (the 2016
  document unico said **60–70%** — the band was widened downward, so sources differ legitimately).
- *Stracciatella* is defined by hand: *"lo sfilaccio effettuato manualmente"*, and the name comes from
  the tearing — *"la pasta filata viene infatti stracciata a mano a formare dei 'lucini' irregolari."*
https://www.masaf.gov.it/flex/files/1/5/4/D.15914ffcb289f1ed2409/Burrata_di_Andria_09.23.pdf (primary,
ministry-published specification)

### F27. "Invented on a farm near Andria" — the IGP may be made anywhere in Puglia

rappresentata **dall'intero territorio della Regione Puglia**."* Lecce, Foggia, Brindisi — all inside the
name. The only spatial rule is that it must be **packed at the plant where it is made**, *"in quanto si
tratta di un prodotto fresco che tende facilmente a deperire"*.
**So "the one from Andria is still the reference" is a statement about reputation, not about the mark.**

### F29. "To use up the scraps" — CONFIRMED by the primary source, and the lineage has a name

> *"…seguendo il concetto di produzione delle **mantèche (involucri di pasta filata stagionata in cui è

done with cream inside a fresh one. **Manteca is itself on Puglia's PAT cheese list**, so the ancestor is

### F32. The asphodel leaves — the specification does not require them, and arguably forbids the real thing

### F35. Caciocavallo Silano DOP — Puglia is genuinely in it, across four provinces

### F36. Canestrato Pugliese DOP — rush, not reed; no breed in the rules; and it is genuinely dying

### F39. *Polpo arricciato* is not "raw octopus" — it is two hours of violence, and it has a 1513 document
**Verdict: CORRECTED, and this is the best single item in the seafood paragraph.**
**PAT-listed 2022** as *"Polpo crudo arricciato"*, dialect *pulpe rizze*, province of Bari. The method:
ink sac and tentacle membrane removed; beaten against stone with a wooden paddle; smashed repeatedly on
the rock; then *slid* across the rock for about fifteen minutes; then oscillated in a flat basket until
*"i cirri o tentacoli, dapprima flaccidi e snervati, si contorcono e assumono la forma di buccoli."*
**About two hours in all.** The PAT sheet calls it ***"cottura meccanica"* / *"cottura a freddo"*** —
mechanical, cold cooking. **Raw in the sense of unheated; not raw in the sense of untreated.**
And it carries the documentation burrata lacks: Athenaeus's *"Plecté poliùpodos piléses"*, beaten octopus
tentacles, and **Bari municipal decrees of 24 October 1513 listing *"pulpo rizzuto"***.
https://www.patpuglia.it/it/12/Polpo_crudo_arricciato/8_341 (primary, official regional register)

### F40. The food-safety rule — real, and it bites on much less of the Bari plate than you would think
**Verdict: CORRECTED — and the precision here matters, because the obvious version of this claim is wrong.**
**Reg. (EC) No 853/2004, Annex III, Section VIII, Chapter III, part D**, as replaced by **Reg. (EU) No
1276/2011 of 8 December 2011**, requires that fishery products *"intended to be consumed raw"* undergo
**freezing at −20 °C for not less than 24 hours, or −35 °C for not less than 15 hours**, to kill
parasites.
**But the obligation runs only to products "derived from finfish or cephalopod molluscs"**, and Annex I
point 3.1 defines fishery products so as to exclude *"**live bivalve molluscs, live echinoderms, live
tunicates and live marine gastropods**"*. Therefore:
- **Octopus and cuttlefish are covered.** *Polpo arricciato*, *allievi* and raw *seppia* fall squarely
  inside the freezing rule.
- **Sea urchins, mussels, cozze pelose, oysters, noci and taratufi are not** — they are governed instead
  by Section VII (classified production areas, purification, dispatch-centre documents).
**So "sea urchins with lemon and nothing else" is legally unremarkable; raw cephalopods off an
unregistered stall are the actual problem.**
Italian implementation: Intesa Stato-Regioni of **16 November 2006** and Ministero della Salute nota
prot. **0004379-P of 17 February 2011**, extending it to restaurants and retail alike as *"trattamento di
bonifica preventiva"*.
https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2011/1276/annex (primary, verbatim replacement text) ·
https://www.legislation.gov.uk/eur/2004/853/annex/I (primary, the definition that does the work) ·
https://asugi.sanita.fvg.it/export/sites/aas1/it/documenti/all_dip/mat_info/dip_ian_sommin_vendita_pesce_crudo.pdf
(Italian health authority)

### F42. The sea-urchin ban — and it is the cleanest "everything you knew" item in reading 3
**Verdict: CONFIRMED. The course must not describe urchins as currently fished in Puglia.**
**Legge Regionale 18 aprile 2023, n. 6**, *"Misure di salvaguardia per la tutela del riccio di mare"*,
BURP n. 38 suppl. of 20 April 2023. Art. 2, comma 1, verbatim:
> *"Nel mare territoriale della Puglia … è vietato il **prelievo, la raccolta, la detenzione, il

> lividus**) … **per un periodo di tre anni**."*

  the stock reconstitution process is not yet complete."*
  https://www.borderline24.com/2026/06/27/niente-ricci-di-mare-a-tavola-fino-al-2029-la-puglia-approva-la-proroga-del-divieto-di-pesca/

### F43. Fave e cicoria — CONFIRMED, and two defining details are missing

### F44. The Greek pedigree of *'ncapriata* — repeated everywhere, documented nowhere

### F45. The agronomy — the one hard mechanism available for the beans
**Verdict: CONFIRMED, with a real number.** In a long-term southern-Italian experiment, **durum wheat
following faba bean yielded on average +12% more, and up to +135% in dry years**, and the mechanism is
partly water rather than nitrogen alone: faba bean used **197 mm** against wheat's **247 mm**, leaving on
average **+84 mm/m** more soil water at wheat sowing, and *"allowed reduction in the chemical application
of nitrogen"*. Garofalo, Di Paolo & Rinaldi, *Crop & Pasture Science*, **2009**:
https://connectsci.au/cp/article-lookup/doi/10.1071/cp08208 (peer-reviewed)
**Why it matters to the reading: the bean is not on the plate beside the wheat by accident — it is what
let the wheat grow.** ⚠️ The "rotation crop of the Tavoliere" framing specifically is unverified (H15),
and the nearest measured fixation study is in Basilicata, not Puglia.

### F48. Focaccia barese — the potato is real, contested, and does the opposite of what the old text says
**Verdict: DISPUTED on "thick", CONFIRMED-with-qualification on the potato.**
The potato **is** in the PAT ingredient list (*"farine, il sale, lo zucchero e la patata"*, listed
**2012**) and in the consortium's proposed specification. **But the quantity is small and its purpose is
not fluffiness.** The president of the Consorzio della Focaccia Barese, **Giovanni Di Serio**, a
fourth-generation baker: *"Ne viene messa **una per ogni chilo di farina**, mentre nelle 'altre' focacce
se ne mettono anche due"*, and the limited potato plus **no second proofing** is precisely what keeps a
true focaccia barese **thin rather than fluffy**. Its function is preservation and sweetness: *"serve a
conservare meglio il prodotto… e poi addolcisce il sapore: è di fatto utilizzata **al posto dello
zucchero**."*
**And Bari bakers do not agree it belongs at all** — Di Serio again: *"A Bari infatti c'è molta 'libertà'
… **chi non mette la patata nell'impasto**, chi utilizza solo la semola o la farina e chi mischia pelati
e pomodori freschi."* **So the potato is normative-by-consortium inside a live disagreement**, not
settled tradition.
https://www.barinedita.it/storie-e-interviste/n5230-niente-pelati-poca-patata-e-solo-olio-extravergine--come-si-prepara-una-vera-focaccia-barese
[local journalism, interviewing the consortium] · https://www.patpuglia.it/it/12/Focaccia_barese/6_199

**pelati are excluded**. And **"sold by the slab" is wrong**: focaccia barese is baked in a **round
teglia** and sold by the wedge; the slab is Roman *pizza in teglia*.

### F50. The *fornello pronto* — real, and the old text describes the wrong cooking method
**Verdict: CONFIRMED as an institution, CORRECTED on mechanism and geography.**
**(a) It is not a grill.** The meat is *"infilzata in appositi spiedi e fatta arrostire in verticale
all'interno di una cupola"* — **skewered and stood vertically inside a stone dome, cooked beside the
flame, not over it**, so the fat drips onto coals away from the meat; the dome's curvature and height are
a local masons' craft. The trade press is explicit: *"meat is not grilled but left to cook with indirect
heat."*
**(b) It is not two towns.** The tradition runs across the Valle d'Itria and beyond: **Martina Franca,
Cisternino, Locorotondo, Ceglie Messapica, Ostuni, Alberobello**, plus Sammichele di Bari, Laterza,
Santeramo in Colle and Crispiano.
https://www.patpuglia.it/it/18/La_bombetta._Tutti_i_segreti_del_fornello_pronto_martinese/84 (Regione
Puglia, primary) · https://www.pubblicitaitalia.com/carne/prodotti/eurocarni/2017/10/16170 [trade press]
**Chronology:** consolidation **mid-twentieth century**; **Zio Pietro** of Cisternino is claimed as the
first butcher to run one, **from 1935** ⚠️ (the establishment's own claim, no licence or register cited);

### F53. Pasticciotto — **from Galatina, and the 1745 story is contradicted by the parish archives**
**Verdict: DISPUTED, strongly. This is the best debunk in the reading and it rests on real documents.**
**(a) The place.** The pasticciotto is **from Galatina**, a comune about 25 km south of Lecce; *leccese*
is an acquisition, not an origin. Eating it in Lecce is correct; calling it Lecce's is not.
**(b) The archives.** From the parish archive of **SS. Pietro e Paolo, Galatina** and the tax rolls of the
Direzione delle Contribuzioni Dirette:
- **Vincenzo Scalone (later Ascalone) married in Galatina on 13 January 1787**, having come **from
  Galatone**. **The family was not in Galatina in 1745.**
- In **1810** Vincenzo is registered not as a pastry cook but as an ***acquavitaro***, a spirits seller.
- **Felice Ascalone's business is officially dated 1860**; **Andrea Ascalone (b. 1865)** took it over in
  **1882**, registered as *"Pasticceria Caffé e affini"*.
- **The *catasto onciario* of Galatina (1745–1754) lists no pastry shops and no pastry makers at all**,
  while Lecce's records for the same period document four.
- **The word *pasticciotto* appears in a 1707 inventory of the bishop of Nardò** — before the claimed
  invention, and somewhere else, as elite vocabulary. (This is very probably the source of the "1707
  origin" sometimes cited as a rival date: it is an attestation of the word, not of the dish.)
- The legend exists in mutually exclusive versions — the inventor is variously "Nicola Ascalone", "Andrea
  Ascalone", or "Nicola son of Andrea". The researcher's own verdict: *"Insomma, non si sa bene quale sia
  la versione giusta."*

citing named archives) · https://www.aracne-galatina.it/storia-della-pasticceria-a-galatina-1-puntata/
(local history journal, same findings) · English Wikipedia carries the caveat: *"recent studies
demonstrate that the Ascalone family was not present in Galatina before 1787"*
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pasticciotto
**(c) The shop is still there**, in Galatina's old centre *"a due passi dalla chiesa dei Santi Pietro e

### F54. *Caffè leccese* — it is almond **syrup**, and nobody invented it on the record
**Verdict: CORRECTED on the ingredient, UNVERIFIABLE on the origin.**

mandorla*: *"The latte di mandorla used in Lecce is a sweet, viscous syrup. It is completely different
from the unsweetened almond milk you pour over cereal back home."* Working ratio about **one part almond

is why the drink takes no sugar. https://doeatbetterexperience.com/blog/caffe-leccese-almond-milk-coffee/
[food-tourism copy, but precise on exactly this point]
**(b) Name no inventor.** The recurring attribution is to **Antonio Quarta**, later founder of the Lecce

barista at the only bar in Lecce with chipped ice, or derive it from Valencia's *café del tiempo* arriving
in the Salento in the seventeenth or the early nineteenth century — **the sources cannot agree on the
century.** No document, date or checkable name exists. ⚠️ **Caffè Alvino appears in these lists as one of

**(c)** *Latte di mandorla* is itself a **Puglia PAT**, so the Salento product has standing. ⚠️ Whether it
uses local almonds, and whether the tradition is a Sicilian import, could not be established (H21).

### F55. The Tavoliere and "Italy's hardest wheat"
**Verdict: CONFIRMED (the plain), UNVERIFIABLE (the superlative).**

  Treccani gives the area *without* making the ranking claim, and other sources say 4,000–4,300 km². Use
  **about 3,000 km²**; the ranking is safe as commonly stated. Treccani adds a point the reading could
  use: it *"fu trasformata solo in tempi più moderni a vasti campi di cereali"* — **the wheat plain is a

## F. Reading 4 — the trulli and Castel del Monte

### D1. The construction — CONFIRMED, and the cone is two shells, not one
**Verdict: CONFIRMED, with one addition worth having.** ICOMOS uses both words as near-synonyms: the
roofs are built of *"successive courses of grey limestone slabs **5-7 cm thick, known as chianche or
chiancarelle**"*, corbelled so each course projects inward. Strictly, *chianche* are the thicker slabs
(25–30 cm, for paving and the inner shell), *chiancarelle* the thinner roofing ones — so the old text's
usage is right.
**What it flattens: the cone is two shells.** An inner corbelled false dome, *la candela*, *"una serie di
anelli in aggetto progressivo"*, self-supporting and **built without centering**, and over it the outer
weatherproof skin of *chiancarelle*. **A dome built with no formwork at all is the better sentence.**
ICOMOS Advisory Body Evaluation No 787 (1996): https://whc.unesco.org/archive/advisory_body_evaluation/787.pdf
⚠️ **Note the echo with reading 3:** *chiancarelle* is also a dialect name for orecchiette in the Valle
d'Itria (F18). Same word, the roof slab and the pasta. **Use it once, deliberately, or not at all.**

### D2. "Without mortar" — CONFIRMED, with a caveat
**Verdict: CONFIRMED.** ICOMOS: the buildings use roughly worked limestone collected *"from neighbouring
fields"* employing exclusively *"**the drystone technique (ie without mortar)**"*. The walls are
double-skinned *a sacco*, **100 to 160 cm thick** (ICOMOS gives 1.5–1.8 m for the wall rise), stability
coming from *"l'attrito tra i conci di pietra"* — friction between the stones.
⚠️ **The cavity fill is bound with red-earth clay**, *"il bolo di terra rossa"*, named as the only binder.
So "mortarless" is right in the technical sense (no lime, no hydraulic binder) but the fill is not
strictly dry. **And the stone came out of the fields being cleared for planting — the houses are a
by-product of making the land farmable, which is the food link (D12).**

### D3. "More than a thousand" — CORRECTED, and the real figures carry the town's structure
**Verdict: CORRECTED.** ICOMOS, 1996: *"The Monti Quarter contains **1,030 trulli** across 6 hectares;
Aja Piccola contains **590**."* UNESCO's Statement of OUV gives the property as *"over 1,500 structures"*
and, under Integrity, *"**over 1,600**"* surviving original buildings; the 2024 Periodic Report repeats
1,030 / 590.
**So "more than a thousand" is the Rione Monti figure doing duty for the whole town.** Correct: **1,030 in
Rione Monti, 590 in Aia Piccola, over 1,600 in the inscribed property.** ⚠️ The widely circulated "about
400" for Aia Piccola [tourist board] conflicts with UNESCO's 590; **prefer 590.**
**Property area 11 hectares across six separate parcels. No buffer zone is defined** — and the 2024
Periodic Report lists inadequate buffer-zone protection among its conservation concerns.
https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/787 · https://whc.unesco.org/document/217273 (both primary)

### D4. **The tax-dodge story — DISPUTED, and ICOMOS itself reverses the causation**
**Claim (old text):** "owes them to a tax dodge… the counts of Conversano… were obliged to pay the crown
a levy on any new settlement they founded; they got round it by allowing their peasants to build only
houses that could be dismantled in a day". Repeated in the recap and in `quiz.js` 46–49.
**Verdict: DISPUTED. This is the most important finding in reading 4, and the nomination dossier the old
text implicitly leans on is the document that undercuts it.**

**(a) The levy is real in outline.** The ***Prammatica de Baronibus*** was a Neapolitan royal pragmatic
sanction requiring royal assent to found a new inhabited centre: *"la costruzione di un nuovo centro
abitato comportava in primo luogo il regio assenso"*, a law *"in vigore fino al XVIII secolo"*.
https://it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alberobello ⚠️ **The pragmatic itself could not be dated** — no source
reached cites an issuing date or text (H22). **Name it, do not date it.**

**(b) The sentence to quote, from ICOMOS's own evaluation:**
> *"Tradition holds that **drystone walling was imposed upon the new settlers so that their houses could
> be quickly dismantled**… However, scholars suggest this technique represented **'a minimal physical
> response to local conditions,' later exploited for fiscal purposes**."*
**That is the whole correction in one sentence: dry stone was how you built on a karst plateau littered
with field-cleared limestone, and the tax dodge was opportunistic and secondary — not the origin of the
form.** ICOMOS also dates the settlement earlier than the counts: *"recent scholarship suggests scattered
rural settlements around AD 1000 gradually coalesced into the later villages of Aja Piccola and Monti."*
**Treccani's entry on *trullo* does not mention tax evasion at all.** https://www.treccani.it/enciclopedia/trullo/

**(c) Two facts that survive, and one the old text omits.** ICOMOS does record that dismantling
*"famously occurred in **1644** to thwart Neapolitan tax inspectors"* ⚠️ (no corroborating or archival
source for the episode was found, H23). And it names a **second motive the old text leaves out**: the
technique enabled *"**dispossession of recalcitrant residents**"* — the count could level a tenant's
house at will. **That is at least as well attested as the tax story, and it is a harder, better fact: the
houses came apart for the landlord's convenience as much as against the taxman's.**

**(d) The count, with two corrections.** **Giangirolamo II Acquaviva d'Aragona** (Conversano 1600 –
Barcelona **14 May 1665**), 20th Count 1626–1665. The nickname is disputed — *"popolarmente soprannominato
il 'Guercio' a causa di un presunto difetto visivo (**non riscontrabile nelle fonti**)"*. And he was in
royal custody for much of the period: arrested 1643, imprisoned in Madrid, arrested again 1649,
incarcerated until 1665. **He was therefore in prison in 1644, the year of the famous dismantling.**
**How to write it:** the tax story is the town's tradition, carried as tradition by the nomination
dossier, with the scholarly position being that this is simply how the Murgia built.

### D5. 1797 — CONFIRMED, but the town did not free itself
**Verdict: CORRECTED in emphasis, and the detail is better than the summary.** Alberobello **petitioned
and was granted**: ICOMOS, *"In 1797, residents obtained royal town status from Ferdinand IV, ending
feudal rule by the Acquaviva family."*
The particulars: the fief ended *"**sabato 27 maggio 1797**"*, when **Ferdinand IV** received a delegation
of **seven gentlemen** — the doctors Giacomo Giuseppe Pezzolla and Martino Lippolis, the master craftsman
Ottavio Ciaccia, and four priests — who petitioned him during his visit to **Taranto**, and issued a
decree raising the village to *città regia*. First mayor elected **22 June 1797**.
**The royal title is right:** Ferdinand IV of Naples = Ferdinand III of Sicily = from 1816 Ferdinand I of
the Two Sicilies. ⚠️ **Whether the decree text survives could not be established**, and the *scomunica*
element of the delegation legend is unattested (H24).

### D6. "Its people kept building the way they knew" — CORRECTED, and the truth is the better ending
**Verdict: CORRECTED. The evidence runs the other way.**
ICOMOS: *"**New trulli construction declined following this period**"* — and the Italian national UNESCO
commission says the same. Within weeks of the decree, in the **summer of 1797**, **Antonio Francesco
D'Amore** built the **Casa d'Amore**, *"il primo trullo a due piani"*, celebrated precisely as the first
house raised once the dry-stone constraint was gone.
**So emancipation marked the end of trullo-building as the default, not its continuation.** That is a
sharper close than the old text's: *the moment they were free to use mortar, they used it.*
⚠️ The "first house built with lime mortar" formulation could not be verified — both Italian Wikipedia
spellings of Casa d'Amore 404 (H25). What is sourced: *first two-storey trullo, summer 1797, Antonio
Francesco D'Amore.*

### D7. UNESCO 1996 — CONFIRMED, with the full citation
**Verdict: CONFIRMED.** **"The Trulli of Alberobello", ref. 787**, inscribed at the **20th session,
Merida, Mexico, 2–7 December 1996**, decision **20 COM VIII.C**, criteria **(iii)(iv)(v)**. The Committee
also **changed the property's name** to "The Trulli of Alberobello" at inscription. Verbatim:
> *"…an exceptional example of a form of building construction deriving from **prehistoric construction
> techniques that have survived intact and functioning into the modern world**."*
Criterion (iii): *"the long-term use of dry-stone building, a technique which has a history of **many
thousands of years** in the Mediterranean region."* https://whc.unesco.org/en/decisions/2994 (primary)

### D8. The symbols and the pinnacles — the "mason's signature" is not documented
**Verdict: whitewash and symbols CONFIRMED; the pinnacle-as-signature DISPUTED.**
Whitewashed walls and bare grey cones: confirmed (Treccani; ICOMOS). The painted symbols are real — the
Italian UNESCO commission notes roofs often bore inscriptions in white ash with mythological or religious
significance — **but their meaning is not documented.** A 1940 catalogue recorded ***"200 segni
differenti"*** and ***"non esiste ancora una chiave di lettura unitaria"*** — no unified interpretive key
exists. One study states flatly that **no written documentation exists** on the symbols' meanings and
that attributing meaning to each is risky. ⚠️ Symbols were restored in **1926** and reinstated in **1934**
for a Mussolini visit [popular history]; whether they are repainted for tourists now is unverified (H26).
**The pinnacle:** the strongest form of the signature claim is explicit conjecture — it *"may* identify
the master builder"*, followed by *"it would be interesting to investigate further"*. English Wikipedia
states it baldly with no source. **What is solidly attested is the apotropaic character:** the cone ends
in *"una chiave di volta frequentemente scolpita con elementi lapidei decorativi a carattere **esoterico,
spirituale o scaramantico**"*. **Write: a carved keystone, usually a charm against bad luck; the
mason's-signature reading is traditional and undocumented.**

### D9. "Most are now holiday rentals" — CORRECTED, and the real number is bleaker and better
**Verdict: CORRECTED.** The only measured breakdown, carried in the UNESCO documentation from a 2007
assessment and repeated unchanged in the **2024 Periodic Report**:
> ***"30% of the trulli are in commercial use, largely in connection with tourism, 40% are abandoned, and
> only 30% in residential use."***
**The largest single category is abandoned.** The 2024 report lists building abandonment first among its
conservation concerns, alongside *"loss of authenticity in individual buildings"*. Comune population
peaked at **11,013 in 2010** and stood at **10,102 in 2024** (ISTAT), a fall of 911; no figure exists for
the historic quarters alone.
**"Cool in summer" — CONFIRMED**: over a metre of wall mass delays heat penetration, interiors running
about **6–8 °C cooler** than outside, with stack-effect ventilation through the cone.
⚠️ **"Damp in winter" — NO SOURCE.** The technical literature describes winter thermal lag, not damp.
**Drop it, or write "cold in winter".** (H27)

### D10. Trullo Sovrano — do not print 1744
**Verdict: DISPUTED on the date; "largest" and "only two-storey" CONFIRMED.**
The house-museum dates the left wing to *"the early 1600s"* and the rest to *"the first half of the
eighteenth century"*, commissioned by the family of the priest **Cataldo Perta (1744–1809)**. ⚠️ **That
is internally impossible — a man born in 1744 cannot have commissioned a building finished in the first
half of the 1700s. The widely quoted "built 1744" is almost certainly Cataldo Perta's birth year mistaken
for a construction date. Do not use 1744.** Italian Wikipedia gives instead a completion *"con atto
notarile **sabato 15 aprile 1797**"* — six weeks before the town's liberation — and spells the family
*Perna*. **The sources are irreconcilable (H28); write "late 1700s".**
Agreed: **main cone about 14 metres** over twelve smaller cones; the only two-storey trullo and the
largest; **National Monument 19 September 1923**; today Puglia's second-most visited monument after Castel
del Monte. ⚠️ Note the awkwardness that **Casa d'Amore is also called the first two-storey trullo** (D6).
**Safest wording: "the largest, and the only one with an upper floor reached by an internal stone stair."**
**For the food test:** a stair of 23 steps connects **the kitchen** to the upper floor, and the space over
the entrance corridor was **grain storage** and a refuge from brigands.

### D11. "The form is ancient" — CONFIRMED, with one caution
**Verdict: CONFIRMED.** UNESCO calls dry-stone building *"a prehistoric building technique still in use in
this region"*; Treccani traces the type to *"la **civiltà megalitica mediterranea**"*.
⚠️ **But the standing trulli are not ancient.** The earliest confirmed date to the *"fine del XVII
secolo"*, and ICOMOS records only about **forty** trulli at Monti by the mid-sixteenth century. **The
technique is prehistoric; the houses are early modern. Do not let the sentence imply otherwise.**

### D12. Castel del Monte, 1240 — the document, and what it will and will not carry
**Verdict: DISPUTED, and "finished within the decade" is the weakest claim in reading 4.**
**The only near-contemporary evidence** is the mandate of **29 January 1240**, issued from **Gubbio** to
**Riccardo da Montefuscolo**, *giustiziere* of Capitanata:
> *"Cum pro castro, quod apud **Sanctam Mariam de Monte** fieri volumus … **actractum ipsum in calce,
> lapidibus et omnibus aliis oportunis fieri facias sine mora**"*
— "since for the castle which we wish to be made at Santa Maria del Monte … have that *actractus* made
**in lime, stones and all other necessary things**, without delay." Edition: Huillard-Bréholles,
*Historia Diplomatica Friderici Secundi*, V/2 (Paris 1859), pp. 696–697.
**What it proves turns on one word, and three scholars disagree:**
- **Dankwart Leistikow (1994)** established that *actractus* means **the procurement and haulage of
  building material**, and concluded the mandate gives **less** chronological certainty than assumed —
  though the facts suggest *"il progettato **inizio** della costruzione."*
- **Giosuè Musca** reads it as **completion** work, the roof-bearing elements already standing.
- **Hubert Houben** (*Federiciana*, Treccani, 2005) **rejects** the near-completion reading: work began in
  1240. https://www.treccani.it/enciclopedia/castel-del-monte_(Federiciana)/
**Was it finished? Probably never.** Houben: surveys of **1990–1996** showed construction with
*"rudimentary technological means"* and the castle **never fully completed**. Italian Wikipedia:
*"Probabilmente alla morte di Federico II (avvenuta nel 1250) l'edificio non era ancora terminato."*
Missing or never executed: marble cladding, sculptural decoration, the wooden walkways round the first
floor.
⚠️ **UNESCO is unreliable on precisely this point.** Its Statement of OUV says the castle *"was completed
in 1240"* and ICOMOS says *"completed in 1240 and serving as his court's permanent seat"* — **a claim no
specialist source supports. Do not cite UNESCO for the chronology.**
**Write: ordered or under way in 1240, and probably never finished.**

### D13. "No moat, no kitchen, no stables" — CONFIRMED and strengthened; "almost nothing else" is wrong
**Verdict: the absences are CONFIRMED by the best source; the implication of emptiness is CORRECTED.**
**ICOMOS No 398 gives a longer and better list than the old text's:** notably absent are *"**outer bailey,
moat, stables, kitchen, storerooms, chapel**"* — adding **storerooms and a chapel**.
**But the castle is full of services, and this is what "almost nothing else" gets wrong:**
- **Six cisterns** — five built into the towers between the vaults and the roof, plus one large cistern
  under the courtyard, still functional.
- **Latrines and bathrooms.** Treccani lists *"canalizzazioni idrauliche, latrine e camini"*; some towers
  held *"una serie di bagni dotati di latrine e lavabi"*, with clay and stone piping and drainage to
  vertical shafts.
- **Five fireplaces**, with *"i resti di altissimi camini"* visible in the halls — serving heating **and
  cooking**, which materially weakens "no kitchen" as evidence of no domestic life. **Houben cites the
  fireplaces and the rainwater systems as positive evidence of residential use.**
⚠️ **The curtain wall is genuinely open.** English Wikipedia says scholars now believe it *"originally had
a curtain wall"*; another reference work notes *"ancient documents mention a surrounding wall, now
destroyed"*. **No excavation report was reached (H29). Treat as an open question, not a settled absence.**
⚠️ **"Windows that face the wrong way for defence" — NO SOURCE FOUND (H30). Drop it.** Replace with the
sourced absences, and note **Raffaele Licinio's** counter-argument that medieval castles routinely lacked
drawbridges and were fortresses nonetheless.
Later additions are documented: in **1277** under Charles I of Anjou, *bertesche* and four turrets were
added and the windows barred.

### D14. The theories — the old text gives esotericists the standing of historians
**Verdict: CORRECTED on attribution.**
**Serious scholarship:** **Houben** — residential (the fireplaces and water systems), representational,
possibly a hunting lodge with falcons, and *no document confirms Frederick ever visited*. **Raffaele
Licinio** (Bari) — genuine defensive capability. **Massimiliano Ambruoso** — territorial control.
**Musca** — fortress and representational residence. **Heinz Götze**, *Castel del Monte: Gestalt und
Symbol der Architektur Friedrichs II* (1984; English, Prestel 1998) — the mathematical/Platonic reading,
tangents of the courtyard octagon meeting at the tower centres in an eight-pointed star. ⚠️ **Götze ran
Springer-Verlag and was a scholar of geometry, not a medieval historian**; the book is serious and widely
cited but is a geometric-symbolic reading, not an archival one. **Fallacara & Occhinegro (2011)** — a
*hammam*-modelled "wellness centre", real academic work but a distinct minority.
**Fringe, and named as such by the sources:** the solstice-alignment readings of **Aldo Tavolaro**, a Bari
**amateur astronomer**; and the Holy Grail, initiatory-temple and observatory readings. *Finestre
sull'Arte* rejects these as *"unsupported by any document"*; **Licinio's word for them is
*fanta-esoteriche*.**
**"The octagon between the square of the earth and the circle of heaven"** is a real and commonly reported
reading, but the literature presents it **anonymously, as an interpretation**. Better grounded are the
possible models of the **Dome of the Rock** and the **Palatine Chapel at Aachen**.
⚠️ **"A treasury" — no source proposes it. Drop it.**
**Write: historians argue over fortress, residence, hunting lodge and statement of imperial power, and
medievalists explicitly reject the astronomical and Grail readings.**

### D15. "25 metres high" — an uncited figure, contradicted by every Italian measurement
**Verdict: DISPUTED. Do not print 25 m.**
The 25 m / 26 m / 16.5 m set **comes from English Wikipedia and carries no footnote on any of those
sentences.** The Italian measured figures are mutually consistent and contradict them: **towers 23 m**
(footnoted) or **24 m**; courtyard wall **20.50 m**, which the towers *"superano di poco"*; outer octagon
side **10.30 m** against the English 16.5 m; tower diameter 7.90 m; courtyard diameter 17.86 m. The
castle's overall diameter is given as **both 40 m and 56 m within a single article.**
**Elevation CONFIRMED: 539 m** (Italian Wikipedia) / **540 m** — "a hill of about 540 metres near Andria"
is fine. ⚠️ **No survey-grade source was obtained**; the state museum site was unreachable on every
attempt (H31). **Say "towers about 24 metres", or give no number.**
**Eight rooms on each of two floors — CONFIRMED** by ICOMOS: *"two stories with **eight equal chambers
per level**"*, trapezoidal, around the octagonal courtyard, with spiral stairs in three of the eight
towers and **the total absence of corridors**.

### D16. UNESCO 1996 and the one-cent coin — both CONFIRMED
**Castel del Monte, ref. 398**, decision **20 COM VIII.C**, Merida, December 1996, criteria
**(i)(ii)(iii)** — ICOMOS had recommended only (i) and (iii); **the Committee added (ii)**. Property
**3.1 ha**, buffer zone **10,847.3 ha**, communes of Andria and Corato. The property had been **deferred
in 1987** before its 1995 re-nomination. Criterion (i): *"a **unique masterpiece of medieval military
architecture** which reflects the humanist ideas of its founder"*. https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/398
**The coin — CONFIRMED.** Castel del Monte is on the Italian **1-cent** coin, designed by **Eugenio
Driutti**; the subjects were chosen by public telephone vote in a **RAI Uno broadcast of 8 February

### D17. Frederick II — *stupor mundi* is quotable; "six languages" is not
**Verdict: *stupor mundi* CONFIRMED with its exact source; "six languages" DISPUTED; "a German emperor"
loose.**
**The phrase is Matthew Paris's**, in the margin of his autograph manuscript (Cambridge, Corpus Christi
College, MS 016II, fol. 245r), at Frederick's death on **13 December 1250**:
> *"Obiit… principum mundi maximus Frethericus, **stupor mundi et immutator mirabilis**"*
— "there died Frederick, greatest of the princes of the world, **the wonder of the world and its
marvellous transformer**." Ed. Liebermann, *MGH SS* XXVIII (Hannover, 1888), p. 319.
⚠️ **Two caveats:** Matthew Paris used the expression of **other figures** too, and in medieval usage
*stupor* carried alarm at someone overturning the established order as much as admiration. **That second
sense is the more interesting one and the reading could use it.**
⚠️ **"Six languages" — the number is a modern secondary claim with no stable list.** Italian Wikipedia:
*"sei lingue (latino, siciliano, tedesco, francese, greco e **forse arabo**)"* — note *perhaps* Arabic.
English Wikipedia gives **two inconsistent lists in one article** (five, including Provençal; and six).
**Norbert Kamp** (*DBI* 45, 1995) and **Michael McVaugh** (*Complete DSB*) — the two scholarly reference
works reached — **enumerate no languages at all.** **Avoid the number.**
⚠️ **"A German emperor" is the least accurate available description.** UNESCO's own wording is better:
*"a Germanic Emperor who was **born at Iesi**, **brought up in Sicily** and attracted at a very early age
by the Oriental world."*

### D18. The falconry book and the menagerie — both CONFIRMED, and both better than the old text
***De arte venandi cum avibus***, composed in the **1240s**, later amended by his son **Manfred**;
McVaugh notes Frederick worked at it over roughly **thirty years**. The method is *"**systematic personal
observation**"*: he tested whether eggs would hatch by the sun's warmth alone, and **covered vultures'
eyes to determine whether they hunt by smell or sight** (concluding sight). His stated principle:
***"Ea que sunt, sicut sunt"*** — to describe things as they are — and he criticised Aristotle where
observation disagreed. **Vatican Pal. lat. 1071** is the illustrated copy commissioned by Manfred, 111
folios, with Manfred's additions marked *"Rex"*, *"Rex Manfredus"*, *"addidit Rex"*, and **nearly 900
images of birds**, some left uncoloured — **the manuscript is itself unfinished**, like the castle.
**The menagerie — documented by contemporaries.** **Salimbene de Adam of Parma**, *Cronica*, s.a. 1235:
Frederick *"sent an **elephant** to Lombardy which many **dromedaries and camels**, along with many
**leopards, gyrfalcons, and hawks**"*. An anonymous Veronese chronicler, 1245: *"**one elephant, 24
camels, and 5 leopards**"*. The **Cremona elephant** was a gift from **Sultan al-Kamil of Egypt in 1229**.
Three permanent menageries, at **Melfi, Lucera and Palermo**. ⚠️ **The giraffe is attested in the
scholarly secondary literature but its donor is not** — al-Kamil's documented gift is the elephant (H32).

### D19. Did he ever stay there? — no record, and the afterlife is documented
**Verdict: the old text does not claim a stay, which is right.** Houben: *"**No documents confirm Frederick
II ever visited**."* The one documented event is the **1246** marriage of his natural daughter **Violante**
to **Riccardo Sanseverino, count of Caserta** ⚠️ (other sources date it 1249 — hedge or omit).
Afterwards: a prison from **1246/1253**; under Charles I of Anjou from **1266/1268** a state prison holding
**Manfred's sons**, to **1299**; celebrations in **1308 and 1326**; damaged **1528**; sold to Count
Fabrizio Carafa in **1552** for **100,000 ducats**; a refuge from **plague** for the noble families of
Andria in **1656**; then abandoned, **stripped of its marble and furnishings**, and shelter for *"pastori,
briganti e profughi politici"*; **bought by the Italian State in 1876 for 25,000 lire**, restored from
1879. **"Quarry" is better put as systematic stripping.**

### D20. THE FOOD TEST — the trulli and Frederick
**The trulli reach a table by four documented routes, and Castel del Monte barely does.**
1. **The settlement was founded to clear land and farm it, on shares.** Andrea Matteo III Acquaviva
   *"introdusse dal feudo di Noci **una quarantina di famiglie di contadini** per bonificare e coltivare
   le terre, **con l'obbligo di consegnargli la decima dei raccolti**"* — forty peasant families, and **a
   tithe of the harvests**. ⚠️ **The crops are not named in any source reached (H33).**
2. **The count's first public buildings were food buildings.** ICOMOS: expansion accelerated in **1620**
   when the count ordered the construction of *"a **bakery, mill, and inn**"*. Italian Wikipedia dates the
   equivalent act to **1635**: an inn *"con annessi **refettorio, taverna** e oratorio"*. ⚠️ The two dates
   conflict; give one or hedge. **Either way the town began with an oven, a mill and a tavern** — and by
   the end of the eighteenth century held over **3,500** people. **This is the single best food fact in
   reading 4.**
3. **The trullo is a farm building as much as a house** — *"case familiari"*, *"depositi agricoli"*,
   *"ricoveri temporanei nei campi"*; the Trullo Sovrano has a kitchen and a grain store. **Rainwater was
   channelled off the *chiancarelle* into underground cisterns** — ICOMOS calls the system *"ingenious"* —
   and **on a waterless karst plateau the cistern is what made cooking and settlement possible at all.**
4. **Frederick as the realm's largest grain merchant**, from **Giuseppe Petralia**, "Commercio",
   *Federiciana*: the register of **1239–1240** shows him accumulating grain from demesne lands and
   peasant levies and selling the surplus abroad *"in Levante o in Barberia"*; in **October 1239** he
   established **eleven new ports** as the sole permitted export points; the **fondaci** concentrated
   external trade; **salt** was a royal monopoly; and from **1234** a circuit of **fairs** ran April to
   November through Lucera, Bari and Taranto. The Constitutions of Melfi (**1 September 1231**) regulated
   **weights and measures uniformly across the realm**.
   **And the reversal worth having, from Jean-Marie Martin, "Agricoltura", *Federiciana*:** *"The
   departure and deportation of the Arabs of Sicily led to the **disappearance** of sugar cane and the
   palm: in **1239 Frederick II prescribed the search for the now-forgotten techniques of sugar
   production**."* **He did not introduce sugar — he tried to recover a technique the deportations he
   ordered had destroyed.** Martin also records *massarie* created **in Capitanata**, oats prescribed
   there, and regional specialisation — *"cereali nel Tavoliere"*.
5. **The Dogana della Mena delle Pecore — the strongest landmark-to-table route in the region.** Founded
   **1 August 1447** by **Alfonso I of Aragon**, seated at Lucera, moved to **Foggia from 1468**. The
   Crown controlled the Tavoliere's state lands and designated **tratturi 60 Neapolitan paces = 111 metres
   wide**; shepherds with more than twenty sheep were **compelled** into the system and paid the *fida*.
   **What they got is the food part:** *"substantial discounts on **flour, oil, wine and salt**"*, tax
   relief on their products, and toll-free migration. **Within five years transhumant sheep doubled to
   nearly one million head**, ranging between about 600,000 and 1,500,000 thereafter. The products were
   **wool, lamb, and the cheeses — caciocavallo and pecorino** (which ties straight back to F35 and F36).
   The network ran about **3,100 km**. **Abolished 21 May 1806** under Joseph Bonaparte.
   ⚠️ **The 1865 disentailment could not be verified (H34); use 1806.** ⚠️ Transhumance is on UNESCO's
   intangible list — **2019, extended 2023; verify before printing a year.**
⚠️ **Margherita di Savoia's salt is real and Puglian — 20 km by 5 km, about 550,000 tonnes a year, called
by the Bourbons *"la più preziosa gemma della loro corona"* — but has NO documented Frederick connection
(H35). Do not weld it to him.**

## G. Reading 4 — Lecce, Otranto and the coast

### D21. "The Florence of the south" — the epithet is "Florence of the **Baroque**"
**Verdict: CORRECTED.** The phrase attributed to **Ferdinand Gregorovius** is ***"Firenze del Barocco"***
— Florence of the Baroque, not of the south. *Firenze del Sud* is the later tourist-board mutation.
https://www.treccani.it/enciclopedia/lecce_(Enciclopedia-Italiana)/ (Treccani, *Enciclopedia Italiana*)
⚠️ **Gregorovius's German wording could not be retrieved (H36) — do not put German words in his mouth.**
Gregorovius toured the south in the 1870s; the Apulian chapter is *Apulische Landschaften* (1877).

### D22. "Flush with money from oil and wheat" — DISPUTED, and the chronology is wrong
**Verdict: DISPUTED. Half-supported, and out by a century.**
- **The oil wealth is real but is Gallipoli's, not Lecce's, and it peaked in the eighteenth century** —
  **after** Santa Croce's façade was finished in 1646.
- **Treccani's account of Lecce's wealth names merchants, not agriculture:** from the fourteenth century
  *"lo spirito militare cede a quello commerciale e manifatturiero"*, and by the 1500s *"Lecce divenne
  convegno di mercanti: **fiorentini (Pigli, Carnesecchi, Peruzzi), veneziani e genovesi**"*. The building
  boom is attributed to private citizens, clergy and ecclesiastical congregations.
- **"Wheat" is unsupported for the Salento.** Puglia's grain plain is the **Tavoliere, in the Capitanata**
  — the other end of the region. The Terra d'Otranto is olive monoculture, *"distese di ulivi secolari"*.
**Rewrite as merchants and oil, or as oil alone and locate it at Gallipoli.**

### D23. Pietra leccese — the geology is CONFIRMED and specific
**Verdict: CONFIRMED.** The **Pietra Leccese Formation**, **late Burdigalian to early Messinian**
(Miocene), an outer-shelf deposit: a homogeneous porous **biomicrite**, pale yellow, fine- to
medium-grained, very rich in planktonic foraminifera. Measured: **total porosity 30–43%**; water
absorption 16–28%; **UCS dry 12.8–23.4 MPa, saturated 7.1–12.2 MPa**; tensile strength dry 1.2–2.7 MPa,
**saturated 0.42–1.5 MPa**.
**The mechanically important fact, and it is the opposite of the folklore: wetting roughly halves its
strength.** https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9145597/ (*Materials* 15(10):3658, 2022,
peer-reviewed conservation science)
**"Cut with a saw and carved like wood" — CONFIRMED.** Treccani: *"facilmente scavabile e tagliabile…
tenerissima e docile, bella di un colore caldo."*

### D24. **"Hardens afterwards in the air" — DISPUTED, and it is promotional, not scientific**
**Verdict: DISPUTED. Also the scored answer in `quiz.js` 54–57 and `quiz.no.js` 54–57.**
**Who asserts it:** the **Camera di Commercio di Lecce / Unioncamere** — *"soft and easy to work when
extracted from the quarry, but once exposed to air and the elements, it becomes hard and resistant"*
[promotional] — and tourism copy claiming it hardens *"to near-marble durability"* [tourist board].
**Who does not: the conservation-science literature, which says the opposite about durability.** The 2022
*Materials* study describes pietra leccese as having *"**low durability and… susceptible to degradation in
the external environment** due to their porosity and low degree of cementation"*, decaying by *"the
crystallization of soluble salts; the formation of efflorescence, subflorescence, black crusts and
patinas; alveolization; biological alteration."* **No measured hardening on exposure appears in any
peer-reviewed source reached.**
**The real phenomenon it is confused with:** **case hardening** is genuine but documented in **silica-rich
porous rocks** — sandstone, quartzite, volcanic ash — not in porous biomicritic limestone. The closest
analogue, Malta's Globigerina Limestone, develops only *"a rosy colour, which browns with the formation of
a protective **patina**"* — **a patina, not bulk hardening.**
**Drop it, or attribute it explicitly as a workshop belief. The quiz question built on it must be
rewritten.**

### D25. What the masons actually put on the stone — a documented replacement fact, and it reaches a table
**Verdict: CONFIRMED, and far better than the folklore it replaces.**
A 2022 analytical study of **ten Lecce buildings and more than fifty samples** (optical microscopy,
ATR-FTIR, pyrolysis-GC-MS), covering the seventeenth to nineteenth centuries, identified the historic
surface treatments:
- **Milk** — the most frequent, identified by *"toluene and indols (derived from tryptophan), pyrrole and
  derivatives"*; possibly sheep's milk on the fatty-acid ratios, though *"the data are not conclusive"*.
- **Walnut oil**, not linseed — *"azelaic (C9) and suberic (C8) acids"* at Palazzo Bernardini.
- **Egg white** (not yolk) at the Ex-Hospital.
- ***Opuntia ficus-indica***, prickly-pear leaf — **the study's first scientific confirmation** of the
  treatment on Lecce stone.
- **No beeswax, lime wash or animal glue** was found.
https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9145597/
**This is the single best find in the Lecce cluster: milk, egg white and prickly pear off the kitchen
shelf, brushed onto cathedral façades. It replaces a folk claim with a documented one, and it is the only
Lecce material that reaches a table.**

### D26. Santa Croce "1549–1695" — the 1695 belongs to a different building
**Verdict: CORRECTED.** The chronology: begun **1549**; **Gabriele Riccardi** builds the lower order,
finished **1582**; dome **1590**; the three portals from **1606** under **Francesco Antonio Zimbalo**;
**Cesare Penna** the upper order and rose window, main altar **1639**; **the rose-window cartouche is
dated 1646**, marking the façade's completion. The basilica's own history: *"fu eretta a partire dal 1549,
ma i lavori proseguirono **per quasi un secolo**"* — **nearly a century, not a century and a half.**
**1695 is the adjoining Palazzo dei Celestini**, whose *"lungo prospetto (**1659-1695**)"* was built by
Giuseppe Zimbalo and Giuseppe Cino. **English Wikipedia conflates the convent with the basilica; the
Italian article does not contain the date 1695 at all.**
**Correct span: 1549–1646, ninety-seven years.** https://basilicasantacrocelecce.it/storia-della-basilica/

### D27. "Three generations of the Zimbalo family" — two, and one of the three hands was not a Zimbalo
**Verdict: CORRECTED.** **Francesco Antonio Zimbalo (1567–1631) was the grandfather of Giuseppe Zimbalo
(1620–1710)**, "lo Zingarello" — two generations, skipping one — and **only two Zimbalos worked on Santa
Croce.** The third hand on the façade was **Cesare Penna, who was not a Zimbalo at all.**
https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francesco_Antonio_Zimbalo

### D28. Gallipoli's oil — the best food-money material in reading 4
**Verdict: CONFIRMED, with figures.** Gallipoli held **35 *frantoi ipogei*** and about **2,000 cisterns**;
roughly **70% of the Salento's olive-oil exports** passed through its port, making it the Mediterranean's
leading ***olio lampante*** port by the eighteenth century. Destinations: London, Paris, Amsterdam, Oslo,
Stockholm, Vienna, Venice — and **Russia, for liturgical lamps**; England took it for textile mills and
dye works. **Vice-consulates operated in Gallipoli until 1923** for a dozen states.
**The detail that makes it a story: this was lamp oil, not cooking oil — high-acidity, inedible, and it
burned without smoke. Puglia's great oil trade lit other people's streets.** The trade collapsed with
electric lighting in the nineteenth century, and the Frantoio del Viceré was rediscovered only in **2002**.
**The one genuinely archival datum:** a **1569 notarial act of notary Cornelio Tollemeto of Nardò** for
**520 *stara*** of oil (≈**8,580 litres**) shipped to Venice via Gallipoli, *"chiaro musto et morga
reservato, franco di piazza et mercantia"*.
⚠️ The often-quoted **85,000 quintals** a year traces to a **1974 magazine article**, not an archive (H37).
⚠️ **The underground mills are cut in *carparo*, not pietra leccese** — *"totalmente scavato nel carparo,
il tufo pugliese"*. **Do not claim pietra leccese for oil presses.**

### D37. Ostuni's whitewash — DISPUTED, and the plague story is retro-fitted
**Verdict: DISPUTED. The caption states folklore as fact.**

cheap and abundant lime, plus light: the custom is *"attestata fin dal Medioevo, deriva, oltre che dalla

**Write: whitewash was the cheap, abundant, light-reflecting lime finish of a limestone country, practised

**The caption in `puglia.js` line 145 — "The whitewash was a defence against plague" — must be rewritten.**

### D40. Griko — true of nine villages, not of the Salento

## H. The delete-the-food test for reading 4

The rule: a history paragraph earns its place only if the food or wine sentences collapse without it.
Applying it to what this pass found:

**REACHES A TABLE — build the reading on these:**
1. **The trulli exist because the fields were being cleared to farm.** The stone in the walls came out of
   the ground being made arable, and **forty peasant families were brought from Noci under obligation to
   hand over a tithe of the harvests** (D20.1). The house is a by-product of the tithe.
2. **Alberobello's first public buildings were a bakery, a mill and an inn** (1620 or 1635 — D20.2). A
   town that begins with an oven, a mill and a tavern is a town the food reading can own.
3. **The cistern under the cone.** Rainwater channelled off the *chiancarelle* into underground cisterns
   on a waterless karst plateau — **without it there is no cooking and no settlement** (D20.3).
4. **The Dogana della Mena delle Pecore, 1447–1806** (D20.5). Compelled transhumance, 111-metre *tratturi*,
   a million sheep, and shepherds paid partly in **discounted flour, oil, wine and salt** — producing the
   **wool, lamb, caciocavallo and pecorino** that reading 3 already eats (F35, F36).
5. **Frederick as the realm's largest grain merchant**, eleven export ports in October 1239, salt a royal
   monopoly, uniform weights and measures from 1231 — and **the 1239 order to go and rediscover how to make
   sugar**, a technique his own deportations had destroyed (D20.4).
6. **Otranto's Labours of the Months** (D31): the grape harvest, the treading, the threshing, the December
   pig, and a November panel containing a ham and a loaf. **A medieval Apulian food calendar in stone.**
7. **Gallipoli's lamp oil** (D28): 35 underground mills, 70% of Salento exports, vice-consulates to 1923 —
   and the twist that **the oil was inedible and lit other people's streets.**
8. **Lecce's façades were dressed with milk, egg white and prickly pear** (D25) — the kitchen shelf applied
   to the cathedral.
9. **Monopoli**: fishing port and oil (D38).

**DOES NOT REACH A TABLE — Key facts box, a clause, or nothing:**
- Castel del Monte's geometry, the octagon symbolism, the tower dimensions, the one-cent coin (D14, D15,
  D16) — **the castle has no kitchen and no storerooms, which is ICOMOS's own finding; it actively resists
  the food test.** The two crumbs are the five fireplaces that also cooked, and the **companion mandate of
  29 January 1240 provisioning the imperial houses *"de gallinis, anseribus et anatibus"*** — with hens,
  geese and ducks. **That is the one charming food fact attached to the castle, and it is enough for a
  clause, not a paragraph.**
- UNESCO inscription dates and criteria for all four sites — **Key facts box.**
- *Stupor mundi*, the six languages, the falconry manuscript, the menagerie (D17, D18) — **colour, not
  structure. The vulture experiment is the only one that earns a sentence, and only if the reading is
  about how he thought.**
- Santa Croce's construction dates, the Zimbalos, pietra leccese's geology (D23, D26, D27).
- Otranto's martyrs, the chapel, the canonisation (D32, D33) — **monument and martyrology.**
- The lighthouse, the basilica, Gallipoli's bridge, the Tremiti, Modugno (D34, D38, D39).
- Monte Sant'Angelo (D36) — **and the pilgrim-provisioning angle does not exist in the record. Do not
  invent it.**
- Griko (D40) — language, not food.

**The structural conclusion: reading 4 as it stands is a monument tour. What this pass found is that its
food spine is available and strong — clearing, tithe, oven, cistern, tratturo, wool and cheese — and that
the spine runs through Alberobello and the Tavoliere, not through Castel del Monte.**

## I. Candidate openings

Ruled out by the brief and not proposed: a named producer or writer introduced with a date; an
archaeological feature described in the present tense; a hypothetical person or animal; a lawsuit or
institution going to court. Each below is sourced, concrete and human-scale, with the chain it opens.

1. **The town that closed a McDonald's (F10).** A McDonald's opens in Altamura in **2001**; a few months
   later the baker **Luca Di Gesù and his brother Giuseppe** open a focaccia shop nearby; **within about a
   year the McDonald's has closed for want of custom**, and by 2002 *Panorama*, *Libération* and the *New
   York Times* are all carrying it. **Chain:** a town where bread is the industry, not the local colour →
   because the bread is a protected designation with four named wheats and a 250 °C oven → and the thing
   that beat the chain was not nostalgia but a cheaper, better lunch. **The best opening for reading 3.**
   A crowd, a shopfront, a date, and a prejudice overturned inside the sentence. ⚠️ Attribute the closure
   as reported; the focacceria was *a* cause, not *the* cause.

2. **The women take their tables in (F21).** After a year of hygiene inspections, a fraud investigation
   opened in **February 2025**, Guardia di Finanza raids and **€5,000 fines**, the Comune of Bari publishes
   its rules in **June 2025**: fresh pasta may no longer be made in the street except for show. **Only six
   pastaie come forward to register.** The rest carry their tables and *telai* indoors and say nothing.
   **Chain:** a shape made by hand for centuries → sold from a doorstep to whoever walked past → which is
   precisely what a food-safety regime cannot certify → so the most photographed street in Bari is now a
   demonstration. One vendor: *«Sì, sono stata multata, ma cosa dovevo fare? La domanda era enorme e non
   riuscivo a tenere il passo.»* **The strongest and most current opening available**, and it makes the
   reading's own sentence honest.

3. **A bread registered on somebody else's poem (F2).** The European Union's specification for Pane di
   Altamura opens its history with Horace — and concedes in its own first clause that the passage
   *"concerns the Murgia region rather than Altamura"*. The poem does not name a town at all: *"oppidulo,
   quod versu dicere non est"*, a little town whose name will not fit a hexameter. **Chain:** the joke is
   metrical, not secret → the commentators' candidate is Venosa, in another region → and the bread's real
   credentials are in the same document anyway: four named wheats, 80%, 20% leaven refreshed three times,
   an oven at 250 °C, the door open for the first fifteen minutes. **Opens on a debunk and lands on the
   thing that is actually impressive.**

4. **Sea urchins, banned until 2029 (F42).** In **April 2023** Puglia made it illegal to take, hold,
   transport, land or sell *Paracentrotus lividus* from its own waters for three years — *"scongiurando il
   rischio di estinzione dovuto ai **massicci prelievi**"*. The Constitutional Court upheld it in **2024**;
   in **June 2026** it was extended to **30 June 2029**. A Bari restaurant may still serve urchins, but not
   Puglian ones. **Chain:** a plate eaten on the quay, straight from the shell → eaten to the edge of the
   species → so the law now separates the dish from its own sea. **Sharp, current, and it corrects the old
   text's present tense in the act of opening on it.**

5. **The drought that rewrote the recipe (F8).** After the **2024 drought halved the harvest**, the
   ministry suspended the rule that Pane di Altamura be made 80% from **appulo, arcangelo, duilio and
   simeto** — decree of **7 February 2025**, in force to 31 July. Any durum from the territory would do.
   **Chain:** a bread defined by four cultivars on one plateau → a year the plateau could not grow them →
   so the definition moved instead of the bread → and **561 tonnes** is the whole annual DOP production
   anyway. **The DOP system caught doing the thing the reading is about.**

6. **Forty families and a tithe (D20.1, D20.2).** The counts of Conversano bring **about forty peasant
   families from Noci** to clear and farm an empty stretch of the Murgia, **obliged to hand over a tithe of
   the harvests**; the first public buildings the count orders are **a bakery, a mill and an inn**.
   **Chain:** the stone in the walls is the stone cleared off the fields → the houses are a by-product of
   making the land arable → the cones are corbelled dry because that is how the Murgia builds, and the
   tax story came afterwards → and a town that opens with an oven, a mill and a tavern is a food town
   before it is a postcard. **The best opening for reading 4**, and it fixes the tax myth without
   announcing that it is fixing it.

7. **November, with a ham in it (D31).** On the floor of Otranto cathedral, laid between **1163 and 1165**,
   the months go round: August the grape harvest, September treading the grapes, July threshing, December
   killing the pig — and November a young man **scattering seed gathered in his tunic, among an axe, a
   drinking horn, a wine barrel, a ham and a loaf**. **Chain:** the calendar sits next to the expulsion
   from Eden, so the work is both the punishment and the way back → which is what a farming year looked
   like from inside → and the same twelve jobs are still the Apulian year. **A food calendar in stone, in
   a building reading 4 already visits.**

8. **The oil that was not for eating (D28).** Gallipoli had **35 underground mills** and about **2,000
   cisterns**, and shipped roughly **70% of the Salento's oil** to London, Amsterdam, Stockholm and Russia
   — **vice-consulates for a dozen states operated in the town until 1923**. **Chain:** the oil was
   *lampante*, high-acid and inedible → it lit other people's streets and greased other people's looms →
   electricity killed the trade → and the mills were cut in *carparo*, under the town, where one was
   rediscovered only in **2002**. **A whole economy that never reached a table, which is why it is worth
   saying so.**

9. **The house that came apart, and who pulled it down (D4).** The tradition says the trulli were built
   dry so the village could be flattened before a royal inspector; ICOMOS records a dismantling in
   **1644**, and adds the motive the story usually leaves out — the technique also enabled **"dispossession
   of recalcitrant residents"**. **Chain:** a house that comes apart in a day is a house the landlord can
   take apart → the taxman is the version the town tells → the scholars' version is that dry stone was
   simply how you built on a karst plateau → **and in 1644 the count himself was in a prison in Madrid.**
   Opens on the legend and dismantles it with a better fact.

10. **The cheese that is a fresh manteca (F29).** The IGP dossier says burrata was invented by making a
    **manteca** — the old trick of sealing butter inside a skin of aged curd — with cream, fresh, because
    the snow had closed the roads and the milk had to be used: *"la tipica connotazione della cultura
    contadina, **refrattaria allo spreco**"*. **Chain:** an emergency became a product → the emergency is
    oral tradition and the dossier says so twice (*si tramanda*, *si racconta*) → the date everyone prints,
    1956, is in no primary source → and today the protected name may be made **anywhere in Puglia, with
    UHT cream**. Opens on a good story and ends on what the law actually protects.

11. **The pastry that was not invented in 1745 (F53).** Galatina says its **pasticciotto** was invented by
    the Ascalone family in **1745**. The parish register of SS. Pietro e Paolo says **Vincenzo Scalone
    married there in 1787**, having come from Galatone; the tax rolls of **1810** call him a spirits
    seller; the ***catasto onciario* of 1745–1754 lists no pastry shops in Galatina at all**; and the word
    *pasticciotto* is already in a **1707 inventory of the bishop of Nardò**. **Chain:** the family was not
    in the town → the trade was not in the town → the word was already elsewhere → and the shop still
    trading on the old centre dates itself to **1740**, five years off its own legend. **The cleanest
    archival debunk in the dossier.**

## L. What Puglia's food story actually is

**The research overturns the frame the old text opens on, and it does so in a way that makes the reading
better rather than poorer.**

Reading 3 begins: *"The cooking of Puglia is cucina povera in the strict sense: what a landless peasant
could grow, gather or afford."* Half of that is solid and half of it is a category the field has stopped
accepting. The poverty is real and documentable — the *latifondo* worked by labourers who lived in towns
and walked out to the fields, the Salento deliberately excluded from the 1950 land reform, two thousand
men walking fifteen kilometres to occupy the Arneo on 28 December 1950 (F12). But *cucina povera* as an
explanatory category is an ideological construction, not a historical description: Montanari shows peasant
foods sitting comfortably inside elite recipe books from the fourteenth century onward, and Helstosky
dates the *valorisation* of austerity to the 1920s and 1930s, when regional cooking became an attribute of
*italianità* (F11). Writing "in the strict sense" asserts precisely the thing the scholarship denies.

**And the frame is wrong in a second, larger way: Puglia's food story is not a story about scarcity at
all. It is a story about surplus that went somewhere else.** This region produced about **a quarter to a
third of Italy's durum wheat** and roughly **a third of its olive-growing hectares** (F13, F14, F55). Its
oil was shipped from Gallipoli — thirty-five underground mills, seventy per cent of the Salento's exports,
vice-consulates for a dozen states operating until 1923 — and **it was lamp oil, inedible, burned in the
streets of London, Amsterdam, Stockholm and in Russian churches** (D28). Its wine went north in tankers to
give colour and alcohol to thinner reds, a point reading 2 already makes. Its wool, its lamb and its
caciocavallo came out of a compelled transhumance system run from Foggia from 1447, where shepherds were
paid partly in **discounted flour, oil, wine and salt** (D20.5). Frederick II ran the kingdom's grain as
its largest single merchant and opened eleven ports in October 1239 for the express purpose of exporting
it (D20.4). Even the trulli are a by-product of extraction: forty families brought in from Noci to clear
and plant, under obligation to hand over a tithe of the harvest, building their houses out of the stone
they cleared (D20.1).

**So the honest sentence is not that Puglians ate poorly because they had nothing. It is that they ate
what stayed behind after the good stuff was loaded onto ships.** Durum wheat in every form, because the
wheat was here and the mills were here. Oil on everything, because oil was the cash crop and the ordinary
grades never left. Bitter greens and dried beans, because the bean was the rotation crop that let the
wheat grow at all (F45). Bread built to last a shepherd's fortnight away from home, because the men were
away from home for a fortnight (F5). Horse, because a worn-out draught animal became food. That is a
cuisine shaped by an export economy, not by an absence of one — and it explains the specific dishes far
better than "what a landless peasant could afford" does.

**The third act is the one the last three years have written, and the old text predates all of it.** The
protected-name era is Puglia finally selling under its own name: Pane di Altamura the first bread in
Europe to hold a designation of *origin* (F1), Burrata di Andria (F25), Capocollo di Martina Franca with
its IGP decision due within days of this pass (F52). But the dossier is full of evidence of what that
costs. A drought halved the 2024 harvest and the ministry suspended the four named wheats rather than let
the bread fail certification (F8). Bari's pasta women were regularised into home food businesses with
HACCP plans, and only six came forward (F21). Sea urchins cannot legally be taken from Puglian water until
30 June 2029 because the dish ate the species (F42). Canestrato Pugliese is down to 27,805 kilos a year
(F36). **That is the living tension the rewrite should carry: a cuisine built on what was left over, now
being defined, certified and in places forbidden — and, once, a town that beat a McDonald's with a
focaccia (F10).**
