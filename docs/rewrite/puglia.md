## 1. What the neighbouring regions have already spent

Written before the outlines. Puglia sits downstream of four regions that have just been rewritten, and three
of its four readings have a live collision.

**Abruzzo 3 and Molise 3 own the transhumance, and Puglia is where it ended up.** Between them they have
already spent: the *tratturi* (111.6 m wide, sixty Neapolitan paces; about 3,100 km of network; the
243-km Pescasseroli–Candela; the death penalty for moving the markers), the **Dogana della Mena delle Pecore**
(founded at Lucera in 1447 by Alfonso of Aragon, moved to **Foggia in 1468** under Ferrante; 4,471,496 sheep
and 9,600 cattle counted in 1592; 622,173 ducats), the Foggia registers naming individual shepherds, the
cheesemaker who walked with every flock, and the animal that could not finish the walk. Abruzzo 3 names the
Tavoliere twice as the destination.

→ **Puglia may not re-tell the drove roads or the customs house.** What is genuinely Puglia's, and unspent, is
the *other* side of that arrangement: the Tavoliere as a plain held as winter pasture by a customs
administration while it could have been ploughed, and what happened to it when that ended. If the research
supports it, that is Puglia's wheat story and it belongs to this region alone. One clause of cross-reference
is allowed; a paragraph is not.

**Molise 3 owns the dragged-pasta family and the whole stretched-curd dairy.** It states in as many words
that Molise's cavatelli "belong to the same dragged family as Puglia's orecchiette and are built the same
way", and it explains *pasta filata* from the curd up: caciocavallo of Agnone heated and stretched "as
mozzarella is", shaped into a pear with a head and hung in pairs; scamorza and its two etymologies; burrino
/manteca, butter inside a skin of stretched curd. Caciocavallo Silano DOP is already named there.

→ **Puglia 3 may not run a taxonomy paragraph** ("Puglia's cheeses are stretched curd: mozzarella, scamorza,
caciocavallo…"). Burrata has to arrive by its own route — the farm, the cream, the two-day shelf life, the
IGP — and the shell it is tied inside can be a clause, not a lesson. Orecchiette must earn its place on the
thumb, the street and the bitterness it is dressed with, not on the drag, which Molise has taught.

**Campania 3 owns mozzarella outright** (the stretching in hot water, the cut that gives the cheese its name,
the whey written into the DOP as a quality, eighteen to twenty degrees at the table) **and owns the
specification-forensics shape**: AVPN against the STG, the flour clause deleted in February 2009 because
Germany objected. Pane di Altamura's DOP is the same *kind* of document.

→ Altamura's rules get stated, not litigated: a short, concrete list of what the specification allows, and no
second reading built on a rulebook disagreeing with itself.

**Liguria 3 owns focaccia**: focaccia genovese sold by weight from seven in the morning and dipped in a
cappuccino, focaccia di Recco IGP and its two origin stories, farinata, and the emigrants' fugazza.

→ **Focaccia barese is one sentence of difference** (potato in the dough, cherry tomatoes and olives, the
oil), inside a paragraph about something else.

**Toscana 3 owns *cucina povera* as a named frame** ("Tuscans call their cooking cucina povera… and they say
it with pride"), and **Basilicata 3, which comes next in the batch order, opens on it**: "Every Italian
region claims cucina povera. Basilicata has the strongest claim of any of them."

→ **Puglia 3 may not open on cucina povera or use it as its spine.** The old text does exactly that ("The
cooking of Puglia is *cucina povera* in the strict sense"). Puglia's kitchen needs its own frame, and the
obvious candidate is the one thing the region has that nobody else has in this quantity: durum wheat off the
Tavoliere and the Murge, and a taste for bitterness — cime di rapa, wild chicory, a Coratina oil that burns
the throat — that runs through the plate from the pasta to the oil poured on it.

**Campania 4 has just used** dry-stone terraces (*macere*), a coast of terraced orchards, and what was in
people's kitchens at one moment.

→ Puglia 4's trulli must be about the **roof and the tenure**, not about dry-stone walling as a craft.

**Minor, noted so no reviewer is surprised:** Molise 4 fortifies Termoli's castle "under Frederick II", so
Frederick is not new to the course; Basilicata (not yet rewritten) holds Melfi, where Frederick issued the
constitutions of 1231, **and Horace, born at Venosa in 65 BC** — so if Altamura's Horace claim survives the
research, Puglia gets Horace *travelling* (Satires I.5) and Basilicata keeps him born. Sicilia and Sardegna
(both later in the order) use *alberello*; Puglia is first to the term in the rewrite and may define it.

**Barred for Puglia 1–4:**

- a named producer, writer or agronomist introduced with a date (Lazio 2, Abruzzo 1, Molise 2, Campania 2);
- an archaeological feature or structure described in the present tense (Lazio 4, Abruzzo 4, Molise 4) — this
  rules out the obvious trullo and Castel del Monte openings, which is the point;
- a hypothetical person or animal (Abruzzo 2, Abruzzo 3);
- a fronted prepositional place phrase before the subject (Lazio 2, Lazio 3, flagged again on Abruzzo 1);
- an institution going to law (Molise 1);
- a lead ending on a withheld name (three in a row in Molise);
- people meeting, or a gathering with a date (Campania 1);
- a bare quotation as the first thing heard (Umbria lesson: a listener cannot hear quotation marks);
- more than **one** of the four leads ending on a correction or a debunk (Campania 1), and none of them in
  reading 4;
- two consecutive leads fronting a time phrase (Umbria 4 / Marche 1 lesson).

**Left, and to be allocated one per reading before drafting:** a crowd doing one thing; a measurement being
taken; an object in transit or in a cargo; a price or a transaction; a refusal — someone declining to do
what they were told; something being counted; a prejudice overturned inside the sentence.

## 3. Map and scene facts, stated up front

(A fact reviewer flags true-but-unsourced geography, so these go in the ledger before the drafts. Figures
marked ⚑ are for the research to confirm or correct.)

- Puglia is the heel of the boot, with the Adriatic on the east and the Ionian on the south and west of the
  heel. It borders **Molise** to the north, **Campania** to the west and **Basilicata** to the south-west.
- Six provinces: Foggia, Barletta-Andria-Trani, Bari, Taranto, Brindisi, Lecce. Bari is the capital.
- **North**: the **Gargano** promontory (forest, cliffs, Monte Sant'Angelo, the Tremiti offshore) and the
  **Tavoliere**, the wheat plain around **Foggia**. The **Monti Dauni** rise on the Campania/Molise border and
  hold the region's highest ground ⚑ (Monte Cornacchia, about 1,150 m), so "almost no mountains" needs care.
- **Middle**: the **Murge**, a limestone plateau ⚑ (old text: to 600 m) behind Bari and Andria, carrying
  **Altamura**, **Gravina**, **Castel del Monte** near Andria and **Gioia del Colle**; on its southern edge
  the **Valle d'Itria** with **Alberobello**, **Locorotondo**, **Martina Franca** and **Cisternino**, and
  **Ostuni** on its own hill above the coastal olives.
- **South**: the **Salento**, roughly everything below the Taranto–Brindisi line — **Lecce** inland,
  **Otranto** (Italy's easternmost comune) and **Gallipoli** on opposite coasts, **Manduria** inland from the
  Ionian between Taranto and Brindisi, and **Santa Maria di Leuca** at the tip.
- **Taranto** is on the Ionian, with its enclosed Mar Piccolo; **Bari**, **Brindisi** and **Otranto** are
  Adriatic. The crossing to Albania from the Otranto side is ⚑ about 70–80 km.
- Directions that must not be got backwards: the Adriatic side of the Salento is the **east** coast, rocky;
  the Ionian side is the **west/south** coast, sandy. Manduria is on the Ionian side, Salice Salentino
  inland behind Brindisi.

## 5. What the wine research changed, before any outline

From `docs/rewrite/puglia-research-wine.md` (W1–W52, ten candidate openings B1–B10, 23 unguarded claims in
Part C, the reframing in Part D). Readings 3 and 4 wait on `-food.md`.

1. **Reading 1's frame is three years out of date, and the correct frame is better.** The old text is written
   as an unfolding catastrophe — a bacterium "quietly destroying" the groves, a front advancing, growers
   replanting. In 2026 every particular of that is wrong. The Salento was destroyed, largely between 2013 and
   2018; the northward advance has since slowed from kilometres a year to **ten or fifteen plants a year**
   around a new focus (Donato Boscia, CNR-IPSP, W19); there has been **symptom remission in surviving Cellina
   di Nardò since about 2021**; and the replanting has largely **not** happened — **440 completed applications
   out of 9,000**, **€5 million spent of €30 million** for grafting (W20). What is happening now is a slow,
   badly funded argument about what replaces a landscape, not an emergency.
2. **And reading 1 has a real subject it does not know about.** The regional register of *olivi monumentali*
   under **L.R. 14 of 4 June 2007** now lists **332,498 individual trees**, each one illegal to fell, uproot,
   damage or trade (W12). The only control for Xylella is felling. A law that counted a third of a million
   trees one at a time, against a disease whose only answer is the saw — that tension is the reading, and it
   is fully documented.
3. **"Resist" is the wrong word and the CNR says so in as many words.** Under heavy natural infection
   pressure: **FS-17 (Favolosa) 12% of plants infected and asymptomatic, Leccino 50%, Ogliarola salentina
   100%**. Francesco Loreto, director of the CNR's bio-agrifood department: *"this is not a resistance, but a
   tolerance."* FS-17 is a Frantoio seedling selected by Giuseppe Fontanazza and patented by CNR-ISAFOM, so
   "the new Favolosa" is right and it is an Italian research product (W20).
4. **The front is at the edge of Bari, not "around Brindisi and Taranto", and the Valle d'Itria is inside the
   delimited area, not north of it** (W18). The crossing of the **41st parallel** was a focus of **six olive
   trees and three almonds** towards **Torre a Mare**, eleven years after Gallipoli. Both of the old text's
   sentences on this are simply false now, including the reassurance about the monumental trees.
5. **The 21 million is Coldiretti's, and it counts trees inside the infected territory, not dead trees**
   (W15). The hard figures: about **4 million infected**, **2.6 million felled**. The felled count is more
   shocking precisely because it is verifiable.
6. **Reading 2 carries a flat error in its most quotable sentence.** Dolce Naturale is **not** "the region's
   only DOCG": Puglia has **four**, and the other three are Castel del Monte Bombino Nero, Nero di Troia
   Riserva and Rosso Riserva, **all four created in 2011** (W35). That is a gift — the region's formal top
   tier is fifteen years old, which is the argument reading 2 is already trying to make. Bombino Nero is a
   **rosé DOCG**, which ties directly to the rosato section.
7. **The redemption arc is weaker than the numbers, and the honest version is more interesting** (Part D).
   **7% of Puglia's ~9 Mhl is DOC, 23% IGT, 70% table wine.** The named denominations are minute: Gioia del
   Colle **123 ha**, Squinzano **52 ha**, Brindisi **394 ha**, Salice Salentino **1,540 ha**; Salice
   Salentino Rosato is about **140,000 bottles** against **6.8 million** of IGT Salento. A very small, very
   good layer formed on top of a bulk region that is still there underneath.
8. **The two grapes are not a stable pair — one is eating the other.** 2000 → 2010: Negroamaro **16,670 →
   ~11,400 ha**, Primitivo **7,440 → 11,766 ha**, now about **12,200 ha** (W42). The Salento's own grape is
   in retreat in the Salento. The old text treats them as equal partners in a recovery; a decade of census
   data says one of them won.
9. **Puglia is not Italy's largest wine producer** (Veneto has led for years; Puglia second, W31) and **not
   the largest producer of table olives** (Sicily, about half; Puglia about 27%, W6 — replace with **Bella di
   Cerignola**, a real Puglian table-olive fact). Whether Puglia was *ever* first by volume is marked CANNOT
   VERIFY; **do not assert it**.
10. **Smaller, all to be silently corrected rather than staged:** the coast is **~800 km**, the longest of any
    mainland region, not 400 (W1); the highest point is **Monte Cornacchia, 1,152 m**, so write "no mountain
    spine down the middle" rather than "almost no mountains" (W2); there are **five** olive-oil DOPs, Terre
    Tarentine being the missing one (W11); the Murge reach **679 m at Monte Caccia**, not 600 (W8); Gioia del
    Colle's zone is **200–450 m**, not 350–500 (W38); Fino's Es vines are about **sixty** years old, not
    ninety (W47); **Minutolo is not a Fiano** — DNA in 2001 put it in the Moscato group (W51); the Manduria
    **Riserva's 24 months including 9 in wood** is missing entirely (W37); the Strait of Otranto's narrowest
    point is **72 km**, and **Punta Palascìa**, not Leuca, is Italy's easternmost point (W24); the Otranto
    martyrs are traditionally **813**, canonised by Pope Francis on **12 May 2013** (W22), which is the fact
    the old text most conspicuously lacks.
11. **Two claims have no source at all and go:** the **Benedictine cutting** (W34) and **"three hundred days
    of sun"** (W3 — Brindisi 1961–90 measures 6.8 sunshine hours a day and **69 rain days**). Also dropped or
    attributed: "more olive trees than any region in Europe" (no comparison with Andalusia, which is credited
    with 170–180 million), "the clearest water in Italy", "the highest polyphenol content of any Italian
    olive" (trade copy — give the comparison, ~1,000 ppm against 300–400 for average extra virgin, not the
    crown), the whitewash-against-plague story (one of at least three accounts), and "pulled up faster than
    anywhere in Italy".

### Reading 1 — Olives, sun and the Salento

**Lead (B1): 332,498 olive trees are on a list.** A tree gets there by documented historical value, or by the
girth and shape of its trunk, or by standing next to something already protected; once listed, damaging,
felling, uprooting or trading it is illegal (L.R. 14 of 4 June 2007, W12). The lead ends on the collision,
stated flat: the only measure that stops Xylella is cutting trees down.

1. **The register first, because it is the frame.** The law, its criteria, its prohibitions, the derogation
   only for public utility, and the transitional ban that covered every centuries-old olive in the region
   while the list was being built. The provisional further **1,751 trees** and their fourteen comuni if it
   earns the space. What is **not** documented, and the ledger says so: how many of the 332,498 now stand
   inside the delimited area (W12).
2. **What the trees are for, in McGee's order** (rule 3). Coratina from the plain between Bari and Andria:
   **about a thousand parts per million of polyphenols against 300–400 for an average extra virgin** (W10),
   which is the burn at the back of the throat and the reason people new to it think the oil has spoiled. The
   comparison, never the crown. Cellina di Nardò and Ogliarola Salentina in the Salento, softer — and the
   reason they matter is epidemiological, which sets up §4: the Salento was close to a monoculture of exactly
   the two cultivars the disease destroys outright (W21).
3. **The scale, honestly bracketed.** Between a third and a half of Italy's oil depending on the year, with
   Mediobanca's **45.1%** named as one measurement and its date given (W5); **161,009 olive-growing holdings**
   at the 2020 census (W4). The 60 million trees attributed to Coldiretti and the regional census, not stated
   as fact, and **no** European superlative. **Bella di Cerignola** replaces the false table-olive claim (W6).
4. **The epidemic, in its true two phases.** Found in the province of Lecce in **October 2013** by the team
   led by **Maria Saponari**, subspecies *pauca*, genotype **ST53** (W13) — and already over 8,000–10,000
   hectares by then, so it was found late rather than caught early. The vector is the meadow spittlebug,
   *Philaenus spumarius*; the bacterium blocks the **xylem** (W14). **No import route is stated** — the Costa
   Rica coffee story is unverified (W14). Then the first staged doubt: Coldiretti's **21 million** is trees
   inside the infected territory, while what is counted is about **4 million infected and 2.6 million
   felled** (W15).
5. **The felling, the refusal and the law, in two sentences and no more** (W17 warns the material is legally
   intricate and the brief bars opening on it): the 2015 seizure that stopped the containment plan, the
   investigation archived in **May 2019**, and the Court of Justice ruling of **5 September 2019** that Italy
   had failed to remove infected plants in the 20-km strip. It is here to explain the delay, not to be a
   story of its own.
6. **Where it actually is now, which is the correction the reading exists to make.** Six olives and three
   almonds at Torre a Mare, the **41st parallel** crossed eleven years after Gallipoli; Triggiano's separate
   *fastidiosa* focus in the vines, **over 30 hectares of vineyard uprooted**, monitoring closed in 2026 with
   no spread; the Valle d'Itria comuni inside a targeted search programme (W18). Then Boscia's **10–15 plants
   a year** against kilometres a year in 2013–18, the five reasons offered, and the remission in Cellina di
   Nardò since about 2021 — with the honest limit, that nobody yet knows whether it lasts (W19).
7. **Second staged doubt, and the reading's argument lands.** 12% / 50% / 100%, and Loreto's sentence: *"this
   is not a resistance, but a tolerance."* Then the replanting that has not happened — **440 of 9,000
   applications, €5 million of €30 million** (W20). The listed trees cannot be cut and cannot be saved, and
   the trees that could replace them are mostly not in the ground.
8. **Ending** on the register against the saw — an image and a limit, not a summary (rule 10). No buying
   advice, no "the groves will recover".

**Moved to the Key facts box:** the five DOP oils with Terre Tarentine restored and Dauno's four sub-zones
(W11); the cultivar list; the L.R. 14/2007 citation and the 332,498; the 2013 date, the ST53 genotype and the
vector's Latin name; the **2.6 million felled** and the **€132 million a year** productivity loss (W15); the
Murge at **679 m**, the Tavoliere at about **3,000 km²** and second in Italy after the Po plain (W7), and the
coast at about **800 km** (W1).

### Reading 2 — Primitivo and Negroamaro

**Lead (from W45): every empty beer bottle in Brindisi.** The southern glassworks were not making wine
bottles in 1943, so the rosato went into recycled beer bottles, as many as could be found in the town. The
order came from **General Charles Poletti**, running Allied procurement, who wanted a large supply of rosé —
an Italian wine with an American name. The house was **Leone de Castris**, the name came from the *Cinque
Rose* contrada of the Salice Salentino fief and from a family in which every generation had five children,
and the wine is still made. Opening move: **a shortage improvised around**, subject the bottles. Closing
beat: the wine in them was centuries older than the bottling — it had always been drunk from the barrel,
which is the defensible half of the primacy claim (W45).

1. **First staged doubt, immediately, because it is the lead's own claim.** "The first rosé bottled and sold
   in Italy" is the house's account and every source repeating it traces back to the house. Attribute it,
   and let the older, safer fact carry the weight.
2. **What the Salento's rosato actually is**, and here the old text is wrong about the method. **Lacrima** is
   drainage, not pressing: after destemming and **20–24 hours** of contact, only the *mosto fiore*, about
   **30% of the mass**, is drawn off. **Salasso** is the other method, 30–40% of the liquid taken at the
   *alzata del cappello*, 18–24 hours after crushing. "*Vino di una notte*" is a third thing, 6–12 hours
   (W44). The Squinzano rulebook writes **12–24 hours of maceration before pressing** into law (W41), which
   is the checkable detail: a rosé whose method is a legal requirement. The honest claim is about **kind and
   not quantity** — a still, deep, food-weight wine made to a defined method, made that way long before the
   pale Provençal style became the world's default (W46). **Not** "more serious rosé than any part of Italy".
3. **Negroamaro**, the grape that lead and rosato belong to. Grown almost only here (W42). The etymology is a
   live dispute and stays one: *niger* plus *mavros*, both meaning black, against the plain Italian reading
   of *amaro* — no ampelographer has settled it (W43). The better fact beside it: **Pliny describes Manduria
   as *viticulosa***, full of vineyards. Salice Salentino from **1976**, and the rulebook has moved: the
   traditional Malvasia Nera partnership is now **history, not a rule** — in the current text Malvasia Nera
   is named only in the Aleatico type, and the red's residual share is anonymous (W40). Sources disagree on
   the red minimum (85% v 90%) and the Riserva (18 v 24 months), so **no number is printed for the red**.
4. **Primitivo, and the identity chain in the order it happened**, which is the order the old text has right:
   a priest at **Gioia del Colle** picks the earliest-ripening plant in a field at the end of the eighteenth
   century and records it as *Primativo*, the first to ripen — the **1799** planting date is the agronomist
   **Sannino**'s attribution a century later, not a receipt, and the ledger says so (W32). **1967** Goheen
   notices the resemblance; **1972** ampelographers call them identical; **1975** Wolfe's isozymes; **1993**
   **Carole Meredith**'s DNA; **December 2001**, after more than 150 old Dalmatian vines sampled with
   **Pejić** and **Maletić**, a match at **Kaštel Novi** — *Crljenak kaštelanski*; **2008**, DNA read from
   century-old herbarium leaves gives the oldest name, **Tribidrag**, written down in Croatia in **1444**
   (W33). Each step is a technique reaching an answer the previous one could not.
5. **Second staged doubt, and the chain's honest end.** How the vine crossed the Adriatic is unknown. The
   Benedictine cutting has no source anywhere (W34) and the dowry story is unverified; the first firm record
   on the Italian side is the Gioia del Colle selection. Saying so is better than repeating either.
6. **The two styles, with the altitudes right.** Manduria on the hot Ionian plain, **DPR 30 October 1974**,
   85% minimum — and that 85% is recent, changed from **100%** in 2009 after two years of consortium work,
   on the argument that it let producers cope with difficult vintages (W37). Gioia del Colle on the Murge at
   **200–450 m**, the varietal type **100% Primitivo** (W38). Picked **from late August**, among the first in
   Italy, with part of every bunch raisined while the rest ripens, which is where both the 14–16% and the
   Dolce Naturale come from (W39).
7. **The four DOCGs, all of 2011**, replacing the "only DOCG" error: Dolce Naturale (DM 23 February 2011,
   **100% Primitivo, minimum 16% natural alcohol, at least 50 g/l residual sugar**, and **not fortified** —
   several English sources call it a fortified sweet red and they are wrong, W36) and the three Castel del
   Monte DOCGs of 4 October 2011, one of which, **Bombino Nero**, is a DOCG for a **rosé** (W35). The top of
   Puglia's pyramid is fifteen years old.
8. **The reframe, which is the reading's real argument** (Part D). About **nine million hectolitres**: **7%
   DOC, 23% IGT, 70% table wine**. Gioia del Colle **123 ha**, Squinzano **52**, Brindisi **394**, Salice
   Salentino **1,540**. Salice Salentino Rosato about **140,000 bottles** against **6.8 million** of IGT
   Salento (W31, W38, W41). A small good layer on top of a large bulk trade that is still there. **Puglia is
   second to the Veneto, not first**, and was never claimed here to have been first.
9. **Ending: one grape is eating the other.** Negroamaro **16,670 → about 11,400 ha** between the 2000 and
   2010 censuses while Primitivo went **7,440 → 11,766**, and about **12,200** now (W42). The Salento's own
   grape is in retreat in the Salento, passed by one that sells abroad under a name American drinkers already
   knew. Ends on the limit of the claim, not a summary.

**Moved to the box:** the DOC and DOCG dates and rules, including the **Manduria Riserva's 24 months with at

### Publishing the preview: expect a refusal, and what to do about it

Worth writing down because it will happen at the end of **every** region from now on, and it cost four tool
calls here.

The Artifact tool refuses a publish from a session that has never viewed the live artifact, and hands back
the live source to merge onto. That rule is right for a hand-authored page and wrong for this one:
`dist/italia-course.html` is **generated** by `build.py` from the source tree, so the live version is simply
an older build of the same pipeline and there is nothing in it to merge. Satisfying the rule literally means
Reading a **13.8 MB** file that is mostly base64 image data, which is not viable.

What worked, in order:

1. Attempt the publish. It is refused and saves the live source to a file.
2. **Do not read that file.** Instead compare the two builds cheaply — file size, and the presence of
   `IMAGE_DATA`, `const REGIONS`, both `READINGS['IT-xx']` objects, the count of `'IT-dd':{name:` in
   `COURSE`, plus a couple of markers unique to the new region. That establishes in one command that the
   live copy is the same pipeline's earlier output and that the only difference is the region just
   rewritten.
3. A second, identical publish is refused again as unchanged content, and `action: "read"` returns only a
   head with the same instruction. Neither advances things.
4. **Ask the owner once, then publish with `force: true`.** The tool requires explicit confirmation for
   force and that is correct, but here the justification is solid and should be stated when asking: the
   artifact is regenerated wholesale from source, so the version being discarded is an older build and no
   hand-authored content can be lost. Published as **Version 49** on 2026-09-17.

**For the next region: go straight to step 1, then step 2, then ask.** Do not spend calls on a second
identical publish or on `action: "read"`.
