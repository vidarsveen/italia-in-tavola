### 1a. The template risk

Eighteen regions in, the shape most likely to repeat itself is **"poor isolated place, therefore authentic food"**.
Basilicata, Molise, Calabria and Abruzzo have all already been written, and Puglia's reading 3 had to be
re-spined for exactly this. Sardegna is the region where that framing is most tempting and most available.
**It is not available.** The island was not poor in the way the Apennine south was poor; it was *separate*,
which is a different fact with different consequences. Isolation here has to earn its place by explaining
something specific — a bread that keeps a year, a language closer to Latin, a louse that cannot cross sand.

### 1b. The Vermentino collision — measured 2026-09-17, and it is serious

§1 flagged this as a risk. Reading Liguria's actual text makes it worse than a risk. **Liguria's reading 2
is titled “Vermentino, Pigato and the sea”** and is built on exactly the moves Sardegna's reading 2 was
about to use:

- **A laboratory overturning a local identity claim.** Liguria opens on a named grower at Ranzo insisting
  Pigato is its own grape, then: “The laboratories say it is Vermentino.” Sardegna's planned lead — Cannonau
  turns out to be Grenache — is **the same move with different nouns**, and Liguria got there first.
- **A disputed origin, Spain versus Italy.** Liguria already carries “Vermentino's origin is disputed: an old
  story brings it by sea from Spain, and the international grape catalogue now gives Italy.” The Cannonau /
  Garnacha direction-of-travel argument is **the same dispute, same two countries**.
- **The synonym reveal.** “In Piemonte the same vine is called Favorita.”

So the old Sardegna reading 2 (“Cannonau, or Grenache, or Garnacha” + “Vermentino and the granite”) is
**Liguria's reading twice over**. Three consequences, decided before drafting:

1. **Reading 2 needs a different spine.** The Grenache question must be *stated in its corrected form* and
   not *performed* as a reveal. Where the vine came from is one sentence, not a section.
2. **Sardegna's Vermentino must be about what granite does**, which Liguria never claims — Liguria's
   Vermentino grows on sea-facing slopes and is discussed through reflected light and salinity. Decomposed
   granite holding no water is a mechanism Liguria does not own. Keep that; drop “mineral” as praise.
3. **Sardegna's real reading-2 asset is what Liguria has nothing of:** the only DOCG on the island, the
   ungrafted old vines in sand, and — pending the W-verdicts — Vernaccia under flor, which the collision
   sweep shows is the one genuinely unused winemaking story in the whole course.

**Method note.** The §1 sweep counted the word and said “Liguria: 20, own it as granite”. That was not enough.
Counting a term tells you it is used; only reading the neighbour's sentences tells you which *argument* is
spent. For the last region, read the colliding reading, not the word count.

### 1d. The Sicilia collision — measured 2026-09-17

I write both remaining regions, so the two must be de-conflicted against each other, not just against the
eighteen already done. Sicilia's current text measures **838 / 826 / 727 / 779** words (already near target;

**Reading 4 is safe.** Sicilia carries *no* Bronze Age, *no* prehistory, one passing “archaeological”, and
nothing about a culture without writing. The nuraghi, the corbelled dome, the bronzetti and the giants'
tombs are Sardegna's alone.

**Reading 1 is not safe.** Sicilia 4 opens: *“Sicily sits in the middle of the Mediterranean… Everyone who
wanted to control the sea wanted the island, and each conqueror built.”* Sardegna 1's existing lead is the
same argument with a longer list — Phoenicians, Carthaginians, Romans, Byzantines, Pisans, Genoese,
Aragonese, Piedmontese. **Two islands, one conquest-list move.**

**Decided:** Sicilia keeps the conquest list, because for Sicily the conquests are the whole point — each
one left a layer in the kitchen. Sardegna takes **the inversion**, which is the true and more interesting
half: *they all took the harbours and none of them took the interior.* The list itself shrinks to a clause.
Sardegna's subject is what the interior did while the coast changed hands, which is also what makes the
bread, the language and the cheese make sense.

Also note Sicilia spends **Greek × 14, temple × 13, UNESCO × 6**. Sardegna must not reach for Greek antiquity
at all — it has no Greek layer worth the name, and the temptation to borrow one is a fabrication risk.

## 3. Map and scene facts, stated up front

Sardegna's direction trap is **not** east/west as Calabria's was. It is **inland versus coast**, and the
inversion that matters: the coasts were malarial and raided, so the population lived *inland*. Any sentence
that treats the island as a Mediterranean beach place has the history backwards. Second trap: **Gallura is
granite, Sulcis is sand, the Supramonte is limestone** — three different rocks doing three different jobs,
and swapping them silently breaks the wine argument.

## 4b. What the food research established (returned 2026-09-17, F1-F70, D1-D27)

### The cheese is the region's real argument, and the numbers are better than the old text knew

- **F2. 92%, not the 97% usually quoted.** Consorzio data certified by IFCQ for 2025/26: of 37,750 t of
  Pecorino Romano, **Sardegna 34,667 t (91.8%)**, Lazio 2,899 t, Grosseto 184 t. The 97% figure traces to an
  uncited blog and Italian Wikipedia.
- **F3. The consortium that protects Rome's cheese has its office in Macomer, in the province of Nuoro.**
  Founded November 1979. Its member list runs to **26-27 Sardinian dairies against three in Lazio and one in
  Grosseto**.
- **F1.** The DOP zone names **Sardegna first** in the legal text.
- **F11. The island's own old cheese is the one that is disappearing.** Fiore Sardo made **339 t** in 2023
  against Pecorino Romano's 36,633 t on the same island - **under one hundredth** - and its output halved
  between 2021 and 2022.
- **F9.** Sardinia produces **67%** of Italy's sheep milk; **70%** of the DOP zone's milk goes into Pecorino
  Romano.
- **F8.** **3.1 million sheep, 45% of the national flock** (BDN census 2018). The "57%" claim is
  irreconcilable with both this and ISMEA. The old text's "more sheep than people, over three million against
  1.6 million inhabitants" stands.

### The 2019 protest, with the dates and the mechanism right

- **F12.** Blockades from 7-8 February 2019; shepherds paid **about 60 cents** a litre against a production
  cost near 70.
- **F13. The accord was 8 March 2019 at 74 cents**, with 72 applied retroactively to February - not February,
  and not 55 cents.
- **F14. The part usually left out:** the price was **indexed to the cheese**. 72 cents a litre when Pecorino
  Romano is at EUR 6/kg, rising to **EUR 1.02 at EUR 8.50/kg**.
- **F15. The consorzio's own supply plan documents the cause as overproduction.** 356,324 q in 2015/16
  preceded the EUR 0.63 collapse of 2016/17; it concedes its earlier ceiling failed (280,000 q allowed against
  341,833 made) and now charges **EUR 0.64/kg on over-ceiling production**.
- **F16.** Milk reached **EUR 1.70-2.03 in April 2023** with the cheese at EUR 14.05/kg, and shepherds were back
  in the streets in September 2026 with the cheese at EUR 10.00. **Do not write that the cheese fell below
  EUR 5 in 2019** - the sourced trough is about EUR 6.33.

### The casu marzu legality, stated precisely

**F20.** The popular claim is right in outcome and wrong in mechanism. What bars sale is **Reg. (CE) 178/2002
art. 14** (food unsafe if unfit for human consumption) with **Legge 283/1962 art. 5(d)**, which forbids food
"invase da parassiti". Reg. 853/2004's traditional-foods derogations concern **premises, not live larvae**.
Decisively: the PAT sheet records it as eaten "esclusivamente in ambito familiare", and its own final field -
request for a hygiene derogation - reads **NO**. PAT listing is a heritage inventory, not a licence.
**F22.** Guinness 2009 does call it the most dangerous cheese, but **no documented case of intestinal myiasis
from casu marzu exists**; the *Piophila casei* cases in the literature are unrelated. Risk stated, not
recorded. **F18. The fly is invited, not suffered** - the sheet lists the techniques: shorten the brining,
press only weakly, pierce the rind and add drops of oil to attract the insect, limit turning.

### The bottarga finding, which inverts the story

**F35.** Slow Food's Presidio page states that **only 0.3% of the Sardinian bottarga on sale comes from Cabras
mullet**, and that bottarga is about 5% of what the town processes. Cabras made **5.5 quintals in 2024** -
single-digit tonnes. **Most bottarga sold in Sardinia is packed on the island from roe landed on the African
Atlantic coast or the Pacific**, and the Cabras product fetches ~EUR 250/kg against EUR 130-150 for the rest.
**F36.** "Sardinia is the world's largest producer" is unsupported; there is **no DOP or IGP**, while Greece
has one. This is the region's answer to Puglia's "surplus that left" - here the *product* arrives from
elsewhere and leaves under a local name. **And section 4a found the old text never says the word bottarga
once.**

### The fava bean fact, which the course has nowhere else

**F54.** G6PD deficiency affects roughly **7.5-12% of Sardinian males**, and Meloni et al. documented **948
acute haemolytic anaemia cases in north Sardinia between 1965 and 1979, 94.4% of them triggered by eating
fresh fava beans**. Cases fell 75% after neonatal screening began in 1971. The prevalence is attributed to
selection under historical malaria - **which ties directly to section 3's coast/interior inversion**: the same
malaria that pushed people inland is written into their blood. On an island whose Carnival dish is favata,
this is the strongest single fact the food research returned.

### Norwegian sourcing, the notes that earn the edition its keep (D1-D27)

The three false friends that would wreck a Norwegian reader's cooking, all to be carried into the recipes:

- **D10. `semulegryn` is not semola rimacinata.** The miller's own description is for *groet, pudding og
  kaker*: coarse porridge semolina, usually soft wheat. A dough made with it is gritty and will not hold a
  shape. Buy **Caputo Durum Semola**, and look for the word *semola*.
- **D19. `multe` is the cloudberry** to every Norwegian reader. *Mugil cephalus* is *ekte multe*, so an
  unqualified "multe" in a fish recipe reads as a berry. Write **multe (fisken)** or give the Latin.
- **D26. Norwegian tinned `ansjos` is spiced, sweetened sprat**, not anchovy. Buy *ansjosfilet* of Italian or
  Spanish origin, or ask for **sardeller**.

And the one genuinely good substitution the research found:

- **D7. Norway makes its own bottarga.** Raa Sjoemat at Averoey salts and dries **skrei cod roe** for grating
  over pasta. Same method, same job, different fish - and it substitutes honestly, provided the text says so
  rather than pretending it is mullet. **D8.** Never offer **Mills Kaviar**: sweetened smoked cod-roe paste,
  and grating is meaningless on a paste.

Also: **D5.** fresh acidic sheep's cheese for seadas **cannot be bought in Norway** - say so plainly; *kesam*,
*cottage cheese*, *prim* and *brunost* are all wrong, and ricotta is excluded by the IGP itself. **D13.** No
myrtle in Norway; **einer** (juniper) is the honest substitute, sharper and pinier - a good Norwegian juniper
roast, not porceddu. **D12.** Mirto is **not at Vinmonopolet** (zero results; Sardinia holds three spirits,
all gin), so the pairing points at Cannonau, Vermentino, Carignano and Vernaccia instead. **D16/D17.** *sau*
and *faarekjoett* are mutton and not interchangeable with lamb; *kje* is a spring, farm-order animal.
**D21.** Favabeans only from specialists - and carry a line on favism, given F54.

## 4c. What the wine research established (returned 2026-09-17, W1-W80)

### The spine of reading 2 is overturned - but not in the direction the island claims

The old text sets up "Sardinian researchers say the vine was here first; the claim is taken seriously and is
not accepted." That is too generous to the local case, and it misdescribes the evidence.

- **W1.** Identity is settled. Cannonau *is* Garnacha Tinta (De Mattia et al., *J. Hort. Sci. Biotech.* 2009).
- **W4. The paper everyone cites for the Sardinian-origin claim says close to the opposite.** De Mattia's
  STRUCTURE analysis put the wild Sardinian accessions in a separate third cluster, **"excluding direct origin
  of Cannonau-Garnacha from the wild grapevines studied in Sardinia"**. It resolves identity, not direction.
- **W10. The most over-read result in Sardinian wine writing.** The Bronze Age pips show **morphometric**
  similarity - seed shape under discriminant analysis - to Garnacha *and to Muscat a petits grains*. That is
  not DNA, and the paper claims no genetic continuity with modern Cannonau.
- **W11.** The same 2024 study found the pips also resemble **Caucasus and Balkan** cultivars, and proposes
  interbreeding between western Asian cultivars and local vines from the Late Bronze Age - eastern
  introduction, which cuts *against* autochthony.
- **W8.** Early Bronze Age Sardinian seeds (2017-1751 cal BC) were **wild in 75% of cases**; only the Late
  Bronze Age material (ca. 1300-1100 BC) looks domesticated.
- **W80.** Medieval remains from Sassari, 1330-1360 AD, show **high similarity to Catalan and Ligurian
  cultivars** - morphometric support for Iberian varietal contact precisely in the Aragonese period.
- **W5.** The "Garnacha comes from Vernaccia" etymology is refuted as a pedigree: Sardinian Vernaccia is
  genetically distinct from both.

**So the honest statement is:** the grape is Garnacha; there were domesticated vines on Sardinia in the Late
Bronze Age; and **nothing links those vines to Cannonau**. Section 1b already ruled that the origin question
shrinks to a clause rather than being performed as a reveal. The research makes that ruling easier, not
harder - the reveal would have been false.

### The longevity material - handle with the hedge budget

- **W31/W32. Corder et al., *Nature* 2006, does not say what it is cited for.** Verbatim: procyanidins are
  higher in wines from south-west France and Sardinia "where **traditional production methods** ensure that
  these compounds are efficiently extracted during vinification. These regions **also happen to be
  associated** with increased longevity." The abstract **never names Cannonau**, never names Nuoro, credits
  **winemaking and not the grape**, and "happen to be associated" is explicitly a geographic coincidence.
- **W33.** No peer-reviewed work quantifies Cannonau as a procyanidin outlier; one 2025 paper notes the
  **absence of procyanidin B2** in a Cannonau extract.
- **W24/W25.** The cluster is real demography. The distinguishing feature is **male** longevity: the
  male-to-female centenarian ratio is **1.35** inside the zone against **2.43** outside.
- **W26.** The discoverers' own words: "The specific mechanism... remains unknown."
- **W29. The diet finding is the reverse of the popular one.** Pes et al. 2015: the zone "depended mostly upon
  livestock rearing, and consumption of animal-derived foods was **relatively higher** than in the rest of the
  island."
- **W30.** Pes et al. 2022, on their own field: it is "too often taken for granted that the mere fact of being
  documented in a long-lived population makes the diet a causal factor... a claim which should be proven."
- **W34/W35.** Age validation is a live dispute (Newman) with a published defence (Austad and Pes 2025);
  Ogliastra is among the **best-validated** zones. Framing: **real demography, contested mechanism.**

The old reading 4 already hedges this well. **The gain is that the wine-longevity link can now be cut
entirely rather than hedged** - there is nothing to hedge, because the source never made the claim.
