## 1. Europe's highest vineyards
- Summary → one sentence: the vines of Morgex and La Salle grow on their own roots because the louse could not
  survive the cold, and the wine tastes of the height.
- Lead: from the cable car at Punta Helbronner (old closing section moved up) the shelf of vines far below the
  ice; then smallest region, least wine, ~400 ha, a Puglia estate makes more; what it has is height.
- Chain: phylloxera → grafting everywhere → not here, too cold for the louse's life cycle → own roots, old vines,
  Prié Blanc nowhere else. Low pergola (against the tall Alpine pergola and Bordeaux wires) → warmth off the stones
  at night, below the wind → worked bent double, no machines. Altitude → short season, cold nights → slow sugar,
  acid kept; UV thickens skins; dry air keeps rot away → pale 11% white, herbs, flowers, apple, salty finish →
  partner of fatty, salty food. High acid and low alcohol → sparkling (Cave Mont Blanc, bottle fermentation, a
  cuvée aged high on the mountain); ice wine, reasonable here, absurd elsewhere. Ending: back to the cable car,
  cold year no ripening, warm year a wine no warmer place can copy.
- Box and tasting table unchanged. The skyway figure moves up beside the lead.
- Ledger: all old facts kept. "acidity like a wire running down the middle" (a figure) → literal. Cut: none.

## 2. Alpine whites and mountain reds
- Summary → the one DOC splits into seven village names, and going down the valley the wine turns from Petit
  Rouge and its cousins to Nebbiolo.
- Lead: a wine list in Aosta from Blanc de Morgex to Vien de Nus, and nobody finds it odd; bilingual region and
  patois; grapes unknown outside; one DOC, seven sub-denominations; few bottles, drunk locally. Then the drive
  down: white high, red in the middle, Nebbiolo low.
- Body as the drive: Arvier (Enfer, amphitheatre that traps the sun, ≥85% Petit Rouge, deeper and riper) → Petit
  Rouge described → Aymavilles and Aosta, Torrette, ≥70%, the commonest label → the other natives (Fumin dark,
  tannic, once blended away, now alone in wood; Cornalin = Humagne Rouge) → Nus (Vien de Nus; Malvoisie is Pinot
  Grigio) and Chambave (dry Moscato) → Petite Arvine from the Valais, the most admired white; Müller-Thurgau,
  Chardonnay, Pinot Gris lean → Arnad-Montjovet and Donnas (Picotendro, ≥85%, two years, ten months wood,
  lighter than Piedmont's) → who makes it: cooperatives as in Alto Adige, the two estates seen abroad, the
  agricultural college that makes good wine, prices above fame, little leaves Italy. Ending: a bottle outside
  the valley is there because somebody went looking for it.
- Box and tasting table unchanged.
- Ledger: all old facts kept. Changed: the chambave caption said "north-facing slope"; the sunny slope of an
  east–west Alpine valley faces south (the photo looks from the Fénis side across to Chambave), so "south-facing".
  Cut: "it is the wine that tells you the Langhe are not far away" (announcing) → "a reminder".

## 3. Fontina and the table of the Alps
- Summary → Fontina is made twice a day because its milk must reach the vat within two hours, and the winter
  kitchen of rye bread, cured fat and melted cheese is built around it.
- Lead: milking morning and evening, the milk straight to the vat, the rule that forces it; then the kitchen of a
  place cut off half the year: no oil, little fish, butter, rye, potatoes, chestnuts, cured fat, game, cheese; as
  Emilia's around Parmigiano.
- Fontina: since the 12th century; the Issogne fresco; rules protected 1996 (breed, raw, three months, rind).
  Result: semi-soft, melts → half the dishes; the alpage summer cheese. Fonduta (soaked in milk, yolks, butter,
  truffle; not fondue, no wine or kirsch, a spoon).
- Winter, as a chain: rye bread baked a few times a year in the village oven → hard, stored on racks, softened
  in broth → seupa à la Vapelenentse. Carbonada, polenta concia, mocetta. The merenda board and the Marende.
- Ending: génépy first, then the coppa dell'amicizia and the grolla; the cup is not put down until it is empty.
- Box unchanged.
- Ledger: all old facts kept. **Changed, sourced**: the Issogne fresco "of about 1480" → "around 1500": the portico
  lunettes are attributed to Magister Collinus and dated late 15th to early 16th century, and the cheese seller's
  wheels are described as the oldest known depiction of Fontina (en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Issogne_Castle;
  it.wikipedia.org/wiki/Castello_di_Issogne). Cut: "is worth seeking out" (announcing); "makes complete sense
  the moment the temperature drops" (a flourish; the half-year winter carries it).

## 4. Castles of the Aosta valley
- Summary → everything crossing the western Alps came through the valley, so it filled with Roman walls and a
  hundred castles, and the last ibex survived in a king's hunting reserve.
- Lead: May 1800, Napoleon over the Great St Bernard, the fort at the gorge, a few hundred men for two weeks, the
  guns smuggled past in straw and dung, the timetable upset, "vilain castel de Bard", demolished.
- Chain: the fort was there because the valley is a corridor → tolls → a hundred castles → Bard rebuilt
  1830–38 by Charles Albert expecting the French, museum 2006, Sokovia 2015. The Romans first: Salassi defeated
  25 BC, Aosta's grid, Arch (crucifix 1449), Porta Praetoria, theatre 22 m, wall; bilingual, Valdôtain.
  The Challant: Fénis (shape, 1242, 1320–1420, Aymon and Boniface, not a fortress, frescoes by Jaquerio,
  d'Andrade 1895, 1935). Issogne: the iron pomegranate fountain, and the portico's painted shops, among them the
  cheese seller with the oldest picture of Fontina. Verrès across the river. Sarre: the king's hunting lodge with
  thousands of horns → but the same king made Gran Paradiso a reserve in 1856 to stop others shooting the last
  sixty ibex → park 1922, recovery, WWII collapse to 400, recovery → every Alpine ibex descends from here.
  Mont Blanc and the Cervino as the walls of the valley. Ending: the gamekeepers' paths are trails, and the ibex
  stand on a rock and look at you.
- Box unchanged.
- Ledger: all old facts kept. **New, sourced**: the Issogne lunettes of shops (baker, butcher, tailor,
  apothecary, market, guard room, cheese seller), painted around 1500 by Magister Collinus, with the oldest
  depiction of Fontina; the wrought-iron pomegranate fountain made around 1502 as a wedding gift by Giorgio di
  Challant (both wiki pages above). **Changed**: "frescoes of fifteenth-century shops" → around 1500, same source.
  **Changed**: the old text listed Sarre among "other Challant castles"; nothing in the old text or my sources
  makes Sarre a Challant castle, so it is now named only as the king's hunting lodge. Cut: "which tells you
  something about how the ibex nearly ended" (announcing; the king's two acts now stand side by side), "unamused".
  This is a landmark reading; its one arrival at the table is the Fontina fresco, which ties it to chapter 3.
