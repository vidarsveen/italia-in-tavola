# Italia in Tavola — Style Bible

Purpose: turn eighty accurate wine-and-food readings into a book people want to read, and can listen to. The facts stay. The voice changes.

The test for every article: could a reader tell this apart from the one before it, and would they want to hear it read aloud in a car?

---

## 1. What is wrong now (diagnosis, so the rules make sense)

1. **Reference voice, not book voice.** Nearly every paragraph opens with a declarative fact and then elaborates. Confident, clean, and identical forty times over.
2. **No people, no scenes.** Thirteen million cases in 1985; May warmth restarting fermentation in the bottle — these are stories delivered as statistics. Nobody is ever in the room.
3. **One template.** Hook → grape family → how it's made → tasting box → why it suits the food → reading the label. Once a reader spots the shape, the prose stops mattering.
4. **Unreadable aloud.** Key-facts bullets and tasting tables are dead air in the audiobook. "Colour: medium ruby, brick at the rim with age" is not a sentence.
5. **Region intros are inventories.** A roll call of protected names with one adjective at the end ("fizzy, cheerful Lambrusco").

---

## 2. The rules (each one checkable)

1. Every article must contain at least one **scene**: a place, a time of day, something happening. A reader should be able to say where they are.
2. Every article must contain at least one **named or unnamed person** doing something — a producer, a cook, a grandmother, a customs officer, a monk. Not a brand.
3. No paragraph may open with *[subject] is / was / has* as its first clause. Vary the entry.
4. No two consecutive articles may use the same **opening move** (see §4).
5. A number appears only if it carries a story. "Thirteen million cases" stays; "10.5–12% alcohol" moves to the data box.
6. Every article must contain one **contradiction, correction, or surprise** — the thing the reader thought they knew that turns out to be otherwise.
7. Maximum one list per article body. Bullets belong in the data box, not in the prose.
8. The "why it works at the table" idea must be **shown once with a dish on a plate** before it is explained.
9. Sentences vary in length. If three consecutive sentences are within four words of each other, break the pattern.
10. Region intros: one concrete image that carries the region, with the famous names arriving inside it as evidence, never as a list.
11. Closing paragraph ends on an image or a line to remember, not on advice ("do not age it") and not on a summary.
12. The "Before you move on" recap and the quiz stay, but they are **not part of the audio track** (see §6).
13. Adjectives earn their place. Cut any adjective that could be deleted without loss of meaning. "Fizzy, cheerful" → one of them, or neither.
14. Address the reader directly at most once per article ("you") — use it where it lands, not as a habit.
15. Norwegian version is a translation of the final English, done article by article, not a parallel rewrite.

---

## 3. Banned list (actual tics from the existing text)

Phrases that appear in the current readings or will recur if not stopped. Any of these in three articles = dead.

- "properly understood"
- "reputation problem" / "earned it honestly"
- "a wine of the year"
- "the first lesson of [region]"
- "not one grape but a family" / "X is a group of related grapes"
- "the palate is…" / "the nose is…" as paragraph openers
- "what it tastes like" as a header
- "why it works at the table" / "why it needs food" as a header (keep the idea, kill the header)
- "reading the label" as a header
- "look for…" as an instruction opener
- "built on [ingredient list]"
- "cuts through" (once per twenty articles, not once per article)
- "it is one of the few / one of the most…"
- "designed for its food"
- "the great [adjective] dish of…"
- Any sentence that begins "Ask what…" or "Understanding why is…"

Structural bans:
- Opening with the grape/dish name as the first word.
- Opening with a reputation ("X has a reputation…").
- Ending on a purchasing tip.

## 4. Openings menu

Each article picks one. Consecutive articles must differ. Track the sequence in a table (see HANDOVER).

| Code | Move | Example seed |
|---|---|---|
| A | A scene, present tense | A bar in Modena at seven, the tumblers already cold |
| B | A person | Cleto Chiarli in 1860, deciding to bottle |
| C | A contradiction | The ugliest landscape in Italy makes the most beautiful food |
| D | A number that shocks | Thirteen million cases in one year, sold as a soft drink |
| E | A myth corrected | Sangiovese is not one grape, and Brunello is not a different one |
| F | A dish on the plate first | Gnocco fritto torn open, the steam, the salame going in |
| G | A question the reader actually has | Why is this red wine served cold? |
| H | History in one line | Before the tank, every farm made it by accident |

## 5. Before and after (worked examples from Emilia-Romagna and Toscana)

### 5.1 Region intro — Emilia-Romagna

**Before**
> Italy's food valley. Parmigiano Reggiano, prosciutto di Parma, balsamic vinegar of Modena and fresh egg pasta all come from the flat Po plain, washed down with fizzy, cheerful Lambrusco.

**After**
> The plain is flat and the fog sits on it for months. Nothing about the landscape is beautiful, and everything about the food is — because this is where Italy learned to wait. Parmigiano ages two years, prosciutto eighteen months, balsamic twelve or twenty-five or a lifetime. Then, against all that patience, a glass of Lambrusco: cold, purple, frothing, gone in a minute.

**Why it's better:** one image (fog, flatness) carries the region; the famous names arrive as evidence of a single idea (patience); Lambrusco lands as the contrast, so the reader already understands the wine before reading the wine chapter.

### 5.2 Region intro — Toscana

**Before**
> Cypress-lined hills, Renaissance cities and Sangiovese in every form: Chianti Classico, Brunello di Montalcino, Vino Nobile. The cooking is peasant-proud: bread, beans, olive oil and a thick Florentine steak.

**After**
> Tuscany looks like the postcard, and the cooking looks like nothing at all. Bread without salt, beans, oil, a soup made of yesterday. The one extravagance is a steak the size of a book, cooked rare over embers, and beside it always the same grape — Sangiovese, sour-cherried and sharp, the wine that only makes sense with something on the fork.

**Why it's better:** contradiction opener (postcard vs. plain food); the DOC names are gone from the intro — they belong in the chapters; the last line plants the thesis of the whole region ("only makes sense with something on the fork").

### 5.3 Article opening — Lambrusco

**Before**
> Lambrusco has a reputation problem, and it earned it honestly. In the 1970s and 1980s sweet, fizzy Lambrusco was the best-selling imported wine in the United States, thirteen million cases in 1985 alone, sold cold as a soft drink with alcohol.

**After (opening move D, then A)**
> Thirteen million cases. That is how much Lambrusco Americans drank in 1985, most of it sweet, most of it from a bottle shaped like a soft drink, and all of it the reason the word still makes a sommelier wince. Now go to Modena. It is seven in the evening, the bar has tumblers, not glasses, and what comes out of the fridge is dry, purple, and foaming violet. It is the same name and a different drink.

**Why it's better:** the shocking number opens rather than being buried mid-sentence; the reader is put in a place; "reputation problem" is gone; the contradiction (same name, different drink) is the thesis and it is stated last, where it lands.

### 5.4 Article opening — Sangiovese

**Before**
> Ask what Tuscany's wine is and the answer is one word: Sangiovese. It is the grape of Chianti, of Brunello di Montalcino, of Vino Nobile di Montepulciano and of Morellino di Scansano, and it is the most widely planted variety in Italy.

**After (opening move E)**
> Chianti and Brunello taste like wines from two different countries. They are the same grape, grown forty kilometres apart. Nothing about Tuscany makes sense until you accept that Sangiovese is less a flavour than a mirror — it tastes of wherever it was planted and of how much care it got, and it does not forgive either.

**Why it's better:** the correction (same grape, different countries) is the hook; the DOC list is deferred to a later section where it has a job; "mirror" gives the reader one idea to carry through four readings.

### 5.5 Mid-article — how the fizz gets in

**Before**
> Before the tank, Lambrusco was made the way every farm made it: fermentation stopped by the winter cold, wine bottled in spring with a little sugar left, and the warmth of May finishing the job in the bottle.

**After**
> Nobody invented the fizz. A farmer pressed his grapes in October, the cold of November stopped the yeast before it had finished, and in spring he bottled what he had. Then May arrived, the cellar warmed, the yeast woke up in the bottle and had nowhere to put the gas. By June every farmhouse on the plain had a cloudy, dry, sparkling red, and nobody had meant to make it.

**Why it's better:** a person (the farmer) and a sequence in time; the process becomes a story with an ending ("nobody had meant to make it"); same facts, no loss of accuracy.

### 5.6 Closing — Lambrusco

**Before**
> And do not age it. Lambrusco is a wine of the year, sold cheap because it is made to be, and at its best it is the most food-friendly sparkling wine in Italy for the price of a sandwich.

**After**
> It will not keep, and it is not supposed to. It is made in the autumn to be finished by the next one, at a table with something fried on it, by people who have never once thought of it as a wine to be understood.

**Why it's better:** ends on an image and a quiet reversal of the old title ("properly understood"); no purchasing advice; no "at its best" hedging.

## 6. Audiobook rules

The audio track is a **separate rendering** of the article, not a straight read of the page.

- Read: title, subtitle, all body prose, image captions (optionally, as "a note on the picture"), the closing.
- Do not read: key-facts box, tasting table, "Before you move on", quiz, photo credits, "Recipes from this reading".
- Tasting table gets **one spoken sentence** in the prose instead: "In the glass it is pale, sharp and bone dry, and it wants to be cold." If the prose already says this, nothing is added.
- Key facts that matter go into the prose; the rest stay visual-only.
- Section headers are not read. If the prose needs a transition, write one.
- Target: 5–7 minutes spoken, which means roughly 700–950 words of body prose.

---

## 7. Data box (what stays structured)

Keep, as visual elements only:
- Key facts (DOCs, dates, soils, styles, serving temperature, alcohol)
- Tasting table (colour / nose / palate / alcohol / serve / at the table)
- Before you move on (three lines)
- Check yourself (three questions)
- Recipes from this reading

Rule: nothing in the data box may be the *only* place a story-bearing fact lives. If it's interesting, it's in the prose too.

---

## 8. Region page fields (all twenty)

- **Intro** (see rule 10, examples 5.1–5.2): 60–90 words, one image, no lists.
- **Landmark**: one sentence of fact, one sentence of why it's worth the look. Currently fine.
- **Classic pairing**: unchanged.
- **At the table**: unchanged (it is a list by nature).

## 9. Definition of done, per article

- [ ] One scene, one person, one surprise — all present
- [ ] Opening move logged and different from the previous article
- [ ] Zero banned phrases (grep)
- [ ] No paragraph opens with subject + linking verb
- [ ] At most one list in the prose
- [ ] Closing ends on an image or a line, not advice
- [ ] Audio rendering produced, 5–7 minutes
- [ ] Data box contains nothing story-bearing that the prose lacks
- [ ] Read next to its two neighbours — distinguishable
- [ ] Every fact in the original is still present or deliberately moved to the data box (no silent losses)
